\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We measure how well heat conducts through the deep lunar regolith --- the
parameter $K_d$ --- separately at the Apollo~15 and Apollo~17 heat-flow
boreholes, and find them to differ by about $1.5\times$
($K_{d,\mathrm{A15}}^{*}=4.60$, $K_{d,\mathrm{A17}}^{*}=7.08$
mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$) --- though at the published basal fluxes this contrast is
only \emph{marginal}, its 95\% interval just including zero ($p\approx0.03$). This guide explains the three things that matter and
nothing else: the \textbf{pipeline} that turns raw Apollo thermometer records
into one number per site (Ch.~\ref{ch:pipeline}); the \textbf{slope method} ---
the flux-anchored equilibrium solver that is the methodological contribution of
this work (Ch.~\ref{ch:slope}); and \textbf{how that method differs} from the
brute-force spin-up it replaces (Ch.~\ref{ch:bruteforce}). It closes with the
result and how to reproduce it. Read it straight through in about ninety
minutes; you should come out able to explain both the pipeline and the slope
method to someone else.
\end{abstract}

% =====================================================================
\chapter{What this study does}\label{ch:study}
% =====================================================================

\begin{plain}[title={\sffamily\bfseries Reading this guide --- the few basics you'll need}]
This guide assumes only school algebra. Each idea below is explained again where it is
used; collected here so nothing is a surprise.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Lunation} --- one lunar day, sunrise to sunrise, $\approx29.5$ Earth days.
  Time here is counted in lunations, not days.
\item \textbf{$\partial T/\partial t$, $\partial T/\partial z$} --- ``partial derivatives'':
  just the rate at which temperature $T$ changes with time $t$ (at fixed depth) or with
  depth $z$ (at fixed time). Read each as a slope, nothing more.
\item \textbf{$\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle$} --- an average over one full lunation. $\langle
  T\rangle$ is the temperature with the day--night swing averaged out (the ``cycle-mean'').
\item \textbf{Steady state} --- the column has stopped changing year to year; every
  lunation now looks identical to the last. $K_d$ is only readable here.
\item \textbf{(Diurnal) skin} --- the shallow layer, top $\sim$tens of cm, that the
  day--night heat wave can still reach. Below it, only the slow internal-heat signal remains.
\item \textbf{$K_d$ vs.\ $K_d^{*}$} --- $K_d$ is the deep conductivity we are after;
  $K_d^{*}$ (with a star) is the single best-fit value the retrieval returns.
\item \textbf{Residual} --- model value minus measured value; a fit makes the residuals small.
  \textbf{RMSE} is the root-mean-square of the residuals: one number, in kelvin, for ``how
  far off.'' \textbf{$\textstyle\sum$} means ``add these up''; \textbf{$O(N)$} means ``the
  work grows in proportion to $N$.''
\item \textbf{Units.} Conductivity $K$: mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ (milliwatts per meter per
  kelvin). Heat flux $Q_b$: mW\,m$^{-2}$. Depth: meters; temperature: kelvin (K).
\end{itemize}
\end{plain}

\noindent\textbf{The question.} Every airless body loses its internal heat
through a blanket of broken rock --- \emph{regolith}. How fast that heat escapes
is set by the regolith's \textbf{thermal conductivity} $K$ (units
W\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$); deep down it settles to a value we call $K_d$. The
standard practice has been to assume \emph{one} global $K_d$ for the whole Moon.
Our question is simply: \emph{what is $K_d$ at each of the two Apollo heat-flow
boreholes, separately --- and do they differ?}\footnote{The full reasoning ---
the complete physics, solver, statistics, geology, and code chapters, plus
practice problems --- lives in the 83-page extended edition archived at
\code{documents/jgr/guidebook/reference/guidebook-extended.tex}. This guide keeps only
the pipeline, the slope method, and the comparison.}

\noindent\textbf{The answer.} The two sites do differ, by roughly $1.5\times$:
the Apollo~17 deep regolith conducts heat about $1.5\times$ better than
Apollo~15's. This is the first time $K_d$ has been retrieved per site rather
than assumed global, though at the published basal fluxes the contrast is
only \emph{marginal} --- its 95\% interval now includes zero ($p\approx0.03$).

\noindent\textbf{The contribution.} The hard part is not the fitting --- it is
reaching the \emph{steady state} in which $K_d$ becomes readable. The literature
spun the model up for a fixed 30 lunations (lunar day--night cycles) and declared it
converged; we show
that test is wrong (the deep column is still drifting) and replace it with the
\textbf{flux-anchored equilibrium solver} --- the ``slope method'' --- which
integrates the steady-state equation directly below an anchor depth and arrives
in a handful of outer cycles (four in production) instead of $\sim$1000 lunations. That solver, and the
per-site retrieval it makes affordable, are what is new here.

\noindent\textbf{What is borrowed, what is new.} The conductivity form $K(T,z)$,
its five constants, the density profile, and the per-site albedo are
Hayne~(2017). The basal flux $Q_b$ is Langseth~(1976). The restored Apollo HFE
record is Nagihara~(2018). Original to this work: the solver, the inversion onto
the HFE deep temperatures, and the per-site numbers.

\begin{keypt}[title={\sffamily\bfseries Headline result}]
\[
\boxed{\;K_d^{*}(\mathrm{A15}) = 4.60\,[4.18,\,6.96]\quad
        K_d^{*}(\mathrm{A17}) = 7.08\,[6.16,\,8.07]\;\;\mathrm{mW\,m^{-1}K^{-1}};\quad
        \Delta K_d = 2.31,\ p\approx 0.031.\;}
\]
Brackets are bootstrap 95\% CIs; quadrature error-budget totals are $\pm1.88$
(A15) and $\pm3.88$ (A17). At the published basal fluxes the contrast is only
marginal ($p\approx3\%$, 95\% CI spanning zero). The single
largest caveat is that the \emph{absolute} numbers ride on Hayne's radiative
calibration $\chi=2.7$; the \emph{direction} of the contrast survives a
low-$\chi$ stress test. See \S\ref{sec:chi}.
\end{keypt}

\paragraph{\textbf{Step 1 --- read the Apollo HFE record.}}
On 31 July 1971 the Apollo~15 crew drilled two boreholes ($\sim$1.4 and $\sim$1.0 m) at Hadley Rille
and emplaced the Heat Flow Experiment (HFE): thin probes carrying
platinum-resistance temperature bridges at several depths. Apollo~17 carried a
similar, deeper pair. The probes recorded subsurface temperatures from 1971 to
1977. For $\sim$40 years the data sat on magnetic tape; in 2018 Nagihara et al.\
recovered and re-digitized the full record (with $\pm2.5$\,cm per-sensor depth
uncertainty). That restored record, read by \code{code/src/lunar/apollo\_helpers.py},
is our raw input.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_intro_probe.pdf}

\textbf{(b)} The two published conductivity profiles evaluated at

\paragraph{\textbf{Step 2 --- extract $T_{\rm eq}$ from the stable window.}}
The instant the probes were emplaced they read $\sim$330\,K: the drill had heated
the borehole wall. For the next $\sim$2.7 years each sensor decayed exponentially
toward the undisturbed regolith temperature and then flattened. We do not trust
the early record. The code scans candidate start points over 55--85\% of each
sensor's series (nominally to 85\%, but a minimum-tail guard discards any
candidate that would keep less than 20\% of the record to average over)
and picks the earliest window whose linear trend is flatter than
$0.08$\,K\,yr$^{-1}$; the mean of that quiet tail is the sensor's equilibrium
temperature $T_{\rm eq}$, and its scatter sets the noise. Only sensors below
80\,cm --- below the daily skin --- are kept: 7 at Apollo~15, 16 at Apollo~17.
These deep $(z,T_{\rm eq})$ dots are what the model must hit
(Figs.~\ref{fig:timelineA15} and~\ref{fig:timelineA17}); Figure~\ref{fig:windowflow} shows the selection
rule as a process.

\begin{plain}
This is the important caveat: $T_{\rm eq}$ is an \emph{estimate} of the
undisturbed equilibrium temperature, not proof that every Apollo sensor had
become perfectly flat. Some sensors pass the strict $0.08$\,K\,yr$^{-1}$ trend
test; others keep the late fallback tail because the record is still drifting
slightly. That does not make the steady-state model meaningless. It means the
model is being compared to the best available estimate of the temperature the
regolith would have after the drilling heat vanished. Residual drift is a
systematic uncertainty in the data target, not a new heat source in the lunar
column.
\end{plain}

{\small\color{dim}\textit{Code: \code{code/src/lunar/apollo\_helpers.py} $\to$ \code{find\_stable\_window}}}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
for frac in np.linspace(0.55, 0.85, 13):      # 13 candidate window starts
    i0 = int(frac * n)
    slope, _ = np.polyfit(t_year[i0:], T[i0:], 1)  # trend of the trailing tail
    if abs(slope) <= 0.08:                    # flatter than 0.08 K/yr ?
        break                                 # earliest flat tail; its mean is T_eq
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{\textbf{Step 3 --- choose a trial $K_d$.}}
$K_d$ is the one well-constrained unknown. Picture the column from the bottom up:
a small steady geothermal flux $Q_b\!\sim\!21$\,mW\,m$^{-2}$ (measured separately
by Langseth, held fixed) passes up through every layer. Where the regolith
conducts well the temperature changes little per meter; where it conducts poorly
the gradient steepens. So the deep gradient is just $Q_b/K_d$ --- and that is
exactly what the deep sensors see. $K_d$ is a property of the material, like
density, not a fudge factor. We will try many values and keep the one that fits.

\paragraph{\textbf{Step 4 --- build $K(T,z)$ and the density profile (Hayne).}}
Fixing the trial $K_d$ determines the whole conductivity surface, via
Hayne~(2017). Heat passes grain-to-grain through tiny contact patches; near the
surface grains are loosely packed (small $K_s$), deeper they compact, so the
contact part rises exponentially toward the deep asymptote $K_d$:
\begin{equation}
K_c(z) \;=\; K_d - (K_d - K_s)\,e^{-z/H},\qquad K_s = 0.74\!\times\!10^{-3},\quad H = 0.06\;\text{m}.
\label{eq:Kc}
\end{equation}
At daytime temperatures photons also hop across the empty pore spaces, a
radiative term rising as $T^3$:
\begin{equation}
K(T,z) \;=\; K_c(z)\,\bigl[\,1 + \chi\,(T/T_{\rm ref})^3\,\bigr],
\qquad \chi = 2.7,\quad T_{\rm ref}=350\;\text{K}.
\label{eq:Khayne}
\end{equation}
{\small\color{dim}\textit{Code: \code{code/src/lunar/properties.py} $\to$ \code{conductivity\_hayne}}}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
Kc = Kd - (Kd - Ks) * np.exp(-z / H)          # contact part   -> Eq. (2.2)
K  = Kc * (1.0 + chi * (T / 350.0) ** 3)      # radiative T^3   -> Eq. (2.3)
\end{lstlisting}

The same paper supplies $\rho(z)$ ($1100\!\to\!1800$\,kg\,m$^{-3}$); Hemingway
(1973) supplies $c_p(T)$; both conductivity terms are derived from their physics
in App.~\ref{app:hayne}. Fix the five Hayne constants and the density profile,
and the only free number left is $K_d$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:kTz}). That is what makes
this a one-parameter retrieval.

{\small\color{dim}\textit{Code: \code{code/src/lunar/properties.py} $\to$ \code{density\_hayne}}}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
rho = rho_d - (rho_d - rho_s) * np.exp(-z / H)   # 1100 -> 1800 kg/m^3 with depth
\end{lstlisting}

\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{figures/fig_book_kTz.pdf}
\caption{The Hayne conductivity $K(T,z)$. It rises with depth (compaction, via

\paragraph{\textbf{Step 5 --- solve the heat equation forward.}}
With $K(T,z)$ chosen, we solve the heat equation in the column (derived in
App.~\ref{app:heateq}),
\begin{equation}
\rho\,c_p\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\left(K\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right),
\label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}
closed by a radiative balance at the top --- sunlight absorbed equals infrared
radiated plus heat conducted downward,
\begin{equation}
(1-A)\,S(t) \;=\; \varepsilon\sigma T_s^4 \;+\; K_{\rm surf}\,\frac{T_s-T_{\rm sub}}{\Delta z_{\rm surf}/2},
\label{eq:surfbc}
\end{equation}
and by the fixed geothermal flux at the bottom (Langseth 1976),
\begin{equation}
K\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\bigg|_{z_{\max}} \;=\; Q_b.
\label{eq:bottombc}
\end{equation}
Here $A$ is the albedo (the fraction of sunlight reflected), $\varepsilon$ the emissivity,
$\sigma$ the Stefan--Boltzmann constant, $S(t)$ the incident sunlight, $T_s$ the surface
temperature, and $Q_b$ the geothermal flux leaking up from the interior.
Both boundary conditions are derived in App.~\ref{app:bc}. Fixing the basal
\emph{flux} (not a temperature) means the deep profile keeps rising at the
geothermal gradient $Q_b/K$ rather than approaching a single value --- physical,
not a drift; a bedrock base layer would flatten it only below the sensors and
would not change $K_d$ (App.~\ref{app:bc}). No closed-form solution exists when
$K$ depends on both $T$ and $z$, so we discretize: a
\emph{geometric} depth grid ($\Delta z_0=2$\,mm at the surface, each cell 8\%
thicker, 69 cells to $z_{\max}=5$\,m) and half-hour time steps
($\Delta t=1800$\,s, $\sim$1417 per lunation, plus the cycle-closing wrap
step --- App.~\ref{app:dtcert} explains why that last step matters). The grid follows one rule of thumb --- fine cells where
the temperature changes fast (millimeters at the surface, which sees a
$\sim$280\,K swing each lunation), coarse cells where it does not (tens of
centimeters by $1$\,m); App.~\ref{app:grid} gives the finite-volume cells and the
harmonic-mean face conductivities. The dimensionless cell-to-cell coupling per step,
$\alpha=\Delta t\,K/(\rho c_p\,\Delta z^2)$, is small for the deep cells ($\sim10^{-3}$;
at the 2-mm surface cells it reaches order unity, which is exactly why an
implicit scheme is required), so deep neighbors nudge each other only gently. Three pieces of
machinery advance one hour, and they are all you need to understand the solver
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cnstencil}):

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Crank--Nicolson} sets the update rule. An \emph{explicit} scheme
  (use the old temperatures) is simple but unstable unless $\Delta t$ is
  absurdly small; an \emph{implicit} scheme (use the new temperatures) is stable
  but only first-order accurate. Crank--Nicolson averages the two: it is
  unconditionally stable \emph{and} second-order accurate. The picture is
  estimating a slope between two points by averaging the slopes at both
  endpoints, not just one. This couples all the new temperatures into one linear
  system $\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{T}^{n+1}=\mathbf{d}$, and because each cell only
  talks to its immediate neighbors, $\mathbf{A}$ is \emph{tridiagonal} (nonzero
  only on three diagonals). Derived, with the truncation-error and stability
  arguments, in App.~\ref{app:cn}; the matrix assembly and boundary rows in
  App.~\ref{app:assembly}.
\item \textbf{The Thomas algorithm} solves that tridiagonal system in $O(N)$
  --- two sweeps (a forward elimination, a backward substitution) instead of the
  $\sim N^3/3$ of general Gaussian elimination. For 69 cells it is
  sub-microsecond (App.~\ref{app:thomas}).
\item \textbf{Newton's method} handles the surface. The top balance
  $(1-A)S(t)=\varepsilon\sigma T_s^4 + \text{(conduction down)}$ is non-linear in
  $T_s$ because of the $T_s^4$ infrared term. Newton slides along the tangent to
  the residual until it hits zero --- 2--3 iterations per hour. It runs
  \emph{every time step} (the insolation $S(t)$ changes hourly), and feeds its
  $T_s^{n+1}$ into the matrix as the top boundary value before Thomas solves
  (App.~\ref{app:newton}).
\end{itemize}

\input{figures-tikz/cnstencil}
\caption{The Crank--Nicolson stencil. Each new temperature $T_i^{n+1}$ is coupled
to its two neighbors at \emph{both} the old time level $t^n$ and the new level
$t^{n+1}$; CN averages the two rows. That symmetry is what buys unconditional
stability and second-order accuracy at once.}
\label{fig:cnstencil}
\end{figure}

\noindent Repeat the step $\sim$1417 times for one lunation and the \emph{thermal
skin wave} appears: the surface swings between $\sim$390\,K (noon) and
$\sim$100\,K (night), and that swing damps exponentially with depth, halving
every $\sim$6\,cm. By 80\,cm the daily wave is gone and only the slow geothermal
signal remains. (The detailed 5-cell worked example for all three pieces ---
matrix, sweeps, Newton step --- is in the extended edition; here we only need to
know what they do.)

\paragraph{\textbf{Step 6 --- run the flux-anchored loop to steady state.}}
One lunation is not enough. $K_d$ is a \emph{steady-state} property: only once
the deep column has stopped drifting does its gradient equal $Q_b/K_d$. Reaching
that state by brute time-stepping would take $\sim$1000 lunations (the diffusive
timescale of a 5\,m column) --- months of compute across the sweep and bootstrap.
Instead the solver alternates a short forward solve (settle the fast skin) with a
direct integration of the steady-state equation below an anchor depth (build the
slow deep column without waiting for diffusion). This is the methodological
centerpiece, and Ch.~\ref{ch:slope} is devoted to it. For the pipeline, the one
thing to hold onto: Step~6 turns a trial $K_d$ into a \emph{certified}
cycle-mean profile $\Tm(z;K_d)$ in well under a second ($\sim$0.6\,s with
the compiled kernel; \S\ref{sec:speedup}). That solve is the innermost,
most-multiplied box of the whole retrieval (Fig.~\ref{fig:costnesting}) --- which
is exactly why the flux-anchored shortcut is worth the trouble.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/costnesting}
\caption{The three nested loops behind one retrieved $K_d^{*}$. The outer sweep

\label{fig:costnesting}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{\textbf{Step 7 --- score the trial with RMSE.}}
The certified profile gives a model temperature at every depth. Interpolate it to
the real sensor depths, compare to the measured $T_{\rm eq}$, and collapse the
residuals into one number:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE}(K_d)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl[\Tm(z_i;K_d)-T^{\rm obs}_i\bigr]^2}.
\label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
One number that says how badly this trial fits the data --- and Step~7 is the
\emph{only} place the Apollo measurements enter (Fig.~\ref{fig:retrievaldemo}).
Steps~5--6 are a pure forward model; the data grade it here, at the comparison,
not inside the anchor solve.

\end{figure}

\paragraph{\textbf{Step 8 --- sweep $K_d$ to a minimum.}}
Repeat Steps 4--7 across a grid of trial $K_d$ (29 values over $1$--$15$ at A15,
32 over $3$--$25$ at A17, densified near each minimum). Plotting RMSE versus $K_d$ gives a smooth bowl --- the
solver is deterministic and the steady state is unique for each $K_d$, so the
curve is clean (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}). The minimum is the best fit. We do not
want to round to the grid, so we fit a parabola through the three lowest points
and read its vertex:
\begin{equation}
K_d^{*}=K_d^{(k)}+\frac{\Delta}{2}\,\frac{y_--y_+}{y_--2y_0+y_+},
\label{eq:vertex}
\end{equation}
where $K_d^{(k)}$ is the grid point with the smallest RMSE, $y_-,y_0,y_+$ are the three
lowest RMSE values (at that point and its two neighbors), and $\Delta$ is the spacing
between grid points.
{\small\color{dim}\textit{Code: \code{code/pipeline/compute/retrieve\_kd.py} $\to$ \code{run\_kd\_sweep\_extended}, \code{kd\_star\_from\_residuals}}}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
rmse = np.sqrt((R[idx]**2).mean(axis=0))  # RMSE at each grid K_d   -> Eq. (2.4)
k = int(np.argmin(rmse))                  # nearest grid minimum
# fit a parabola through the three lowest (K_d, RMSE) points:
kd_star = -b / (2*a)                       # its vertex             -> Eq. (2.5)
\end{lstlisting}

giving sub-grid precision with no extra (9-second) solves (App.~\ref{app:vertex}).
For Apollo~15 the
vertex sits at $K_d^{*}=4.60$\,mW\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ with RMSE $\approx1.00$\,K.

\paragraph{\textbf{Step 9 --- bootstrap for a confidence interval.}}
Seven dots, one number --- how confident are we? We cannot re-fly the mission, so
the bootstrap pretends we can (the plug-in principle behind this is justified in
App.~\ref{app:stats}). Treat the seven sensors as the population, draw
seven \emph{with replacement} (some appear twice, some not at all), jitter each
depth by $\mathcal{N}(0,2.5\,\mathrm{cm})$ to fold in Nagihara's positional
uncertainty, and re-fit $K_d^{*}$. Repeat 1500 times. The 2.5th and 97.5th
percentiles of the 1500 re-fits are the 95\% confidence interval:
$K_d^{*}(\mathrm{A15})\in[4.18,\,6.96]$. Crucially the 29 forward profiles are
computed once and cached --- each resample only re-selects which residuals enter
the RMSE, so the whole bootstrap costs seconds, not $1500\times$ the sweep.

\paragraph{\textbf{Step 10 --- the headline.}}
Run the same ten steps at Apollo~17 ($Q_b=16$, 16 sensors) and you get
$K_d^{*}(\mathrm{A17})=7.08\,[6.16,\,8.07]$. Subtract the two on each joint
bootstrap draw and the median contrast is $\Delta K_d=2.31$
($\sim$$1.5\times$), with tail probability $p\approx0.031$ under the null of
equal $K_d$. That is the headline of Ch.~\ref{ch:study}. The rest of this guide
explains the one step that made it possible (Ch.~\ref{ch:slope}), why it beats
the old way (Ch.~\ref{ch:bruteforce}), and what the number means
(Ch.~\ref{ch:result}).

\chapter{The slope method: deep regolith temperature without waiting for diffusion}\label{ch:slope}
% =====================================================================

This is the chapter to read slowly. Everything in the pipeline hangs on Step~6 ---
turning a trial $K_d$ into a \emph{certified} steady state --- and that step is
the contribution of this work. The name we use for it is the \textbf{slope
method}, because instead of waiting for heat to diffuse it computes the deep
temperature \emph{slope} directly and walks the profile down. We build it from
the ground up: why the old way is slow, why its convergence test lied, the one
insight that fixes it, the equation it integrates, and then the machinery, with
real numbers. (A full, point-by-point comparison with the brute-force spin-up it
replaces is saved for Ch.~\ref{ch:bruteforce}; here we only need enough of it to
see why a new method is required.)

\section{Why brute force is slow}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/spinup_filmstrip.pdf}
\caption{Brute-force spin-up as snapshots: the whole column is
time-stepped and only creeps toward the steady state (dashed) on the deep
diffusive clock --- still visibly short at the literature's 30-lunation
cut-off, and not settled until $\sim$1000 lunations. This slow creep is
the cost the slope method eliminates.}
\label{fig:spinupstrip}
\end{figure}
\label{sec:slow}

The Apollo deep sensors report temperatures that have stopped changing year to
year --- the column is in \emph{periodic steady state}, repeating identically
each lunation.

\begin{plain}
Periodic steady state means $T(z,t+P)=T(z,t)$ for every depth $z$ and every
time $t$, where $P$ is one lunation. It does \emph{not} mean the temperature is
time-independent: the surface can still swing by hundreds of kelvin between
day and night. It means the movie repeats frame-for-frame each cycle. The deep
column reaches that state only after the arbitrary starting profile has fully
diffused away.
\end{plain}

$K_d$ is only readable there, because only in steady state does
the same conductive flux pass through every slab, making the deep gradient
exactly $Q_b/K_d$. Anything earlier still remembers the arbitrary starting guess.

How long must we time-step to forget that guess? The relevant time is the
\emph{diffusive timescale} for the column depth:
\begin{equation}
\tau \;\sim\; \frac{z_{\max}^2}{\kappa}
\;\sim\; \frac{(5\,\text{m})^2}{10^{-8}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}}
\;\sim\; 2.5\!\times\!10^{9}\,\text{s}
\;\sim\; 80\ \text{years}
\;\sim\; 1000\ \text{lunations}.
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
\emph{Where this comes from.} Balance the heat equation by dimensions: the
storage term $\partial_t T\sim T/\tau$ against $\kappa\,\partial_z^2 T\sim\kappa
T/L^2$, so the time to diffuse across a length $L$ is $\tau\sim L^2/\kappa$. The
quadratic in $L$ is the whole story, and it splits the column cleanly in two: a
thin skin of thickness $\ell\sim20$\,cm settles in
$\tau_{\rm skin}\sim\ell^2/\kappa\sim$ a few lunations, while the full $5$\,m
column takes $(5/0.2)^2\approx600$ times longer --- of order $10^3$ lunations.
That factor of $\sim$600 between the fast skin and the slow column is exactly the
trap the next section springs.

Picture the approach as a damped exponential: the deep column halves its
remaining error every $\sim$10 lunations. Starting $\sim$25\,K from the truth, reaching $0.05$\,K takes about
nine halvings --- $\sim$90 lunations just to \emph{almost} converge, and far more
to nail it. At ${\sim}0.5$~s per lunation that is ${\sim}26$ minutes per trial $K_d$; across $\sim$30 trials,
2 sites, and 1500 bootstraps, brute force runs to months. It does not scale.

\section{The F1 bug: convergence that wasn't}\label{sec:f1}

The literature spin-up sidestepped this by running a fixed 30 lunations and
checking ``has the cycle-to-cycle change dropped below $0.01$\,K?''. By 30
lunations that test always passed --- but only because the thin \emph{skin} had
equilibrated (it settles in $\sim$10 lunations, since
$\tau_{\rm skin}\sim\ell^2/\kappa$ with $\ell\!\sim\!20$\,cm). The deep column
was still 10--40\,K from its true value, drifting so slowly
($\sim$$10^{-3}$\,K/lunation) that the cycle-to-cycle change was already under
threshold. The test measured whether progress had \emph{slowed}, not whether the
profile had \emph{arrived}. We call this flag F1.

\begin{plain}
Picture a puddle next to a lake. The puddle settles in seconds; the lake takes
hours. If you only watch the puddle, you declare ``calm'' as soon as the ripples
stop --- while the lake is still draining. The 30-lunation test watched the
puddle (the skin) when the lake (the deep column) was the part that mattered.
\end{plain}

\noindent The consequence is fatal for the retrieval: the deep sensors at
0.8--2.4\,m sit exactly in the unsettled zone, so their modeled temperatures
depend partly on $K_d$ and partly on the starting guess. Initialise at 240\,K and
you get one $K_d$; at 260\,K, another. The starting guess had become a hidden
free parameter (Fig.~\ref{fig:f1bug}).

\section{The key insight}\label{sec:insight}

\begin{plain}
\textbf{One fact unlocks the whole method.} In periodic steady state the column
stores no net heat over a lunation, so the cycle-mean heat flux is \emph{the same
at every depth} and equals the geothermal flux $Q_b$ (full argument in
\S\ref{sec:closure}). A constant flux through a known conductivity is just a
\emph{slope} you read off --- like the voltage drop across a stack of resistors
carrying one fixed current. So the deep column never has to be \emph{diffused}
into place over a thousand lunations: once we know its temperature at a single
anchor depth, the rest of the profile is \emph{integrated} downward in one pass.
The only part that genuinely needs time-stepping is the thin daily skin
(Fig.~\ref{fig:truncation}).
\end{plain}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/truncation}
\caption{Which depths actually get time-stepped. Brute force (left) marches
the \emph{whole} 5\,m column hour by hour for ${\sim}10^{3}$ lunations, even
though the deep column settles only on that slow diffusive clock. The anchor
method (right) time-steps just the thin diurnal skin (coral), reads the anchor
at $z_0=0.55$\,m --- safely below the rectification zone --- and
\emph{reconstructs} everything beneath it from the flux ODE (green), including
the whole sensor span. Both columns receive the same basal $Q_b$.}
\label{fig:truncation}
\end{figure}

\begin{keypt}[title={The slope method in five moves}]
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Step the skin.} Run a few lunations of the hour-by-hour solver
  (\S\ref{sec:stepA}). In that time only the top ${\sim}0.5$\,m settles --- that is
  all we trust.
\item \textbf{Read the anchor.} Take the cycle-mean temperature $\Tm(z_0)$ at the
  anchor depth $z_0=0.55$\,m, just below the settled skin.
\item \textbf{Walk the slope down.} From the anchor, integrate
  $d\Tm/dz=(Q_b-u_{\rm rect})/K$ cell by cell to the bottom (\S\ref{sec:stepB}).
  This \emph{builds} the whole deep profile --- no diffusion, microseconds.
\item \textbf{Feed back.} Use the rebuilt profile as the next starting guess and
  return to move~1.
\item \textbf{Stop when the anchor stops moving.} When $\Tm(z_0)$ shifts by
  ${<}\,0.005$\,K between passes, the skin and the deep column are mutually
  consistent --- and that \emph{is} the periodic steady state.
\end{enumerate}
\end{keypt}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{figures/fig_anchor_anatomy.pdf}
\caption{Anatomy of one flux-anchored solve --- real Apollo~17 output at
$K_d^{*}=7.08$. \textbf{(a)} The converged cycle-mean profile $\Tm(z)$. Inside
the daily skin (coral, top ${\sim}0.3$\,m) the mean is set by the surface energy
balance and is found by \emph{time-stepping} (Step~A); from the anchor (dot,
$z_0=0.55$\,m) downward the profile is \emph{reconstructed} by walking the
closure slope (Step~B). The gray band marks the Apollo deep sensors
(0.8--2.4\,m), which the reconstruction passes straight through. \textbf{(b)} The
cycle-mean conductive flux: below the skin it sits on the geothermal flux $Q_b$
(dashed) at \emph{every} depth --- the constant-flux fact the whole method rests
on --- while inside the skin it still varies, which is exactly why that thin
layer is time-stepped and the rest is not.}
\label{fig:anchoranatomy}
\end{figure}

\noindent Here is the same idea, unpacked (Fig.~\ref{fig:anchoranatomy}). The
slow part --- the deep column's drift --- does not actually require time
integration at all. In steady state the deep temperature
obeys a simple first-order ODE in depth (derived in App.~\ref{app:closure}):
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\Tm}{dz} \;=\; \frac{Q_b - u_{\rm rect}(z)}{K(\Tm,\,z)}.
\label{eq:closure}
\end{equation}
At any depth, the slope is the flux ($Q_b$, known, plus a small correction
$u_{\rm rect}$) divided by the conductivity at that depth (computable once we
know the temperature there). So if we know the temperature at \emph{one} depth
below the skin, we can compute the next depth down, and the next --- building the
entire deep profile by walking down the slope, never simulating the slow
diffusion. The one temperature we need is the \textbf{anchor}: the cycle-mean
temperature at a depth $z_0$ just below the daily skin, where the skin has
already settled (the skin-depth scale $\delta$ is derived in App.~\ref{app:skin}).
The whole method is two operations, alternated
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fluxanchored}):

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Step A --- forward solve.} From the current guess of the cycle-mean
  profile, run a few lunations of hour-by-hour Crank--Nicolson+Newton+Thomas.
  The fast skin settles into its true oscillation. Read off the anchor
  temperature $\Tm(z_0)$ and the rectified flux $u_{\rm rect}(z)$.
\item \textbf{Step B --- closure reconstruction.} From the anchor, integrate
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:closure} downward (RK2). This rebuilds the deep profile in
  microseconds.
\end{itemize}
\noindent Set the guess to the reconstruction and repeat. When the anchor
temperature stops moving by more than $0.005$\,K, declare convergence --- 4
outer cycles at production settings, $\sim$0.6\,s per trial $K_d$ (\S\ref{sec:fullsolve}).

\caption{The flux-anchored equilibrium loop. Step~A consumes the current
cycle-mean guess and runs a short forward solve, returning the anchor

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering

one-variable closure ODE. The whole trick is the shaded box: on a periodic
cycle the stored-heat term averages to exactly zero, so time drops out of
the problem. Worked line by line in App.~\ref{app:pde2ode}.}
\label{fig:closureflow}
\end{figure}
\label{sec:closure}

Equation~\eqref{eq:closure} is the heart of the method, so here is its
derivation. Picture a deep parcel of regolith over one full lunation: the
temperature oscillates but returns exactly to itself a lunation later (the
definition of periodic steady state). Time-average the heat equation
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:heat}) over that lunation:
\[
\Bigl\langle \rho c_p\,\partial_t T\Bigr\rangle
\;=\;
\Bigl\langle \partial_z\bigl(K\,\partial_z T\bigr)\Bigr\rangle.
\]
The left side averages to \emph{zero}: every bit of warming during the lunar day
is exactly canceled by cooling during the night. The right side is a $z$-
derivative of a time average, so the average passes through: $\partial_z\langle
K\partial_z T\rangle=0$. Integrate once in $z$ and use the basal condition
$\langle K\partial_z T\rangle|_{z_{\max}}=Q_b$:
\begin{equation}
\bigl\langle K(T,z)\,\partial_z T\bigr\rangle \;=\; Q_b
\qquad\text{at every depth.}
\label{eq:fluxsame}
\end{equation}
In plain words: \emph{the cycle-mean conductive flux is the same at every depth
and equals the geothermal flux}. That is what ``the column carries $Q_b$ in
steady state'' means. To turn this into a slope we must peel apart the average ---
and because $K$ depends on $T$, and $T$ oscillates, $\langle K\partial_z
T\rangle \ne K(\Tm)\,\partial_z\Tm$ in general. Name the difference the
\textbf{rectified flux}:
\[
u_{\rm rect}(z) \;\equiv\;
\bigl\langle K(T,z)\,\partial_z T\bigr\rangle \;-\; K(\Tm,z)\,\partial_z\Tm.
\]
Substitute and rearrange, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:closure} drops out. The closure has
just two ingredients: the known $Q_b$ (a number from Langseth) and $u_{\rm rect}$
(computed once per outer cycle as a by-product of Step~A's cycle mean).

\section{Why $u_{\rm rect}$ exists --- and vanishes below the skin}

The conductivity $K(T,z)$ depends on temperature, mostly through the radiative
$\chi(T/T_{\rm ref})^3$ term. When $T(t)$ swings by $\Delta T$ around its mean,
$K$ swings too, and the swing of $K$ correlates with the swing of the gradient
$\partial_z T$ in a way that does not average out --- that residual is
$u_{\rm rect}$. Quantitatively, expand $K$ about the mean: a swing $\Delta T$
produces $\Delta K\sim\tfrac12 K''(\Tm)\,\Delta T^2$ (the linear term averages
away; the curvature term does not), and multiplying that by the oscillating
gradient and time-averaging leaves a non-zero eddy flux. Because it scales as $\Delta T^2$, it falls off very steeply with depth
(Fig.~\ref{fig:anchorplacement}). In the top few centimeters the mean gradient is
so steep that the mean-field flux $K(\Tm)\,\partial_z\Tm$ overshoots $Q_b$
many-fold, and $u_{\rm rect}$ --- which makes up the difference --- is
\emph{larger than $Q_b$ itself}; it is still $\sim$80\% of $Q_b$ at the shallow
stage-1 anchor ($0.25$\,m). But by the production anchor at $z_0=0.55$\,m the
daily swing is only $\sim$0.2\,K and $u_{\rm rect}$ has fallen to ${\sim}0.2\%$ of
$Q_b$ --- negligible. That steep fall is precisely why the anchor sits there:
below the rectification zone the closure is simply $d\Tm/dz=Q_b/K$, with the eddy
term safely dropped.

\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/fig_anchor_placement.pdf}
\caption{Why the anchor is placed at $0.55$\,m --- real Apollo~17 output (note the

\label{fig:anchorplacement}
\end{figure}

\section{Step A, hour by hour}
\label{sec:stepA}

Step~A is the forward solver of \S\,Step~5 run for $n_{\rm in}$ lunations. Think
of it as a state machine carrying just the temperature vector $T^n$ (69 numbers),
the surface temperature $T_s^n$, and the running sums needed for the cycle mean.
Each hour runs three sub-operations in order (\code{code/src/lunar/solver.py},
\code{\_step}); Figure~\ref{fig:marchflow} draws the whole loop as a
process, including the two midpoint-Picard corrector sweeps and the
cycle-closing wrap step of App.~\ref{app:dtcert}:

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/marchflow}
\caption{One time step of the certified march
(\code{solver.\_step}, dispatched to the compiled kernel
\code{\_march\_radiative\_hayne} in production), and how steps chain
into lunations: the teal loop is the midpoint-Picard property correction
(App.~\ref{app:dtcert}); the green box is the wrap step that closes each
cycle exactly.}
\label{fig:marchflow}
\end{figure}

{\small\color{dim}\textit{Code: \code{code/src/lunar/solver.py} $\to$ \code{\_step}}}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Freeze material properties at $T^n$.} Evaluate $K,\rho,c_p$ at the
  old temperature. This linearisation costs nothing: $K$ changes by far
  less than a percent over one step.
\item \textbf{Newton at the surface.} With $T_{\rm sub}=T_0^n$, solve
  $(1-A)S(t)-\varepsilon\sigma T_s^4 - K_{\rm surf}(T_s-T_{\rm sub})/(\Delta z_0/2)=0$
  to $10^{-4}$\,K --- 2--3 iterations.
\item \textbf{Assemble the tridiagonal system and run Thomas.} Build
  $a_i,b_i,c_i,d_i$ from $T^n$, modify row~0 (surface ghost from Newton's
  $T_s^{n+1}$) and row $n{-}1$ (basal ghost from $Q_b$), solve once for
  $T^{n+1}$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Nothing is carried between hours except the temperature state and the
running sums: the matrix is rebuilt each hour (because $K$ depends on the current
$T$), and Newton restarts each hour (because $S(t)$ has moved). After
$n_{\rm in}$ lunations Step~A returns three things for Step~B: the cycle mean
$\Tm(z)$, the anchor value $\Tm(z_0)$, and $u_{\rm rect}(z)$. Wall-clock cost:
a few seconds per 96-lunation stage-2 Step~A, JIT-compiled (the full 4-cycle
solve is $\sim$0.6\,s, \S\ref{sec:fullsolve}).

\section{One Crank--Nicolson hour, worked}\label{sec:cnhour}

The three sub-operations are abstract until you watch them move real numbers, so
do one hour by hand on the 5-cell toy of Fig.~\ref{fig:toy}: uniform
$\Delta z=0.10$\,m, $K=10$\,mW\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, volumetric heat capacity
$\rho c_p=9\!\times\!10^{5}$, so each cell's heat capacity per area is
$C=\rho c_p\,\Delta z=9\!\times\!10^{4}$ and the coupling is
\[
\alpha=\frac{\Delta t\,K}{\Delta z\,C}=\frac{3600\times0.010}{0.10\times9\!\times\!10^{4}}=4\!\times\!10^{-3}.
\]
Read $\alpha$ as ``how hard does a cell pull its neighbor in one hour?'' --- here,
very gently. Collecting every $T^{n+1}$ on the left, the CN update for an interior
cell is
\[
-\tfrac12\alpha\,T_{i-1}^{n+1}+(1+\alpha)\,T_i^{n+1}-\tfrac12\alpha\,T_{i+1}^{n+1}
=\tfrac12\alpha\,T_{i-1}^{n}+(1-\alpha)\,T_i^{n}+\tfrac12\alpha\,T_{i+1}^{n},
\]
so each row carries coefficients $(-0.002,\,1.004,\,-0.002)$ --- tridiagonal,
because a cell two rows away contributes nothing in one step.

\input{figures-tikz/toy5cell}
\caption{The 5-cell toy column. The surface temperature comes from the Newton
solve; the basal flux $Q_b$ enters at the bottom. It is used twice: for one
worked Crank--Nicolson hour (this section) and for the Step~B downward walk
(\S\ref{sec:stepB}).}
\label{fig:toy}
\end{figure}

\noindent Start the toy uniform at $250$\,K. The boundary conditions are \textbf{four edits, nothing
more}: \emph{Newton at the surface} returns $T_s^{n+1}\approx372$\,K at midday
($S\approx1221$\,W\,m$^{-2}$) and enters row 0 only through its right-hand side,
$d_0\to d_0-\tfrac12\alpha T_0^n+\tfrac12\alpha(T_s^n+T_s^{n+1})\approx250.99$;
the \emph{basal ghost cell} carries $Q_b$ into row 4 by subtracting
$\tfrac12\alpha$ from the last diagonal ($b_4=1.004\to1.002$) and adding the flux
to the RHS ($d_4=250+\Delta t\,Q_b/C\approx250.00084$). The system is then
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
1.004 & -0.002 & & & \\
-0.002 & 1.004 & -0.002 & & \\

 & & -0.002 & 1.004 & -0.002 \\
 & & & -0.002 & \mathbf{1.002}
\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{T}^{n+1}=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{250.99}\\250\\250\\250\\\mathbf{250.00084}\end{bmatrix},
\]
the bold entries the only ones the boundaries touched. Now \textbf{Thomas}: the
forward sweep replaces each $(b_i,d_i)$ by modified $(c_i',d_i')$,
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}cccc@{}}
\toprule
$i$ & $b_i-a_ic_{i-1}'$ & $c_i'$ & $d_i'$ \\
\midrule
0 & 1.004    & $-0.001992$ & 249.988 \\
1 & 1.003996 & $-0.001992$ & 249.503 \\
2 & 1.003996 & $-0.001992$ & 249.503 \\
3 & 1.003996 & $-0.001992$ & 249.503 \\
4 & 1.001996 & ---         & 249.999 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
and backward substitution from the bottom up gives
\[
\mathbf{T}^{n+1}=[\,250.486,\;250.001,\;250.001,\;250.001,\;249.999\,]\;\text{K}.
\]
The surface cell warmed $\sim$0.49\,K (it sits next to the 372\,K surface, and the
implicit half-step hands it about half the conductive pull); the middle cells
barely moved (one hour is far too short for heat to reach them); the deepest cell
gained $\sim$0.001\,K from the basal flux. That is one hour. Step~A is this hour
repeated $\sim$1417 times per lunation for 96 lunations --- and \emph{that} is
the whole forward solve the slope method leans on.

\section{Step B as RK2 --- walking the deep profile down}\label{sec:stepB}

Step~B integrates the closure ODE from the anchor down to $z_{\max}$ by the
midpoint rule (Runge--Kutta order 2; its $O(\Delta z^3)$ local error is shown in
App.~\ref{app:rk2}), one cell at a time. To see it concretely,
walk the same toy column (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy}) with anchor at $z_0=0.05$\,m,
$K=10$\,mW\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ (taken constant for clarity),
$Q_b=21$\,mW\,m$^{-2}$, $u_{\rm rect}\approx0$ (we are below the skin), and
suppose Step~A returned $\Tm(z_0)=253.40$\,K. The step from cell $i$ to cell
$i{+}1$ is:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \emph{Slope at the start of the cell:}
  $s_i = (Q_b-u_{{\rm rect},i})/K(\Tm_i,z_i)= 0.021/0.010 = 2.10$\,K\,m$^{-1}$.
\item \emph{Predict at the midpoint:}
  $\Tm_{i+1/2} = \Tm_i + (\Delta z/2)\,s_i = 253.40 + 0.05\times2.10 = 253.505$\,K.
\item \emph{Slope at the midpoint:}
  $s_{i+1/2} = (Q_b-u_{{\rm rect},i+1/2})/K(\Tm_{i+1/2},z_{i+1/2}) \approx 2.10$\,K\,m$^{-1}$.
\item \emph{Step using the midpoint slope:}
  $\Tm_{i+1} = \Tm_i + \Delta z\,s_{i+1/2} = 253.40 + 0.10\times2.10 = 253.61$\,K.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Repeat cell by cell to the bottom. With $K$ taken constant here the
slope is $2.10$\,K\,m$^{-1}$ at every step (RK2 is then exact), so each $0.10$\,m
cell adds $0.21$\,K:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}cccccc@{}}
\toprule
$i$ & $z_i$ (m) & $\Tm_i$ (K) & slope $s_i$ (K\,m$^{-1}$) & $\Tm_{i+1/2}$ (K) & $\Tm_{i+1}$ (K) \\
\midrule
0 & 0.05 & 253.40 & 2.10 & 253.505 & 253.61 \\
1 & 0.15 & 253.61 & 2.10 & 253.715 & 253.82 \\
2 & 0.25 & 253.82 & 2.10 & 253.925 & 254.03 \\
3 & 0.35 & 254.03 & 2.10 & 254.135 & 254.24 \\
4 & 0.45 & 254.24 & --- & --- & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The whole walk is five evaluations of $K$ and four cell-to-cell slopes
--- the deep column rebuilds itself in microseconds. In the real solver $K(\Tm,z)$
rises with depth, so the slope shrinks slightly cell to cell and the column curves
gently rather than running perfectly straight; the arithmetic is identical. The
real code (\code{\_reconstruct\_subskin},
\code{code/src/lunar/equilibrium.py}) is identical except it loops over $\sim$60
cells and uses the true depth- and temperature-dependent $K(\Tm,z)$ at each step:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
T_new = T_mean.copy()                    # keep the skin as-is from Step A
for i in range(i0, N-1):                 # walk downward from the anchor
    dz = z[i+1] - z[i]
    q  = Q_b - u_rect[i]                 # flux minus the small eddy correction
    Ki = K(T_new[i], z[i])               # conductivity here
    T_half = T_new[i] + 0.5*q/Ki*dz      # RK2 midpoint
    K_half = K(T_half, 0.5*(z[i]+z[i+1]))
    T_new[i+1] = T_new[i] + q/K_half*dz  # THE STEP: T_next = T_here + slope*dz
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{plain}
The part people find hardest to believe is that we know the deep temperature
\emph{without simulating} it. In steady state, energy conservation fixes the
answer: the same $Q_b$ watts flow through every layer, and Fourier's law turns
that flux into a slope. So we walk downward adding ``slope $\times$ step.'' It is
exactly like finding elevation on a hill --- know one point and the slope
everywhere, and you can compute the height anywhere.
\end{plain}

\section{The outer loop as a fixed-point search}\label{sec:fixedpoint}

Steps A and B together define an operator on profiles:
\[
T_{\rm init}(z)\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{A}\;}\;\bigl(\Tm(z),\;u_{\rm rect}(z)\bigr)
\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{B}\;}\;T_{\rm recon}(z),
\]
and we set $T_{\rm init}\leftarrow T_{\rm recon}$ and repeat
(Fig.~\ref{fig:stepsABflow} draws this loop, decision diamond and all).
Convergence is a
\emph{fixed point}: $T_{\rm init}=T_{\rm recon}$. Why does a unique one exist?
Two contracting properties:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A is contractive on the skin.} Any starting profile, however wrong,
  relaxes toward the true skin solution within $\sim$3 lunations of Step~A,
  because the skin diffusive timescale is short.
\item \textbf{B is exact below the anchor.} The ODE Step~B integrates \emph{is}
  the steady-state equation, not an approximation. Given a correct anchor, B
  returns the true deep profile in one application.
\end{itemize}
Composed, $B\circ A$ pulls every starting profile toward the unique fixed point
where the skin matches the average of the forward solves \emph{and} the deep
column matches its own steady-state ODE. That fixed point \emph{is} the periodic
steady state.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/fixedpoint}
\caption{The outer loop as a fixed-point search. Each cycle (Step~A $+$
Step~B) moves the anchor temperature $\sim$60\% less than the last ---
the geometric contraction of a convergent fixed-point iteration; the loop
stops when the drift enters the $\pm 0.005$~K band. The drift sequence shown
is the illustrative example of App.~\ref{app:worked}.}
\label{fig:fixedpoint}
\end{figure}

\begin{plain}
\textbf{Why the drift shrinks so fast.} The anchor temperature is set almost
entirely by the surface sunlight balance, and the geothermal flux $Q_b$ is only
${\sim}10^{-4}$ of the peak daily flux. So when Step~B rebuilds the deep column
underneath, it barely tugs the anchor --- each outer cycle removes most of the
error that is left, and the cycle-to-cycle ``drift'' (how far $\Tm(z_0)$ moved
since last pass) falls by a near-constant factor ($\sim$0.6 in the trace below).
Three to five cycles take it under tolerance. This is why we watch the
\emph{anchor's} drift, not the skin's --- the skin settled long ago, so the
anchor is the last thing still moving, and when it stops, everything has arrived.
\end{plain}

\noindent Ch.~\ref{ch:bruteforce} confirms the landing point empirically: a fully
converged brute-force run reaches the \emph{same} profile, agreeing to
${\sim}0.1$\,K below $0.3$\,m (Fig.~\ref{fig:overlay}).

{\small\color{dim}\textit{Code: \code{code/src/lunar/equilibrium.py} $\to$ \code{solve\_periodic\_equilibrium}}}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
for stage in stages:                 # (z0=0.25, loose) then (z0=0.55, tight)
    for it in range(stage["max_it"]):
        out = solve_pixel(...)       # Step A: settle the skin over n_in lunations
        u_rect = _rectified_flux(out.T, z, K_func)
        T_init = _reconstruct_subskin(T_mean, z, i_s, Q_b, K_func, u_rect)  # Step B
        if abs(anchor - anchor_prev) < stage["tol"]:   # anchor stopped moving?
            break                    # converged -> advance to the next stage
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent The rectified flux $u_{\rm rect}$ is concentrated in the top
$\sim$30\,cm. Anchor inside that zone and you integrate through a layer where

fast but slightly biased. So stage~1 \emph{uses} that on purpose: a shallow
anchor at 0.25\,m with short 4-lunation runs drags the bulk of the deep column
into the right ballpark cheaply (loose 0.10\,K tolerance, because stage~2 will
tighten it). Stage~2 then drops the anchor to 0.55\,m, comfortably below the
daily wave where $u_{\rm rect}\approx0$, and runs 96-lunation inner solves long
enough for the skin to fully equilibrate, closing the residual at a ten-times
tighter $0.005$\,K. In plain words: stage~1 gets you to the right neighborhood
cheaply; stage~2 polishes you to the final number.

\section{Certification --- three checks, with real numbers}\label{sec:cert}

When the loop thinks it has converged, the solver runs three diagnostics
(\code{\_mean\_flux\_closure}, \code{code/src/lunar/equilibrium.py}). None of them
is the F1 trap (cycle-to-cycle change), and none can be passed by accident ---
each is designed to kill one specific failure mode
(Fig.~\ref{fig:certflow}). For the certified $K_d=4.60$ solve at Apollo~15:

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/certflow}
\caption{Certification: a converged solve must survive three independent
checks before its profile is allowed into the $K_d$ sweep. Each check is
matched to the failure mode it rules out.}
\label{fig:certflow}
\end{figure}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Check & Quantity measured & Threshold & Observed \\
\midrule
Guess-independence & $\max_z|T_{(240)}(z)-T_{(260)}(z)|$  & $\le 0.05$ K   & $5\times10^{-6}$ K \\
Flux closure       & $|K\,d\Tm/dz - Q_b|/Q_b$ at $0.55,1.5,3.0$ m & $\le 10\%$ & $1.2\%,\,0.8\%,\,0.4\%$ \\
Mean drift         & 120-lun.\ honest re-run drift     & $\le 0.08$ K   & $0.0024$ K \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent All three pass with margin, and each rules out a specific failure:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Guess-independence} rules out the F1 trap: if the answer depended on
  the starting profile, we would be at a fixed point of ``equations $+$ guess,''
  not of the equations.
\item \emph{Flux closure} rules out an inconsistent Step~B: if $K\,d\Tm/dz\ne
  Q_b$ below the anchor, the deep ODE is not satisfied and the iterate is not
  steady.
\item \emph{Mean drift} rules out near-misses: a slightly-off iterate would be
  pushed by honest time integration; $\le0.08$\,K over 120 lunations means we are
  on the manifold of true steady states, not merely near it.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{\textbf{One level up: is the inner spin-up itself converged?}} The
three checks above certify a \emph{single} solve at a given inner spin-up length
$n_{\rm inner}$ (stage~2 of \S\ref{sec:twostage}). A quieter question sits one
level up: is $n_{\rm inner}$ \emph{itself} long enough? Stop the skin's spin-up
too early and every solve is still internally consistent --- yet biased --- and the
retrieved $K_d^{*}$ inherits the bias. The honest test is to \emph{lengthen}
$n_{\rm inner}$ and watch whether the answer stops moving (Fig.~\ref{fig:ninner}).
It does: at Apollo~17 the retrieved $K_d^{*}$ falls from $7.5$ (badly
under-resolved at $n_{\rm inner}=12$) and settles onto a \emph{plateau} at
$7.08$\,mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$; convergence is declared on that plateau ---
$|\Delta K_d^{*}|<0.005$\,mW between successive $n_{\rm inner}$ --- which the
production setting $n_{\rm inner}=96$ clears with room to spare. The flux-closure
residual (panel~b) corroborates: it drops below $0.5\%$ of $Q_b$ well before $96$,
then \emph{floors} at the grid limit ($\approx0.11\%$). That floor is exactly why
$K_d^{*}$ stability --- not flux closure alone --- is the primary criterion:
closure cannot tell ``converged'' apart from ``grid-limited.'' Apollo~15, lower in
conductivity and hence in diffusivity, converges slower still, so it is the
binding site that fixes $n_{\rm inner}=96$ for both.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{figures/fig_convergence_study.pdf}
\caption{Strict $n_{\rm inner}$ convergence of the production truncated-Step~A

\emph{(a)} the retrieved $K_d^{*}$ reaching its plateau at $7.08$; the shaded band

\section{The speed-up, in measured numbers}\label{sec:speedup}

The payoff is wall-clock, and it is worth stating with the real timings
rather than a round figure --- and, since 2026-07-03, there are \emph{two}
independent speed-ups to state. All numbers below are measured on this
machine (median of three quiet-machine runs) at the certified production
settings ($\Delta t = 1800$\,s with the wrap step, two midpoint-Picard
sweeps per step) and archived in \code{code/results/speedup\_benchmark.json}
and \code{code/results/cpp\_benchmark.json}.

\paragraph{\textbf{Speed-up 1 --- the method (fewer steps).}} A single
flux-anchored solve reaches the certified steady state in $\sim$75\,s with
the plain interpreted march. Brute-force time integration reaches the
\emph{same} state only on the diffusive timescale: $\sim$523\,ms per
simulated lunation, so the $\sim$3000 lunations needed to come within
$\sim$0.1\,K cost $\sim$1570\,s ($\sim$26 minutes) --- a per-solve
speed-up of
\[
\frac{T_{\rm brute}}{T_{\rm anchored}}
\;\approx\;\frac{\sim 1570\,\text{s}}{\sim 75\,\text{s}}
\;\approx\;\boxed{21\times.}
\]
This gain comes from stepping \emph{less}: the flux-anchored loop
time-steps only the truncated skin column for a few hundred sub-skin
lunations instead of $\sim$3000 full-column ones.

\paragraph{\textbf{Speed-up 2 --- the kernel (cheaper steps).}} The
corrected numerics tripled the arithmetic per step (three tridiagonal
solves instead of one) and halved the time step, which exposed the real
bottleneck: the tridiagonal \emph{assembly} and the Newton surface solve
ran as interpreted Python. The march is now compiled end-to-end as a Numba
kernel (\code{solver.\_march\_radiative\_hayne}, dispatched via
\code{PixelInputs.hayne\_params} and proven equal to the interpreted path
to $<10^{-9}$\,K by a unit test). Same physics, same machine: one
flux-anchored solve drops from $\sim$75\,s to $\boxed{0.64\,\text{s}}$ ---
another $\sim$117$\times$, \emph{multiplicative} with the method's
$21\times$.

\paragraph{\textbf{What it means for the pipeline.}} The retrieval calls
the solver $29+32=61$ times (the dense $K_d$ sweep at both sites). The
three tiers of the same sweep:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
 & one solve & full 61-solve sweep \\
\midrule
brute force                     & $\sim$26 min & $\sim$26.6 h \\
flux-anchored (interpreted)     & $\sim$75 s   & $\sim$76 min \\
flux-anchored $+$ compiled kernel & $\sim$0.64 s & $\sim$39 s \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The $1500$-draw bootstrap multiplies \emph{none} of these: it re-uses the
$61$ cached profiles and only re-selects which residuals enter each RMSE,
adding seconds (\S\,Step~9). The kernel is also what makes the
\emph{certification} machinery routine: a full $K_d^{*}$ dt ladder
(App.~\ref{app:dtcert}), the direct degeneracy map
(\S\ref{sec:degmeasured}), and the 234-solve MCMC surface each cost
minutes, not afternoons.

\paragraph{\textbf{The C++ cross-check.}} How close is the compiled Python
kernel to native code? A line-for-line C++ port of the march
(\code{code/cpp/solver.cpp}) is kept as an independent verification: all three
implementations agree to $\sim$$10^{-12}$\,K, and per simulated lunation
they time at $443$\,ms (interpreted Python), $3.9$\,ms (compiled Python
kernel), and $2.8$\,ms (C++). The kernel therefore captures $\sim$70\% of
native speed with no build step or foreign-function boundary --- which is
why it is the production path and the C++ port is the cross-check rather
than the engine (\code{code/results/cpp\_benchmark.json}).

\chapter{How the slope method differs from brute force}\label{ch:bruteforce}
% =====================================================================

Chapter~\ref{ch:slope} built the slope method. This chapter puts it side by side
with the brute-force spin-up it replaces, so the difference is unmistakable. It
is not merely ``faster'': it computes a different thing, tests a different thing,
and certifies what the old method could not.

\section{The conceptual difference}

Brute force and the slope method answer the same question --- what is the
periodic steady state? --- by opposite routes.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.6cm}p{5.4cm}p{5.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Brute force} & \textbf{Slope method (flux-anchored)} \\
\midrule
Strategy & Integrate forward in time until things stop changing. & Use the steady-state equation directly below an anchor depth. \\
\addlinespace
Deep column & Waits for heat to diffuse in --- the $\sim$1000-lunation timescale of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tau}. & Reconstructed by integrating $d\Tm/dz=(Q_b-u_{\rm rect})/K$ in microseconds. \\
\addlinespace
Convergence test & Cycle-to-cycle change $\Delta T<0.01$\,K --- which the skin satisfies long before the deep column does. & Anchor-temperature change between outer cycles $<0.005$\,K --- the quantity that actually still moves. \\
\addlinespace
What you get & A profile that may still remember the starting guess. & A certified, guess-independent steady state. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The asymmetry is the point. Brute force spends all its effort on the
\emph{slow} part of the problem (the deep diffusion) and tests the \emph{fast}
part (the skin). The slope method does the opposite: it spends a short forward
solve on the fast skin --- which genuinely needs time-stepping --- and
\emph{computes} the slow deep column from the equation it must satisfy. You only
time-step what is fast; you solve what is slow.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/old_vs_new_filmstrip.pdf}
\caption{The two routes to steady state, as snapshots (real solver output).
\emph{Left two panels --- old:} brute force has barely moved the deep column at
30 lunations and is still creeping at $\sim$1000. \emph{Right four panels ---
new:} a short run settles the skin, the anchor is dropped at $z_0=0.55$\,m, the
closure slope is walked downward to fill the deep column, and the solve is
converged in $\sim$75\,s interpreted ($\sim$0.6\,s compiled). An animated version (\code{figures/anim/old\_vs\_new.gif})
is available for talks.}
\label{fig:spinfilm}
\end{figure}

\section{The convergence-test difference}

This is the subtle, load-bearing difference. The brute-force test asks ``did the
profile stop changing between lunations?'' --- but the deep drift is so slow
($\sim$$10^{-3}$\,K/lunation) that the answer is ``yes'' while the profile is
still tens of kelvin off (\S\ref{sec:f1}). It is an \emph{honest-looking} test of
the \emph{wrong quantity}. The slope method's test asks ``did the anchor
temperature stop moving between outer cycles?'' Because each outer cycle
reconstructs the entire deep column from the anchor, a wrong anchor produces a
visibly wrong reconstruction on the next pass --- so when the anchor stops
moving, the deep column really has arrived. The test tracks the quantity that is
still in motion, not the one that settled first.

\section{The accuracy difference}

When brute force is given \emph{enough} time --- not 30 lunations but several
hundred --- it converges to the same profile the slope method produces in
seconds, from any starting guess. We checked directly (Fig.~\ref{fig:overlay}):
brute-force runs from two very different starting temperatures (240\,K and
260\,K) both march toward the \emph{same} flux-anchored equilibrium, and their
gap to it decays past the shortcut's own tolerance only after several hundred
lunations. At the literature's 30-lunation cut-off that gap is still many kelvin;
the slope method reaches the identical answer in $\sim$75\,s from either guess
(interpreted tier; $\sim$0.6\,s with the compiled kernel).
The two methods agree --- the slope method just refuses to wait. (The
guess-independence is certified to ${\sim}10^{-5}$\,K directly in \S\ref{sec:cert}.)

\begin{keypt}[title={Head-to-head, re-checked at $Q_b(\mathrm{A17})=16$}]
A direct run at the corrected basal flux makes the agreement concrete. Measuring
a brute-force spin-up against the flux-anchored answer over the sensor band
(0.8--2.4\,m):
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
brute-force spin-up & 120 lun. & 360 lun. & 720 lun. \\
\midrule
gap to shortcut (cold 240\,K start) & 10.7\,K & 5.1\,K & 1.7\,K \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent The gap falls monotonically and is still shrinking at 720 lunations,
heading for the ${\sim}0.1$\,K merge that full convergence reaches near 3000
(Fig.~\ref{fig:overlay}). Meanwhile two \emph{different} brute-force starts
(240 and 270\,K) still disagree with \emph{each other} by $8.8$\,K at 360
lunations --- proof that at any affordable spin-up brute force is still
remembering its initial guess, while the shortcut has already arrived and is
guess-independent. \emph{Same destination, opposite economics.}
\end{keypt}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_equilibrium_demo.pdf}
\caption{Brute force \emph{reaches} the flux-anchored shortcut. \textbf{(a)} Two
brute-force runs from very different starting guesses (240\,K, 260\,K) both
converge onto the \emph{same} shortcut equilibrium (dashed): by $\sim$3000
lunations the two curves have merged onto it --- they have arrived, not just

\section{The wall-clock difference}

We measured this in \S\ref{sec:speedup}: per solve, brute force needs
$\sim$3000 lunations ($\sim$26 minutes) to approach the steady state the
flux-anchored loop certifies in $\sim$75\,s --- about $21\times$ faster --- and
across the 61-solve sweep that is $\sim$76 minutes versus $\sim$26.6 hours at the interpreted tier --- and $\sim$39 seconds with the compiled kernel (the
bootstrap re-uses cached profiles, so it multiplies neither side). The point for
this comparison is \emph{where} the saving comes from: it is not a faster inner step
(the inner step is identical), it is replacing 1000 lunations of deep diffusion
with a $\sim$microsecond ODE integration. The skin work is unchanged; the deep
work all but vanishes.

\section{Why nobody did this before}

A fair question: the closure equation~\eqref{eq:closure} was always known --- it
is just energy conservation in steady state. Why was it not used as a solver? The
honest answer is that the literature treated the closure as a \emph{diagnostic}
--- a check you run on a converged profile to confirm the flux balances --- rather
than as a \emph{solver primitive} you integrate to \emph{produce} the profile.
Once you see that the deep column does not need to be diffused into place but can
be \emph{integrated} into place below an anchor, the 1000-lunation wait
evaporates. Treating the closure as the integrator itself, with an anchor and an
outer fixed-point loop, is the methodological contribution of this work.

\chapter{Where every input comes from}\label{ch:inputs}
% =====================================================================

After this chapter you will be able to take \emph{any} number that enters the
retrieval --- a physical constant, a solver setting, a convergence tolerance, a
statistical choice --- and point to the evidence that justifies it: a paper you
can open, a measurement on this machine, or a convergence test archived in
\code{code/results/}. Just as importantly, you will know what an \emph{unfounded}
input looks like, because this project was bitten by three of them.

\begin{plain}
A model is a machine that turns inputs into an answer. If one input is wrong,
the machine still runs, still converges, still prints a confident number ---
just the \emph{wrong} number, with no alarm. So every input needs a
\emph{pedigree}: where it comes from, and what would show us if it were wrong.
The rule used throughout this study: \textbf{an input is founded when (i) it
has a source you can open, or (ii) a test in \code{code/results/} shows the answer
has stopped depending on it.} Anything else is a guess wearing a lab coat.
\end{plain}

\section{Three inputs that bit us}\label{sec:threebites}

Each of these was believed settled, and was not. They are worth studying
because each teaches a different way an input can be unfounded.

\paragraph{\textbf{1. The convergence criterion that tested the wrong thing
(the F1 bug).}} The original spin-up ran a \emph{fixed 30 lunations} and
declared victory when consecutive cycles agreed. But cycle-to-cycle agreement
tests the \emph{fast} part of the system (the skin, settled in $\sim$10
lunations) while the slow deep column was still 10--40\,K from its
destination (\S\ref{sec:f1}). The input here was a \emph{criterion}, and it
was unfounded because it never asked the question we cared about.
\emph{The fix:} convergence is now declared on the quantity that matters ---
the anchor temperature drift, $|\Delta\Tm(z_0)|<0.005$\,K --- and then
\emph{certified} by three independent checks (\S\ref{sec:cert}), each designed
to rule out a specific failure.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/threebugs}
\caption{The three inputs that bit us, as a process: what each wrong value
looked like, the symptom that exposed it, the structural fix, and the move
it produced in $K_d^{*}$(A17). Every move is recorded --- the audit trail
is part of the result.}
\label{fig:threebugs}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{\textbf{2. The spin-up length that looked converged and wasn't
($n_{\rm inner}=12$).}} Every individual solve at $n_{\rm inner}=12$ was
internally consistent --- it passed its own tolerance --- yet the retrieved
$K_d^{*}$(A17) was biased $\sim$0.4\,mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ high. The lesson is
subtle: an input can pass \emph{its own} test and still contaminate the answer.
The only honest test is to \emph{lengthen} the input and watch the final answer:
$K_d^{*}$ drifts monotonically and then reaches a plateau
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ninner}), and the criterion is plateau stability,
$|\Delta K_d^{*}|<0.005$\,mW between successive $n_{\rm inner}$. Note that the
flux-closure residual could \emph{not} serve as the criterion --- it floors at
the grid limit ($\approx$0.12\%), so ``closure is small'' cannot distinguish
``converged'' from ``grid-limited.'' The production value $n_{\rm inner}=96$ is
therefore not a preference; it is where the plateau test puts it, at the
slower-converging site (Apollo~15, lower $K$, lower diffusivity).

\paragraph{\textbf{3. The physical constant that was silently wrong
($Q_b$ at Apollo 17).}} For weeks the pipeline ran with $Q_b(\mathrm{A17})=
0.015$\,W\,m$^{-2}$. The revised borehole value in Langseth et al.\ (1976) is
$1.6\,\mu$W\,cm$^{-2} = 0.016$\,W\,m$^{-2}$. Nothing crashed; the retrieval
simply returned a different $K_d^{*}$. Worse, a stale copy of the old value
survived inside the error-budget script after the main config was fixed ---
\emph{duplicated constants drift independently}. \emph{The fix:} one source of
truth (\code{config.SITES}), a source comment naming the paper beside the
value, and an audit habit of grepping for hardcoded duplicates.

\begin{keypt}
Three failure modes, one meta-lesson: ``it is in the code and the code runs''
is not a pedigree. A criterion must test the quantity you report; a numerical
setting must be pushed until the \emph{answer} stops moving; a physical
constant must cite a page you can check --- and live in exactly one place.
\end{keypt}

\section{How to challenge an input yourself}\label{sec:challenge}

The register is only trustworthy if it can be re-audited.
Figure~\ref{fig:inputflow} is the decision walk every number must survive;
the recipe below is the same walk in prose.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/inputflow}
\caption{Is this input founded? Three chances to justify a number ---
literature, convergence artifact, sensitivity row --- and a loud default when
all three fail. Every bug in \S\ref{sec:threebites} lived in the red box.}
\label{fig:inputflow}
\end{figure}

The recipe used in this project, in four steps:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Name the criterion.} What question does this input answer, and
  what quantity does its test actually measure? (The F1 bug lived in the gap
  between those two.)
\item \textbf{Open the evidence.} A citation goes to the paper; a
  convergence-tested value goes to its scan
  (\code{code/results/convergence\_scan.json}); a measured timing goes to its
  archive (\code{code/results/speedup\_benchmark.json}, written by
  \code{code/pipeline/compute/benchmark\_speedup.py}).
\item \textbf{Wiggle it.} Change the input and re-run; the error budget's
  sensitivity rows are exactly this, automated
  (\code{code/pipeline/compute/compute\_error\_budget.py}).
\item \textbf{Record the before/after.} A moved number is a finding, not an
  embarrassment. The $Q_b$ correction changed $K_d^{*}$(A17) from 8.12 to 7.51;
  the $n_{\rm inner}$ fix moved it again to 7.16; the wrap-step and
  midpoint-Picard corrections (App.~\ref{app:dtcert}) moved it to 7.08. Each
  move is documented, and
  that is what makes the final number credible.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{warn}
The most dangerous inputs are the ones nobody remembers choosing. If you meet
a constant in the code with no comment, no citation, and no scan --- treat it
as wrong until proven otherwise. That posture found the $Q_b$ error; the
posture ``it has always been that value'' hid it.
\end{warn}

\noindent\textbf{Is a factor of 1.5 big?} For dry regolith, deep conductivity is
set mostly by grain-to-grain contact area, which scales super-linearly with
packing density ($K\propto\rho^{n}$, $n\gtrsim4$ in many granular-contact
models). Going from loose mare regolith to a compacted, rock-rich highland column
is only a $\sim$15\% density jump, but the contact-area scaling can push
conductivity by a factor of $\sim$2. So the contrast we measure sits right in the
middle of the range you would expect from a real mare/highland change --- neither
implausibly large nor a fluke.

\noindent\textbf{Why both numbers sit above Hayne's 3.4.} Hayne's global value is
an area-weighted average of the whole Moon. Weighting our two retrievals by the
same global coverage (17\% mare, 83\% highland) gives
$\bar K_d=0.17(4.60)+0.83(7.08)\approx6.7$ --- about twice Hayne's $3.4$. Two
points cannot overturn a global fit, but they do show the local in-situ signal
can sit a factor of $\sim$2 above the global average, and that any future
heat-flow probe will see a similar spread between its borehole and the global
mean.

\noindent\textbf{What the contrast does and does not imply.} It \emph{does} imply
the deep regolith at Apollo~17 is denser, more compacted, or more rock-rich than
at Apollo~15 --- all expected from the geology (Taurus--Littrow is a highland
valley with dark-mantle pyroclastics and buried massif blocks; Hadley Rille is
mare basalt). It does \emph{not} imply the geothermal interior differs: $Q_b$ is
a separate, fixed input. And it does \emph{not} make one site ``right'' and the
other ``wrong'' --- both are correct at their own borehole.

\section{The single largest caveat: conditionality on $\chi$}\label{sec:chi}

The formal error budget below is honest about what \emph{varies} in the published
inputs. But the largest single sensitivity is not in that budget at all --- it is
the radiative coefficient $\chi$ in Hayne's $K(T,z)$.

\begin{warn}
$\chi$ is held fixed at $2.7$ because Hayne~(2017) reports that as the best global
Diviner fit --- and Vasavada et al.~(2012) adopt the \emph{same} value, stating that
they ``increase $c$ to 2.7 (from 1.5)'' in their revised model. So there is no
published disagreement to appeal to: $2.7$ is the consensus calibration.
\emph{(An earlier draft of this book, and the manuscripts, described
$\chi=1.48$ as ``the Vasavada normalization''. That attribution is wrong --- 1.48
appears in neither paper, and 1.5 is Vasavada's superseded pre-revision value.)}
What the sensitivity actually tests is a \emph{low-$\chi$ stress test}. Push $\chi$
materially below $2.7$ --- at $1.48$ or at $1.5$, the two are numerically
indistinguishable --- and the retrieval loses its interior minimum entirely, railing
at the sweep edges ($K_d^{*}(\mathrm{A15})\!\to\!1$,
$K_d^{*}(\mathrm{A17})\!\to\!25$): a far larger swing than the quadrature totals
($1.88$, $3.88$). The \emph{absolute} $K_d^{*}$ values are therefore conditional on
the adopted radiative calibration; the \emph{direction} of the contrast survives
(A17 more conductive than A15 at either $\chi$).
\end{warn}

\noindent We still report the $\chi=2.7$ numbers as headline because $\chi$ is
not a free parameter we are entitled to vary --- it is one knob of the published
Hayne form, and that form is what makes Hayne's global $3.4$ directly comparable
to our per-site values. The honest convention is to quote $K_d$ \emph{together
with} the $\chi$ it assumes; every $K_d$ in the literature, Hayne's included,
carries the same conditionality.

\section{Cross-checks: three independent ways in}

A $K_d$ that fits seven thermometers could still be wrong if the surrounding
physics is mis-specified. Three independent checks say it is not
(Fig.~\ref{fig:holdout} shows the sharpest one).
\emph{(i)~Diviner surface closure}: the same conductivity that fits the deep,
steady HFE temperatures also reproduces the \emph{surface} diurnal swing that
LRO's Diviner radiometer measures from orbit, to within $\sim$4--6\,K at both
sites --- two disjoint slices of physics (deep gradient $\propto Q_b/K$; surface
swing $\propto K$ via skin depth), mutually consistent. \emph{(ii)~Martínez
density model}: re-running with the entirely different Martínez \& Siegler (2021)
density-based conductivity form, and fitting a density scalar instead of $K_d$,
confirms that a simple bulk-density rescaling does \emph{not} by itself reproduce
the Hayne contrast --- so the A17 excess is better read as contact geometry or
buried blocks than as bulk density, a useful negative control. \emph{(iii)~Robustness
battery}: sliding the 80\,cm borestem cut over $\{70,80,90\}$\,cm, changing the
stable-window flatness threshold by $\pm$50\%, adding a $\pm$2\,K calibration

\section{The bootstrap CI and the error budget}\label{sec:resultstats}

Two kinds of uncertainty, measured two ways. The \textbf{bootstrap} (\S\,Step~9)
captures the \emph{statistical} scatter from the finite, noisy sensor set:
resample sensors with replacement, jitter depths, re-fit 1500 times, read the
percentiles. The two site distributions now overlap appreciably (Fig.~\ref{fig:boot}), and
the contrast tail probability is $p\approx0.031$ --- meaning that \emph{if} the
two sites truly had identical $K_d$, sensor noise alone would produce a contrast
this large about 3.1\% of the time. It is not the probability the sites are the
same; it is how surprising the data would be if they were.

The 95\% intervals, $[4.18,\,6.96]$ at A15 and $[6.16,\,8.07]$ at A17,
come from the bootstrap plug-in principle. We treat the observed sensors as a
stand-in population, draw 1500 sensor sets with replacement, re-fit $K_d^{*}$
for each draw, and read off the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. No Gaussian error
model is assumed; the resampling directly asks how much the answer would move if
the experiment had sampled a different set of deep thermometers from the same
site. The wider A15 interval reflects how strongly its result depends on which
of its seven deep sensors happen to carry the fit.

\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{figures/fig_bootstrap.pdf}

distributions overlap at the margins --- the contrast is only marginally
resolved, its 95\% interval now spanning zero ($p\approx0.031$).}
\label{fig:boot}
\end{figure}

the fixed inputs. Each published input (basal flux, albedo, surface
conductivity, density, depths, thresholds) is perturbed by its stated
uncertainty, the retrieval is re-run, and the induced shift in $K_d^{*}$ is
recorded; the independent contributions add in quadrature (justified in
App.~\ref{app:stats}):

\noindent Two lessons. The basal flux $Q_b$ and the albedo $A$ dominate ---
$Q_b$ because $K_d$ can only be reported \emph{conditional} on the assumed

$K_d^{*}(Q_b)$ map of \S\ref{sec:degmeasured} (not the old ratio rule,
which understated A15 and had A17's direction wrong). And Apollo~17's
budget is $\sim$2$\times$ wider than Apollo~15's, because its
absolute-temperature-dominated fit amplifies the same input
uncertainties. Through all of it the contrast direction holds: letting
$Q_b$ vary jointly with $K_d$ in the MCMC cross-check on the direct
surface, Apollo~17 stays the more conductive site in $99.2$--$99.4\%$ of
posterior draws whether the $Q_b$ prior is $\pm$10\% or $\pm$40\% wide
(\S\ref{sec:mcmczero}). The robust finding is the \emph{direction}; the
absolute numbers ride on the published $Q_b$ and $\chi$.

\begin{keypt}[title={What to take away}]
Three things, in order of confidence:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{$K_d$ can be measured per site}, not just assumed global for the
  whole Moon --- the Apollo~15 and~17 boreholes give $4.60$ and $7.08$
  mW\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$.
\item \textbf{The slope method is what made that affordable \emph{and} honest}:
  it reaches a \emph{certified} steady state in ${\sim}4$ outer cycles instead of
  ${\sim}3000$ lunations, and --- unlike a fixed spin-up --- carries no hidden
  initial-guess parameter to bias the answer.
\item \textbf{The contrast's \emph{direction} is robust; its \emph{size} is
  marginal.} Apollo~17 is the more conductive site in every cross-check, but at
  the published basal fluxes the 95\% interval just includes zero --- a firmer
  per-site $Q_b$ is what would settle it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{keypt}

\section{One number from many misses: RMSE}\label{sec:rmsezero}

\begin{plain}
Imagine throwing seven darts and asking ``how good was that, overall?'' You
could average the miss distances --- but a 3\,cm miss and a 30\,cm miss should
not feel merely 10$\times$ different; the big miss is the real problem. The
root-mean-square error (RMSE) is the average that \emph{punishes big misses}:
square each miss (so 30 hurts 100$\times$ more than 3), average the squares,
then take the square root so the answer is back in ordinary units.
\end{plain}

For each deep sensor $i$ the model predicts a temperature
$\Tm(z_i; K_d)$ and Apollo measured $T_{{\rm obs},i}$. The \emph{residual} is
the miss, $r_i = \Tm(z_i;K_d) - T_{{\rm obs},i}$, in kelvin. With $N$ sensors
($N{=}7$ at Apollo~15, $N{=}16$ at Apollo~17):
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}(K_d) \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} r_i^{\,2}}
\qquad\text{[K].}
\]
Read it inside-out: square each residual ($r_i^2$, punishing outliers), average
them ($\frac{1}{N}\sum$), undo the squaring ($\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Worked, by hand.} Three sensors miss by $r = (+0.3, -0.5,
+0.4)$\,K. Squares: $0.09, 0.25, 0.16$. Mean: $(0.09{+}0.25{+}0.16)/3 =
0.1667$. Root: $\sqrt{0.1667} = 0.41$\,K. Note the sign of each miss vanished
at the squaring step --- RMSE measures \emph{size} of disagreement, not
direction. The real site-fit values are $1.00$\,K (A15) and $0.40$\,K (A17).

\section{From a curve to one best value: the vertex}\label{sec:vertexzero}

\begin{plain}
Compute RMSE for many trial $K_d$ values and you get a curve with a valley:
too-low $K_d$ fits badly, too-high fits badly, and somewhere in between is a
bottom. The retrieved $K_d^{*}$ is the bottom of the valley. Because the sweep
only \emph{samples} the curve at grid points, the true bottom usually falls
between two samples --- so we fit a parabola through the three lowest points
and take \emph{its} minimum, which is exact if the valley is locally
parabolic (near a smooth minimum, everything is).
\end{plain}

The three-point vertex formula is derived step by step in
App.~\ref{app:vertex}, and App.~\ref{app:sweepworked} carries the real
Apollo~17 numbers through it by hand (bracket $6.80/7.00/7.20 \rightarrow
K_d^{*}=7.08$). One provenance note (\S\ref{sec:register}): the vertex is only
as good as the sampling around it --- the early \emph{uniform} sweep grid
carried ${\sim}0.04$\,mW of vertex bias, which is why the production grid packs
a dense $0.2$\,mW band around the minimum.

\section{``How wrong could it be?'' --- the bootstrap}\label{sec:bootzero}

\begin{plain}
$K_d^{*}$ came from seven (or sixteen) sensors. Had Apollo drilled a hand's
width to the left, every reading would differ slightly, and so would
$K_d^{*}$. We cannot re-fly the mission --- but we can ask the data itself to
stand in for the missions we never flew: build many \emph{alternative
datasets} by resampling the sensors we do have (drawing $N$ of them \emph{with
replacement}, so some appear twice and others not at all) and re-fitting
$K_d^{*}$ on each. The spread of those refits is an honest estimate of how
much the answer depends on the luck of the sample. That is the bootstrap.
\end{plain}

One bootstrap \emph{draw} here does two things: (i) resample the $N$ deep
sensors with replacement; (ii) jitter each sensor's depth by a random
$\mathcal{N}(0, 2.5\,\mathrm{cm})$ nudge --- the emplacement uncertainty from
Nagihara et al.\ (2018) --- then re-run the full vertex fit on the perturbed
dataset. Repeating 1500 times (seed 42, archived) gives 1500 plausible
$K_d^{*}$ values per site: the clouds of Fig.~\ref{fig:statsboot}.
Figure~\ref{fig:bootflow} traces one draw end to end; note that the loop is
cheap because it never calls the solver --- it re-reads the 29/32 profiles the
sweep already computed.

\section{The 95\% confidence interval}\label{sec:cizero}

\begin{plain}
Sort the 1500 draws from smallest to largest and cut off the bottom
2.5\,\% and the top 2.5\,\%. What remains is the middle 95\,\% --- the
\emph{percentile confidence interval}. It is the range of answers the data
consider plausible; values outside it required a luckier sample than 19 out
of 20.
\end{plain}

Sorting 1500 draws, the bottom cut falls after draw
$0.025 \times 1500 = 37.5 \approx 38$ and the top cut before draw
$\approx 1463$. For Apollo~15 those two draws sit at 4.18 and
6.96\,mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$; for Apollo~17 at 6.16 and 8.07. Notice the
Apollo~15 interval is \emph{not} symmetric about its median 4.63 ---
$-0.45$ below but $+2.33$ above --- because the cloud's long right tail
(Fig.~\ref{fig:statsboot}) pushes the upper cut far out. A symmetric
``$\pm\sigma$'' summary would hide that; the percentile interval keeps it.

\section{Do the two sites really differ? The contrast and its
tail}\label{sec:pzero}

\begin{plain}
Apollo 17's $K_d^{*}$ is higher than Apollo 15's. But both numbers wobble ---
so is the \emph{difference} real, or could it be luck? The bootstrap answers
directly: in each of the 1500 draws, subtract the A15 refit from the A17
refit (the draws are paired --- same random seed, draw $k$ against draw $k$).
That gives 1500 plausible values of the \emph{contrast}. Now count how many
of them come out at or below zero. That count, as a fraction, is how often
our own resampling failed to reproduce the ordering we are claiming.
\end{plain}

Panel~(b) of Figure~\ref{fig:boot} is exactly that count, drawn. The vertical
line at zero is ``no difference''; 46 of the 1500 draws fall at or below it,
so the fraction is $46/1500 = 0.031$.

\begin{warn}
\textbf{This number is not a $p$-value, and this book will not call it one.}
A $p$-value is computed under an assumed null hypothesis --- you build a world
in which the two sites are genuinely identical, and ask how often that world
produces data as lopsided as yours. We never built that world. What we did was
resample \emph{our own} sensors and re-run \emph{our own} retrieval. So
$0.031$ is a \textbf{bootstrap tail proportion}, written
$P_{\rm boot}(\Delta K_d^{*}\le 0)$: the fraction of our resamples that failed
to put Apollo~17 on top. It is a statement about the stability of our answer
under resampling, not about a null hypothesis. The letter and the thesis are
careful about this wording and so are we.
\end{warn}

\noindent\textbf{Worked, by hand.} Suppose 20 paired draws of a difference
come out as
\[
\{1.9,\, 2.4,\, 0.8,\, 3.1,\, {-0.2},\, 2.2,\, 1.5,\, 2.8,\, 0.9,\, 2.0,\,
1.7,\, 2.6,\, 3.4,\, 1.1,\, 2.3,\, 0.5,\, 2.9,\, 1.8,\, 2.1,\, 2.5\}.
\]
Exactly one ($-0.2$) is $\leq 0$, so the tail proportion is $1/20 = 0.05$:
one resample in twenty lost the ordering. The real count is 46 of 1500,
$0.031$ --- about one in thirty-three. Equivalently, $\sim$97\% of the paired
draws favor Apollo~17.

\section{What ``marginal'' means --- and why we say it}\label{sec:marginalzero}

Two numbers describe the same 1500 draws, and at first glance they look like
they disagree:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item the tail proportion, $P_{\rm boot} = 0.031$ --- only 3\% of resamples
  lost the ordering, which sounds decisive;
\item the \textbf{95\% CI}, $[-0.12, 3.56]$ --- it \emph{includes} zero, which
  sounds like we have nothing.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keypt}
There is no paradox here, and the reason is worth understanding once because
it settles the question for good. The tail proportion is \textbf{one-sided}:
it looks only at the lower end, and asks what fraction sits below zero. The
95\% interval is \textbf{two-sided}: it cuts $2.5\%$ off \emph{each} end and
reports what is left. For zero to fall outside a two-sided 95\% interval, the
lower tail would have to be smaller than $0.025$. Ours is $0.031$. Since
$0.031 > 0.025$, zero is inside the interval --- necessarily, and with no
tension whatsoever. The two numbers are two views of one distribution, and
they agree exactly.
\end{keypt}

\noindent So the honest summary is the sterner of the two readings: the
contrast is \emph{marginal}. Strongly suggestive --- 97\% of draws favor
Apollo~17, and every independent cross-check points the same way
(\S\ref{sec:crosschecks}) --- but not established at the conventional 95\%
standard.

\begin{warn}
The tempting shortcut --- ``$0.031<0.05$, therefore significant, full stop''
--- would be doubly wrong: it treats a tail proportion as a $p$-value, and it
compares a one-sided number against a two-sided threshold. A firmer per-site
$Q_b$ is what would actually settle the question (\S\ref{sec:register}); no
amount of re-analysis of the same seven-plus-sixteen sensors can.
\end{warn}

\section{A second road to the same answer: the MCMC
cross-check}\label{sec:mcmczero}

\begin{plain}
Everything so far \emph{fixed} $Q_b$ at its published value and asked only
about $K_d$. But $Q_b$ has its own uncertainty --- so a fair worry is: did
fixing it rig the answer? The Markov-chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) cross-check
lets \emph{both} numbers float and explores every $(K_d, Q_b)$ pair, keeping
each pair in proportion to how well it explains the sensors. Imagine 32
hikers dropped at random on a foggy landscape whose altitude is ``agreement
with the data'': each repeatedly proposes a step, takes it happily uphill and
only reluctantly downhill, and after thousands of steps the hikers' positions
map the high ground. That map is the \emph{posterior}.
\end{plain}

\begin{keypt}
\textbf{The two machines answer different questions.} This is the single
thing to hold on to, because otherwise the bootstrap and the MCMC look like
they contradict each other.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item The \textbf{bootstrap} holds $Q_b$ \emph{fixed} at the published value
  and asks: \emph{how much does the answer move when the sensors are
  resampled?} It measures sensitivity to \textbf{data noise}.
\item The \textbf{MCMC} lets $Q_b$ \emph{float} across its published
  uncertainty and asks: \emph{across every flux the literature allows, does
  Apollo~17 still come out on top?} It measures sensitivity to the
  \textbf{flux assumption}.
\end{itemize}
So when the bootstrap interval includes zero and the MCMC interval does not,
nothing is broken. They are different questions with different answers.
\end{keypt}

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:mcmcflow} traces one step of the sampler. What
follows unpacks each piece of it, in the order the code executes them.

\subsection*{Step 1 --- Precompute the landscape (117 real solves per site)}
The sampler will need the misfit at thousands of $(K_d,Q_b)$ pairs, and each
one costs a full equilibrium solve. So we do not solve inside the loop.
Instead we solve \emph{once}, in advance, on a fixed grid: 13 values of $K_d$
$\times$ 9 values of $Q_b$ = \textbf{117 real solves per site}, 234 in total.
Every node of that grid is a genuine flux-anchored solve, not a formula.
Between nodes the sampler reads the surface by \textbf{bilinear
interpolation}, and if a walker steps outside the solved rectangle it is
rejected outright --- we never extrapolate a misfit we did not compute.

\begin{warn}
Be honest about what this costs. The grid is coarse: at Apollo~15 the $Q_b$
spacing is $2.25$~mW\,m$^{-2}$, and the entire 68\% credible interval in
$Q_b$ spans only about \emph{one and a half grid cells}. The shape of the
$Q_b$ marginal is therefore substantially set by the interpolation, not by
the data. No convergence study of this surface exists in the repository, and
the guidebook will not pretend otherwise. The $K_d$ direction is better
resolved, at two to three cells.
\end{warn}

\noindent It is worth recording that this section used to teach a shortcut
instead: \emph{assume} the deep profile feels only the ratio $Q_b/K_d$, so
the 1-D sweep curve could be re-read at
$K_{\rm eff} = K_d\,(Q_b^{\rm pub}/Q_b)$ with no new solves. The 2026-07-03
audit put that assumption on trial and it lost (\S\ref{sec:degmeasured}): the
sensors also pin \emph{absolute} temperatures, which do not obey the ratio
rule. The 117-solve surface is what replaced it.

\subsection*{Step 2 --- State the prior}
The prior is where our existing knowledge enters. $Q_b$ gets a Gaussian
centred on the published Langseth value with a width of \textbf{15\%} of it:
$21\pm3.2$ at Apollo~15, $16\pm2.4$ at Apollo~17, in mW\,m$^{-2}$. $K_d$ gets
no informative prior at all --- flat in $\log K_d$, meaning no preferred
scale --- bounded only by the solved rectangle.

One technical detail that is easy to get wrong and was in fact got wrong
once. The sampler works in $\log Q_b$, but the prior is Gaussian in
\emph{linear} $Q_b$. Changing variables requires the Jacobian
$|\mathrm{d}Q_b/\mathrm{d}\log Q_b| = Q_b$, i.e.\ an extra $+\log Q_b$ in the
log-density. Omitting it under-weighted the high-flux end. The 2026-07-13
audit caught it; correcting it moved the headline ordering probability from
$99.2\%$ to $99.2\%$ and the medians by less than $0.2$~mW.

\subsection*{Step 3 --- State the likelihood}
Given a pair $(K_d,Q_b)$, read RMSE off the surface and score it as
\[
  \log L \;=\; -\tfrac12\,N\left(\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}}{\sigma}\right)^{2},
\]
with $N$ the sensor count (7 or 16). Because $N\cdot\mathrm{RMSE}^2$ is the
sum of squared residuals, this is the ordinary independent-Gaussian
likelihood --- but note that only the \emph{aggregate} misfit enters. The
individual residuals never appear.

\begin{warn}
$\sigma = 0.5$~K is an \textbf{assumed} per-sensor noise scale. It is not
calibrated against anything, and the code says so in its own docstring. It
was chosen near the scale of the model--data misfit rather than the
$0.03$--$0.05$~K thermometer precision. That choice has a consequence worth
saying out loud: it effectively pins the best-fit reduced $\chi^2$ near one,
which means \emph{this likelihood can never declare the model wrong}. It can
only rank $(K_d,Q_b)$ pairs against each other. Treat the MCMC credible
intervals as conditional on $\sigma$, and prefer the bootstrap for headline
uncertainty.
\end{warn}

\subsection*{Step 4 --- Run the walkers}
The sampler is \code{emcee}'s affine-invariant ensemble sampler.
\textbf{32 walkers}, \textbf{4000 steps} each, the first \textbf{1000
discarded} as warm-up, \textbf{no thinning} --- so $32\times3000 =
\mathbf{96{,}000}$ retained samples per site. A walker moves by the
``stretch'' rule: pick another walker at random and step along the line
joining them, by a random factor. There is no step size to tune, which is why
this sampler is popular. The measured acceptance rate is about $0.69$.

The two sites are run as \textbf{two separate 2-D chains}, not one joint 4-D
chain, and the contrast is formed afterwards by pairing the two independent
sample sets. That is legitimate precisely because the two boreholes' data are
independent.

\subsection*{Step 5 --- Check that it converged}
The one diagnostic computed is the integrated autocorrelation time $\tau$ ---
roughly, how many steps the chain needs to forget where it was. Measured:
$\tau \approx 32$--$36$ steps. The 1000-step burn-in is therefore about
$30\tau$, and the 3000 retained steps about $90\tau$, giving an effective
sample size of \textbf{2911} (A15) and \textbf{2694} (A17). Comfortable.

\begin{warn}
No Gelman--Rubin $\hat{R}$, no trace plots, no posterior-predictive check
exist in this pipeline. For a smooth 2-D single-peaked posterior with
$\mathrm{ESS}\approx2700$ that is defensible, but the absence should be
stated rather than glossed over --- so it is stated here.
\end{warn}

\section{The $K_d$--$Q_b$ degeneracy, measured}\label{sec:degmeasured}

\begin{plain}
Double the heater under a blanket and double the blanket's conductivity, and
the \emph{temperature drop} across the blanket is unchanged --- so you might
think a borehole, which measures that drop, can never tell $Q_b$ and $K_d$
apart, only their ratio. We believed exactly this (it was even in the
letter). Then we tested it, and it failed --- because the sensors do not
only measure the \emph{drop}; they sit at an \emph{absolute} temperature,
set by the whole column above them. That absolute level shifts when $Q_b$
changes, and it does not obey the ratio rule.
\end{plain}

Why not? The offset between the surface and the sensors is
$\Delta T \approx Q_b \int_0^{z}\!\mathrm{d}z'/K(z')$, and the integral is
dominated by the first few centimeters where $K$ is a thousand times smaller
than at depth. Rescale $(K_d, Q_b)\to(\alpha K_d, \alpha Q_b)$ and the
\emph{deep} part of the integrand scales as $1/\alpha$, canceling the
$\alpha$ in $Q_b$ --- but the skin part is set by $K_s$, which does not
scale. The measured verdict: applying $\alpha = 10/16$ at Apollo~17 shifts
the whole sensor band by a depth-uniform $-1.3$~K --- fifty times the
retrieval's certification scale. The invariance is dead; the honest map is
one direct re-retrieval per assumed $Q_b$
(\code{code/pipeline/compute/compute\_qb\_degeneracy.py}, one second per solve).

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_qb_degeneracy.pdf}
\caption{The degeneracy, measured (\code{code/results/qb\_degeneracy.json}).
\emph{(a)} Retrieved $K_d^{*}$ as a function of the \emph{assumed} basal
flux: the two sites respond in opposite directions, and neither follows the
disproved proportional rule (dotted). \emph{(b)} The best-fit RMSE at each
assumed flux: both sites fit \emph{better} at fluxes below the published
values, so fit quality alone cannot arbitrate the basal-flux debate.}
\label{fig:qbdegeneracy}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:qbdegeneracy}a is the result, and it teaches three things.
First, \textbf{Apollo 15 and Apollo 17 respond in opposite directions.} At
A15 the deep gradient dominates the fit, so a larger assumed $Q_b$ demands a
larger $K_d$ (2.07 up to 5.44 mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ across the debated
range) --- the naive direction, though not the naive magnitude. At A17 the
absolute level dominates, so a larger $Q_b$ demands a \emph{smaller} $K_d$
(10.13 down to 6.80). This asymmetry is not a curiosity; it is the same
mechanism that made A17 --- and only A17 --- hypersensitive to the solver's
old $O(\Delta t)$ bias (App.~\ref{app:dtcert}), which also entered as a
depth-uniform offset.

Second, \textbf{the opposite responses protect the contrast.} If a future
reanalysis revises both fluxes downward, A17's retrieval climbs while A15's
falls --- the contrast \emph{grows}. Even the most adverse independent
assignment in the published envelope (both fluxes at their upper bounds)
only compresses $K_d^{\rm A17}-K_d^{\rm A15}$ to about $+1.5$
mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$; there is no admissible corner where it vanishes.
This is why the MCMC's ordering probability barely moves as the $Q_b$ prior
widens (\S\ref{sec:mcmczero}): flux uncertainty smears the two marginals
\emph{apart}, not together.

Third, \textbf{fit quality cannot pick the flux}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:qbdegeneracy}b). At A17 the record actually fits better
under the Saito et al.\ (2007) reanalysis flux than under the published value
(RMSE 0.29 vs 0.40 K). The retrieval therefore \emph{reports its
conditionality} rather than hiding it: the absolute $K_d^{*}$ values are
``given the published $Q_b$'', and only the ordering is flux-robust.

\begin{warn}
The old proportional shortcut was not lazy --- it was a physically motivated
approximation that happened to encode untested physics. The lesson
generalises: \emph{a shortcut that embeds an assumption about the solution
is a hypothesis, and hypotheses get tested}. One afternoon of direct solves
replaced the assumption with a measurement and flipped two of the letter's
sentences.
\end{warn}

\section{Adding errors that don't know about each other:
quadrature}\label{sec:quadzero}

\begin{plain}
The bootstrap spread is only \emph{one} source of uncertainty (the luck of the
sensors). $Q_b$ could be off; the albedo could be off; $K_s$ could be off.
These errors are \emph{independent} --- the albedo doesn't care what $Q_b$ is
doing --- and independent errors add like the legs of a random walk: they
partially cancel, so the total is not the sum of the pieces but the square
root of the sum of their \emph{squares}. That rule is called adding in
quadrature.
\end{plain}

\[
\sigma_{\rm tot} \;=\; \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + \cdots + \sigma_n^2}.
\]

\noindent\textbf{Worked, by hand (the real Apollo 15 budget,
\S\ref{sec:resultstats}).} The rows are $\sigma_{\rm stat}=0.53$,
$\sigma_{Q_b}=1.61$ (the direct degeneracy map of \S\ref{sec:degmeasured},
not the disproved proportional rule), $\sigma_{A}=0.72$,
$\sigma_{K_s}=0.36$, and the pooled
small terms $0.13$ (all mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$). Squares: $0.2809$, $2.5921$,
$0.5184$, $0.1296$, $0.0169$. Sum: $3.538$. Root: $\boxed{1.88}$ --- the
published total. Two lessons fall out of the arithmetic: the total ($1.88$) is
much less than the plain sum ($3.35$) because independent errors partially
cancel; and the biggest row dominates once squared ($Q_b$ alone contributes
$2.5921/3.538 \approx 73\%$ of the variance), which is why the error budget's
conclusion is always ``improve $Q_b$ first.''

\section{From Fourier's law to the heat equation}\label{app:heateq}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/controlvolume}
\caption{The control volume behind the heat equation: what flows in minus
what flows out is what gets stored. Every term of the PDE is one piece of
this picture.}
\label{fig:controlvolume}
\end{figure}

Picture a thin horizontal slab of regolith between depth $z$ and $z+dz$, of unit
area (Fig.~\ref{fig:controlvolume}). Conservation of energy says the rate at which it stores heat equals the
flux in at its top face minus the flux out at its bottom face. Fourier's law
gives the conductive flux through any face,
\[
q(z) \;=\; -\,K\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}
\qquad(\text{W\,m}^{-2}),
\]
the minus sign because heat flows down-gradient.

\begin{plain}
Fourier's law is the thermal version of ``current follows voltage downhill.''
The temperature slope $\partial T/\partial z$ says which way the thermal hill
tilts and how steep it is; the conductivity $K$ says how easily heat can move
through the material. Their product is a heat flow per square meter. The minus
sign is not decoration: if temperature increases downward, the slope is
positive, so the heat flux points upward, from the warmer deeper material toward
the cooler surface.
\end{plain}

The slab stores $\rho c_p T$ per
unit volume, so its storage rate per unit area is $\rho c_p\,(\partial T/\partial
t)\,dz$. Balancing,
\[
\rho c_p\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}\,dz
\;=\; q(z) - q(z+dz)
\;=\; -\,\frac{\partial q}{\partial z}\,dz .
\]
Divide by $dz$ and insert Fourier's law:
\[
\boxed{\;\rho c_p\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
\;=\; -\frac{\partial q}{\partial z}
\;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\left(K\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right).\;}
\]
The conductivity stays \emph{inside} the derivative because $K=K(T,z)$ depends on
both depth (compaction) and temperature (radiation); pulling it out, as the
constant-$K$ textbook form $\kappa\,\partial_{zz}T$ does, would be wrong here.
This is Eq.~\eqref{eq:heat}.

\section{The Hayne conductivity, term by term}\label{app:hayne}
Hayne's $K(T,z)$ is a product of a depth-dependent contact part and a
temperature-dependent radiative enhancement; each comes from a separate physical
mechanism.

\emph{Contact conductivity.} Heat crosses from grain to grain through small
contact patches whose area grows as overburden compacts the column. Model the
contact conductivity as relaxing exponentially from its surface value $K_s$ to a
deep asymptote $K_d$ with $e$-folding depth $H$:
\[
K_c(z) \;=\; K_d - (K_d - K_s)\,e^{-z/H}.
\]
Check the limits: at $z=0$, $K_c=K_s$; as $z\to\infty$, $K_c\to K_d$. The
exponential-compaction \emph{form} is physics; the three numbers $K_s$, $H$, and
(for us) $K_d$ are fitted --- $K_s$ and $H$ from Hayne~(2017), $K_d$ retrieved
here. This is Eq.~\eqref{eq:Kc}.

\emph{Radiative enhancement.} Across the gas-empty pore between grains, heat also
moves by thermal radiation, whose flux scales as $\sigma T^4$
(Stefan--Boltzmann). The \emph{conductivity} that represents this is the
temperature derivative of that flux,
\[
\frac{d}{dT}\big(\sigma T^4\big) = 4\sigma T^3 \;\propto\; T^3,
\]
so the radiative conductivity rises as $T^3$. Hayne folds it in as a
multiplicative enhancement of the contact term,
\[
\boxed{\;K(T,z) \;=\; K_c(z)\,\Big[\,1 + \chi\,(T/T_{\rm ref})^3\,\Big],\;}
\]
with $T_{\rm ref}=350$\,K a reference convention and $\chi$ the fitted strength
($\chi=2.7$, Hayne \emph{and} Vasavada; see \S\ref{sec:chi}). The $T^3$ scaling
is physics; $\chi$ and $T_{\rm ref}$ are Hayne's constants. This is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Khayne}, implemented in \code{code/src/lunar/properties.py}
(\code{conductivity\_hayne}).

\section{The thermal skin depth}\label{app:skin}
Why can we anchor the deep solve just below the surface? Because the diurnal wave
dies exponentially with depth. Linearise the heat equation with constant $K$, so
$\partial_t T = \kappa\,\partial_{zz}T$ with diffusivity $\kappa=K/(\rho c_p)$,
and drive the surface harmonically at angular frequency $\omega=2\pi/P$. Seek a
solution $T=\Tm + \mathrm{Re}\big[A(z)\,e^{i\omega t}\big]$. Substituting,
\[
i\omega\,A = \kappa\,A'' \;\Rightarrow\; A'' = \frac{i\omega}{\kappa}\,A
\;\Rightarrow\; A(z) = A_0\,e^{-qz},\quad
q=\sqrt{\tfrac{i\omega}{\kappa}}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega}{2\kappa}}\,(1+i).
\]
Writing $\delta \equiv \sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}$, the real part of $q$ is $1/\delta$,
so the amplitude decays as $e^{-z/\delta}$ (the imaginary part is just a
depth-lag):
\[
\boxed{\;\delta = \sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}\;\approx\;0.04\text{--}0.10\;\text{m}.\;}
\]
With $\kappa\sim10^{-8}$\,m$^2$\,s$^{-1}$ and $P=29.53$\,d, the daily swing falls
by $e$ every $\delta$, and by $e^{-3}\approx5\%$ within $\sim$3 skin depths. That
is why an anchor at $z_0=0.25$--$0.55$\,m sits comfortably below the diurnal wave
(\S\ref{sec:insight}). Figure~\ref{fig:skinwave} shows the real solver doing
exactly what the algebra predicts.

\section{The boundary conditions, derived}\label{app:bc}
\emph{Surface (radiative balance).} At $z=0$ the absorbed sunlight must equal the
emitted infrared plus the heat conducted into the column. Absorbed:
$(1-A)\,S(t)$, with $A$ the Bond albedo. Emitted: $\varepsilon\sigma T_s^4$.
Conducted into the top half-cell, whose center lies $\Delta z_{\rm surf}/2$ below
the surface: $K_{\rm surf}(T_s-T_{\rm sub})/(\Delta z_{\rm surf}/2)$. Hence
\[
\boxed{\;(1-A)\,S(t) \;=\; \varepsilon\sigma T_s^4
\;+\; K_{\rm surf}\,\frac{T_s-T_{\rm sub}}{\Delta z_{\rm surf}/2},\;}
\]
nonlinear in $T_s$ through the $T_s^4$ term --- which is why it needs Newton
(\S\ref{app:newton}). This is Eq.~\eqref{eq:surfbc}
(\code{code/src/lunar/solver.py}, \code{surface\_energy\_balance\_residual}).

\emph{Base (geothermal flux).} Deep enough that no surface signal reaches, the
only heat crossing the bottom face is the steady geothermal flux $Q_b$. Fourier's
law then fixes the \emph{gradient} rather than the temperature:
\[
\boxed{\;K\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\bigg|_{z_{\max}} \;=\; Q_b\;}
\qquad(Q_b\text{ from Langseth 1976}).
\]
This Neumann condition is Eq.~\eqref{eq:bottombc}; with $z$ increasing downward
and $T$ rising with depth, $\partial_z T>0$.

\emph{Why the deep profile keeps rising, and what a base layer would change.}
Because the bottom condition fixes a \emph{flux} and not a temperature, the
cycle-mean profile does not approach a single deep value (Fig.~\ref{fig:baselayer}).
In steady state
$d\langle T\rangle/dz = Q_b/K$ (\S\ref{sec:closure}), so a constant geothermal
flux through a roughly constant deep conductivity makes the temperature rise
almost linearly with depth --- the geothermal gradient, $\sim$2--3\,K\,m$^{-1}$
here. That continued rise is \emph{physical} (the Moon loses internal heat
through the column), not a numerical drift, and it is why a profile plotted to a
few meters still ``drifts'' rather than leveling off. The profile would flatten
toward a near-constant only beneath a high-conductivity \emph{bedrock base},
where $K$ is $\sim$200$\times$ larger so the gradient $Q_b/K$ nearly vanishes
(or trivially if $Q_b=0$, i.e.\ no internal heat). But the Apollo deep sensors
sit at $0.8$--$2.4$\,m, entirely within the regolith and far above any such
base, so adding a base layer changes the profile only \emph{below the data} and
leaves the retrieved $K_d$ unchanged. This is also why the model imposes a
fixed-flux bottom rather than a fixed-temperature one: $Q_b$ is the measured
quantity, and no sensor reaches bedrock.

\section{The geometric grid and finite-volume cells}\label{app:grid}
\emph{Why geometric.} The surface skin needs millimeter cells to resolve a
$\sim$280\,K swing; by $1$\,m the profile is smooth and centimeter cells suffice.
A uniform millimeter grid to $5$\,m would need $2500$ cells; a geometric one
needs $69$. The cell edges follow the recurrence
\[
z_0^{\rm face}=0,\quad z_{j+1}^{\rm face}=z_j^{\rm face}+\Delta z_j,\quad
\Delta z_{j+1}=(1+g)\,\Delta z_j,
\]
with $\Delta z_0=2$\,mm and growth $g=0.08$ until $z_{\max}=5$\,m; nodes sit at
cell centres $z_i=\tfrac12(z_i^{\rm face}+z_{i+1}^{\rm face})$
(\code{code/src/lunar/grid.py}, \code{make\_geometric\_grid}; parameters in
\code{config.py}, \code{GRID}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/gridcells}
\caption{The 69-cell geometric grid as finite-volume cells (first six cells
sketched, widths to scale in spirit): temperatures live at cell centres,
fluxes cross the faces between them, and the harmonic mean combines the two
neighboring conductivities in series at each face.}
\label{fig:gridcells}
\end{figure}


\begin{plain}
The grid is small because the problem is uneven. The surface can swing by
$\sim$280\,K over a lunation, so the top millimeters need fine cells; by
$1$\,m the swing is $<1$\,K and the profile changes slowly. A uniform 2\,mm grid
would spend thousands of cells resolving smooth deep material. The geometric
grid spends resolution where curvature and flux changes are large, then lets
cells grow where the solution has become gentle.
\end{plain}

{\small\color{dim}\textit{Code: \code{code/src/lunar/grid.py} $\to$ \code{make\_geometric\_grid}}}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
faces = [0.0]; dz = dz0
while faces[-1] < z_max:
    faces.append(faces[-1] + dz)
    dz *= 1.0 + growth          # each cell (1+g)x thicker than the last
\end{lstlisting}

\emph{Why finite volume.} Integrate the heat equation over one cell
$[z_{i-1/2},z_{i+1/2}]$:
\[
C_i\,\frac{dT_i}{dt} \;=\; q_{i+1/2}-q_{i-1/2},\qquad
q_{i+1/2}=K_{i+1/2}\,\frac{T_{i+1}-T_i}{\Delta z_{i+1/2}},
\]
with $C_i=\rho c_p\,\Delta z_i$. The flux \emph{leaving} cell $i$ across the face
$i{+}1/2$ is exactly the flux \emph{entering} cell $i{+}1$; summing over cells the
interior fluxes telescope and cancel, so total energy changes only through the
two boundaries --- energy is conserved to machine precision, which a plain
finite-difference stencil does not guarantee.

\emph{Why the harmonic mean at faces.} Heat crossing the face between cells $i$
and $i{+}1$ passes through two half-cells in \emph{series}: thickness
$\Delta z/2$ at conductivity $K_i$, then $\Delta z/2$ at $K_{i+1}$. Thermal
resistances add in series, $R=\Delta z/K_{\rm face}$, so
\[
\frac{\Delta z}{K_{i+1/2}} = \frac{\Delta z/2}{K_i}+\frac{\Delta z/2}{K_{i+1}}
\;\Rightarrow\;
\boxed{\;K_{i+1/2}=\frac{2\,K_i K_{i+1}}{K_i+K_{i+1}}\;}
\]
--- the harmonic mean (\code{code/src/lunar/solver.py},
\code{\_face\_harmonic\_mean}). The arithmetic mean would let a high-$K$ cell
``short-circuit'' a neighboring low-$K$ cell and overstate the flux; the series
result is the physically correct one, and it matters where $K$ jumps --- the
steep $K_c$ rise through the top few centimeters.

\begin{plain}
The word ``series'' means the heat must cross one material and then the next,
like two resistors end to end. The slower layer limits the same flux through the
whole path, so the effective conductivity is pulled toward the smaller $K$.
That is why the harmonic mean is the right face value; a parallel-rule or
arithmetic average would imply heat can bypass the bottleneck, which it cannot
do in a one-dimensional column.
\end{plain}

{\small\color{dim}\textit{Code: \code{code/src/lunar/solver.py} $\to$ \code{\_face\_harmonic\_mean}}}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
K_face[i] = 2.0 * K[i-1] * K[i] / (K[i-1] + K[i])   # two half-cells in series
\end{lstlisting}

\section{The Crank--Nicolson scheme}\label{app:cn}
Write the spatial operator as $L[T]_i = (q_{i+1/2}-q_{i-1/2})/C_i$, so the
semi-discrete equation is $dT_i/dt=L[T]_i$. The \emph{$\theta$-method} advances
one step with a weighted blend of $L$ at the old and new times,
\[
\frac{T_i^{n+1}-T_i^{n}}{\Delta t}
= (1-\theta)\,L[T^{n}]_i + \theta\,L[T^{n+1}]_i,
\]
$\theta=0$ being explicit Euler, $\theta=1$ implicit Euler, and
$\theta=\tfrac12$ their average --- Crank--Nicolson.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/thetastencil}
\caption{Which time levels each $\theta$-scheme touches. Explicit uses
only the known level (cheap, unstable for large $\alpha$); implicit only
the new one (stable, first-order); Crank--Nicolson averages both (stable,
second-order) --- the reason it is the production scheme.}
\label{fig:thetastencil}
\end{figure}

\emph{Second-order accuracy.} Expand about $t^{n+1/2}$. The left side is a centered
difference, $(T^{n+1}-T^n)/\Delta t=\partial_t T|^{n+1/2}+O(\Delta t^2)$. At
$\theta=\tfrac12$ the right side is the midpoint average
$\tfrac12(L^n+L^{n+1})=L^{n+1/2}+O(\Delta t^2)$. Both sides match
$\partial_t T=L$ at $t^{n+1/2}$ to $O(\Delta t^2)$, so CN is second-order in
$\Delta t$. For any $\theta\neq\tfrac12$ a first-order term
$(\theta-\tfrac12)\,\Delta t\,\partial_t L$ survives --- only the average cancels it.

\emph{Unconditional stability (von Neumann).} Linearise ($K$ const, uniform
$\Delta z$), so $L[T]_i=(\kappa/\Delta z^2)(T_{i-1}-2T_i+T_{i+1})$, and insert a
Fourier mode $T_i^n=\xi^n e^{\,ik\,i\Delta z}$. The discrete Laplacian gives
$e^{-ik\Delta z}-2+e^{ik\Delta z}=-4\sin^2(k\Delta z/2)$, so with $r=\kappa\Delta
t/\Delta z^2$ and $\beta=4r\sin^2(k\Delta z/2)\ge0$ the $\theta$-scheme yields
\[
\xi-1=-(1-\theta)\beta-\theta\beta\,\xi
\;\Rightarrow\;
\xi=\frac{1-(1-\theta)\beta}{1+\theta\beta}.
\]
At $\theta=\tfrac12$, $\xi=(1-\beta/2)/(1+\beta/2)$, and since $\beta\ge0$ we have
$|\xi|\le1$ for \emph{every} mode $k$ and \emph{every} $\Delta t$ --- unconditional
stability.

\begin{plain}
Physically, each Fourier mode is a wrinkle in the temperature profile. A stable
heat solver should damp wrinkles rather than make them grow. Explicit Euler can
overreact: if the time step is too large, it subtracts too much heat and the
wrinkle flips sign with a larger amplitude. Crank--Nicolson averages the old and
new diffusion tendencies, giving the factor $|(1-r)/(1+r)|\le1$ for each
diffusive mode, so the wrinkle can damp or flip sign while shrinking, but it
cannot snowball.
\end{plain}

Explicit Euler ($\theta=0$) gives $\xi=1-\beta$, which needs
$\beta\le2$, i.e.\ $\Delta t\le\Delta z^2/2\kappa$. At the $2$\,mm surface cell
with $\kappa\sim10^{-8}$\,m$^2$\,s$^{-1}$ that floor is
\[
\Delta t \le \frac{(0.002)^2}{2\times10^{-8}} \approx 200\;\text{s},
\]
versus CN's $\Delta t=1800$\,s used freely --- and $200$\,s is only the
\emph{stability} floor; accuracy would force it lower still.

\section{Assembling the tridiagonal system, boundary rows included}\label{app:assembly}
Apply CN to the finite-volume equation and collect every $T^{n+1}$ on the left.
An interior cell becomes one row of a tridiagonal system
$a_i T_{i-1}^{n+1}+b_i T_i^{n+1}+c_i T_{i+1}^{n+1}=d_i$ with
\[
a_i=-\tfrac{\Delta t}{2C_i}\,\frac{K_{i-1/2}}{\Delta z_{i-1/2}},\quad
c_i=-\tfrac{\Delta t}{2C_i}\,\frac{K_{i+1/2}}{\Delta z_{i+1/2}},\quad
b_i=1-a_i-c_i,
\]
and $d_i=T_i^n+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2C_i}\,(q_{i+1/2}^n-q_{i-1/2}^n)$ (the explicit
half). For the uniform toy these reduce to $(-\tfrac12\alpha,\,1+\alpha,\,
-\tfrac12\alpha)$ with $\alpha=\Delta t\,K/(C\,\Delta z)$, exactly the row of
\S\ref{sec:cnhour}. The two boundaries modify only the first and last rows.

{\small\color{dim}\textit{Code: \code{code/src/lunar/solver.py} $\to$ \code{\_step}}}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/trimatrix}
\caption{The assembled system at a glance: a banded (tridiagonal) matrix in
which every interior row is the same CN stencil, and only the first and last
rows differ --- the surface row through a Dirichlet ghost (the Newton-solved
$T_s$ moves to the right-hand side) and the basal row through a Neumann ghost
(the fixed flux $Q_b$ enters as a source). The edits are derived below.}
\label{fig:trimatrix}
\end{figure}

\emph{Surface --- a Dirichlet ghost.} The face above cell~0 conducts from the
\emph{known} surface temperature $T_s$ (Newton has already solved it) across
$\Delta z_0/2$. Because $T_s$ is known, it is not an unknown in the matrix: its
contribution moves to the right-hand side. Splitting the CN surface-face flux
into its explicit ($T_s^n$) and implicit ($T_s^{n+1}$) halves,
\[
d_0 \;\to\; d_0 \;-\;\tfrac12\alpha\,T_0^{n}
\;+\;\tfrac12\alpha\,\big(T_s^{n}+T_s^{n+1}\big),
\]
and the interior coefficients $b_0,c_0$ are unchanged. The surface is one RHS
edit.

\emph{Base --- a Neumann ghost cell.} Introduce an imaginary cell $n$ just below
$z_{\max}$. The basal condition $K\,\partial_z T=Q_b$, discretized across the last
face, fixes its temperature:
\[
\frac{T_n^{\rm ghost}-T_{n-1}}{\Delta z}=\frac{Q_b}{K}
\;\Rightarrow\;
T_n^{\rm ghost}=T_{n-1}+\frac{Q_b\,\Delta z}{K}.
\]
Substitute this into cell $(n{-}1)$'s row. Its coupling to the ghost,
$-\tfrac12\alpha\,T_n^{\rm ghost}=-\tfrac12\alpha\,T_{n-1}-\tfrac12\alpha\,
\tfrac{Q_b\Delta z}{K}$, splits in two: the $T_{n-1}$ piece folds into the
diagonal and the constant piece becomes a flux source on the RHS. Carrying both
time levels through CN,
\[
\boxed{\;b_{n-1}\to b_{n-1}-\tfrac12\alpha,\qquad
d_{n-1}\to d_{n-1}+\frac{\Delta t\,Q_b}{C}.\;}
\]
These are exactly the two basal-ghost edits in \code{code/src/lunar/solver.py}:
the imaginary cell never appears in the stored matrix.

\section{The Thomas algorithm}\label{app:thomas}
A tridiagonal system is Gaussian elimination with almost everything already zero.

\begin{plain}
Thomas is not a new kind of algebra; it is ordinary elimination with the zeros
used aggressively. In a dense matrix, eliminating one row can fill many columns,
which is why the work grows like $N^3/3$. Here each row touches only its two
neighbors, so the forward sweep subtracts one multiple of the previous row,
records the new diagonal/right-hand-side pair, and moves on. Back-substitution
then peels the answers back upward. For 69 cells this is only a few hundred
arithmetic operations, so solving the column is effectively free compared with
the physical stepping.
\end{plain}

\emph{Forward sweep} (eliminate each sub-diagonal entry using the row above),
\[
c_0'=\frac{c_0}{b_0},\quad d_0'=\frac{d_0}{b_0};\qquad
c_i'=\frac{c_i}{b_i-a_i c_{i-1}'},\quad
d_i'=\frac{d_i-a_i d_{i-1}'}{b_i-a_i c_{i-1}'}\;\;(i\ge1).
\]
After it, each row reads $T_i+c_i' T_{i+1}=d_i'$. \emph{Back-substitution} from
the bottom, where there is no $T_{i+1}$:
\[
T_{n-1}=d_{n-1}',\qquad T_i=d_i'-c_i'\,T_{i+1}\;\;(i=n{-}2,\dots,0).
\]
Each sweep touches every row a constant number of times, so the cost is
$\sim$$8N$ operations --- $O(N)$, not the $\sim N^3/3$ of dense elimination. For
$N=69$, a few hundred flops (\code{code/src/lunar/solver.py}, \code{\_thomas}).

{\small\color{dim}\textit{Code: \code{code/src/lunar/solver.py} $\to$ \code{\_thomas}}}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
for i in range(1, n):                    # forward sweep (eliminate sub-diagonal)
    m = b[i] - a[i]*cp[i-1]
    cp[i] = c[i]/m;  dp[i] = (d[i] - a[i]*dp[i-1]) / m
x[n-1] = dp[n-1]
for i in range(n-2, -1, -1):             # back-substitution from the bottom up
    x[i] = dp[i] - cp[i]*x[i+1]
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Newton's method at the surface}\label{app:newton}
Solving the surface balance (\S\ref{app:bc}) means finding the root of
\[
R(T_s)=(1-A)\,S(t)-\varepsilon\sigma T_s^4
-K_{\rm surf}\,\frac{T_s-T_{\rm sub}}{\Delta z_{\rm surf}/2}.
\]

\begin{plain}
The surface equation is nonlinear because radiated heat scales as $T_s^4$. Newton
handles that by replacing the curve with its tangent at the current guess:
$T_s\leftarrow T_s-R/R'$. The analytic derivative below gives the tangent
exactly, and the previous hour's surface temperature is already close to the new
answer because sunlight changes smoothly from hour to hour. Once the guess is
near the root, the error squares each iteration, so only a few corrections are
needed.
\end{plain}

Its derivative is analytic,
\[
R'(T_s)=-4\varepsilon\sigma T_s^3-\frac{K_{\rm surf}}{\Delta z_{\rm surf}/2}
=-4\varepsilon\sigma T_s^3-\frac{2K_{\rm surf}}{\Delta z_{\rm surf}}\;<0,
\]
strictly negative, so $R$ is monotone and has a unique root. Newton iterates
$T_s\leftarrow T_s-R(T_s)/R'(T_s)$; near the root the error squares each step
($e_{k+1}\approx\tfrac12|R''/R'|\,e_k^2$), so $2$--$3$ iterations reach
$10^{-4}$\,K. It runs \emph{every} time step because the insolation $S(t)$ has
moved (\code{code/src/lunar/solver.py}, \code{\_solve\_surface\_newton}).

{\small\color{dim}\textit{Code: \code{code/src/lunar/solver.py} $\to$ \code{\_solve\_surface\_newton}}}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
for _ in range(max_iter):
    R  = surface_energy_balance_residual(T_s, ...)        # absorbed - emitted - conducted
    dR = -4*emissivity*SIGMA_SB*T_s**3 - 2*K_surf/dz_surf  # analytic derivative (< 0)
    T_s_new = T_s - R/dR                                  # Newton step
    if abs(T_s_new - T_s) < 1e-4:                         # 2-3 iters reach 1e-4 K
        return T_s_new
    T_s = T_s_new
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Certifying the time step: the wrap-step bug and the dt
ladder}\label{app:dtcert}

\begin{plain}
Imagine a clock that runs 23 hours and 59 minutes and then declares
``midnight --- new day.'' Each day looks fine on its own, but every day
starts one minute earlier than it should, and after a while the clock's
noon no longer matches the Sun's. Our solver did exactly this to the lunar
day. The periodic time grid stores the samples $t_0{=}0$ through
$t_{n-1}{=}P{-}\Delta t$ (the sample at $P$ would duplicate the one at 0),
and the march stepped from $t_0$ to $t_{n-1}$ --- and then \emph{relabeled}
that state as the start of the next lunation. The final step, from
$P{-}\Delta t$ back around to $P\equiv 0$, was never taken
(Fig.~\ref{fig:wrapstep}).
\end{plain}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/wrapstep}
\caption{The wrap-step bug (\code{solver.solve\_pixel}, fixed 2026-07-03).
\emph{Top}: a march over the periodic grid that stops at $P{-}\Delta t$ and
relabels slips phase by $\Delta t$ every lunation. \emph{Bottom}: one more
step --- forced by the $t{=}0$ insolation --- closes the cycle exactly.}
\label{fig:wrapstep}
\end{figure}

Why does a one-step phase slip matter? Because it never averages away. The
converged periodic state --- the attractor the whole retrieval is built on
--- becomes the fixed point of ``evolve for $P-\Delta t$, then jump the
forcing by $\Delta t$'', which differs from the true attractor by an error
\emph{proportional to} $\Delta t$: halve the step and the error halves,
first-order convergence. Measured at the certified settings, the slip
shifted the sensor-band cycle-mean by a depth-uniform $+61$ mK per halving
--- and a depth-uniform offset is precisely the perturbation Apollo 17's
retrieval is most sensitive to (\S\ref{sec:degmeasured}), which is how a
sub-0.1-K numerical artifact became a $+0.6$ mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ bias on
$K_d^{*}$(A17) while leaving A15 nearly untouched.

\paragraph{Certify the quantity you publish.} How fine a $\Delta t$ is fine
enough? The honest test is not ``does the temperature look converged'' but
``has \emph{the number we publish} stopped moving.'' The \emph{dt ladder}
(\code{code/pipeline/compute/dt\_ladder.py}) re-runs a miniature $K_d$ retrieval
at $\Delta t$, $\Delta t/2$, $\Delta t/4$ and watches $K_d^{*}$ itself:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
$K_d^{*}$(A17) [mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$] & $\Delta t{=}1800$ s & 900 s & 450 s \\
\midrule
buggy march (missing wrap step) & 7.699 & 7.397 & 7.251 \\
fixed march (wrap $+$ midpoint Picard) & 7.0795 & 7.0975 & 7.1045 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The buggy row halves its change with every halving of $\Delta t$ (ratio
2.07) --- the fingerprint of a first-order error. For such a sequence the
limit can be extrapolated without ever reaching it: if the error is
$c\,\Delta t$, then $K^{*}(0) = 2K^{*}(\Delta t/2) - K^{*}(\Delta t)$
(\emph{Richardson extrapolation}), which gives $7.104$. The fixed row barely
moves ($\leq 0.018$ mW per halving, inside the 0.05 criterion) --- and at
$\Delta t = 450$ s it reads $7.1045$: the same number to four digits. Two
different discretizations, one continuum limit
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dtladder}). That agreement is the strongest internal
validation a numerical result can offer, and it is what certifies the
production setting ($\Delta t = 1800$ s) in the register of
\S\ref{sec:register}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/fig_dt_ladder.pdf}
\caption{The dt ladder at Apollo 17, in the published quantity. The buggy
march converges first-order toward its Richardson limit; the fixed march is
flat and sits on that limit. Data:
\code{code/results/dt\_kdstar\_certification\_*.json}.}
\label{fig:dtladder}
\end{figure}

Two implementation notes close the loop. The fix itself is one extra
Crank--Nicolson step per lunation, forced by the $t{=}0$ insolation, whose
result becomes the next cycle's starting state \emph{and} starting surface
temperature (\code{solver.solve\_pixel}; the C++ port mirrors it and agrees
to $10^{-12}$ K). And because the corrected march exposed a smaller
first-order residual from evaluating $K(T)$, $\rho$, $c_p$ at the old time
level, each step now re-solves twice with properties at the midpoint state
$\tfrac12(T^{n}{+}T^{n+1})$ (\code{config.CN\_PICARD\_SWEEPS}$\,=2$) ---
the implicit-midpoint prescription that makes the attractor genuinely
second-order.

\begin{warn}
The wrap sliver had already been ``fixed'' once: an earlier audit replaced a
non-commensurate \code{arange} grid (slip ${\sim}6243$ s) with a
commensurate one (slip $\Delta t$) and the ladder \emph{looked} converged
because both rungs shared the same bug. The lesson is the meta-rule of
\S\ref{sec:register}: a convergence claim is certified only in the quantity
you publish, on evidence that would catch the failure mode you have not
thought of yet --- here, two independent discretizations forced to agree on
the limit.
\end{warn}

\section{The steady-state closure and the rectified flux}\label{app:closure}
This completes the sketch of \S\ref{sec:closure}. Time-average the heat equation
over one lunation. In periodic steady state the storage term averages to zero
(every bit of day-warming is undone by night-cooling), so
$\partial_z\langle K\partial_z T\rangle=0$; integrating once and applying the
basal condition gives the flux identity $\langle K\partial_z T\rangle=Q_b$ at
every depth. Now split each factor into its mean and fluctuation,
$K=K(\Tm)+\delta K$ and $\partial_z T=\partial_z\Tm+\delta(\partial_z T)$. The
cross-terms carrying a single fluctuation average to zero, leaving
\[
\langle K\,\partial_z T\rangle
= K(\Tm)\,\partial_z\Tm \;+\; \underbrace{\langle \delta K\,\delta(\partial_z
T)\rangle}_{\textstyle u_{\rm rect}}.
\]
Setting this equal to $Q_b$ and solving for the mean gradient,
\[
\boxed{\;\frac{d\Tm}{dz}=\frac{Q_b-u_{\rm rect}(z)}{K(\Tm,z)},\qquad
u_{\rm rect}\equiv\langle K\,\partial_z T\rangle-K(\Tm)\,\partial_z\Tm.\;}
\]
The geothermal flux enters as $Q_b-u_{\rm rect}$ with this definition of
$u_{\rm rect}$ --- exactly the convention computed in
\code{code/src/lunar/equilibrium.py} (\code{\_rectified\_flux} returns
$\langle K\,\partial_z T\rangle-K(\Tm)\partial_z\Tm$) and integrated in
\code{:100} (\code{\_reconstruct\_subskin}, $q=Q_b-u_{\rm rect}$). This is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:closure}.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
total     = (K_func(T_cycle) * dTdz_t).mean(axis=1)   # <K dT/dz>, averaged over the cycle
meanfield = K_func(T_mean) * np.gradient(T_mean, z)    # K(<T>) d<T>/dz

\end{lstlisting}

\emph{Why it is small.} Taylor-expand $\delta K\approx K'(\Tm)\,\delta T
+\tfrac12 K''(\Tm)\,\delta T^2$. The leading correlation
$\langle\delta K\,\delta(\partial_z T)\rangle\approx
K'(\Tm)\,\langle\delta T\,\delta(\partial_z T)\rangle$ scales with the square of
the diurnal amplitude $\delta T$, so $u_{\rm rect}$ lives in the skin (${\sim}0.2\%$
of $Q_b$ at the production anchor) and falls to numerical noise below $\sim$1\,m ---
which is why the production anchor sits at $0.55$\,m (\S\ref{sec:twostage}).

\section{RK2 reconstruction and its local error}\label{app:rk2}
Step~B integrates $d\Tm/dz=f(\Tm,z)$ with $f=(Q_b-u_{\rm rect})/K(\Tm,z)$ by the
midpoint rule. From cell $i$ over a step $h=\Delta z$:
\[
s_i=f(\Tm_i,z_i),\quad
\Tm_{i+1/2}=\Tm_i+\tfrac{h}{2}s_i,\quad
s_{i+1/2}=f(\Tm_{i+1/2},z_{i+1/2}),\quad
\Tm_{i+1}=\Tm_i+h\,s_{i+1/2}.
\]
Taylor-expanding the true solution, $\Tm(z+h)=\Tm+h\Tm'+\tfrac12h^2\Tm''
+\tfrac16h^3\Tm'''+\dots$; evaluating the slope at the \emph{midpoint} reproduces
the $h$ and $h^2$ terms, so the local truncation error is $O(h^3)$ --- one order
better than forward Euler's $O(h^2)$. That is why the cell-by-cell walk of
\S\ref{sec:stepB} is accurate to $\ll1$\,mK per cell at the production resolution
(\code{code/src/lunar/equilibrium.py}).

\section{Parabolic vertex refinement}\label{app:vertex}
The RMSE bowl is locally quadratic, so fit a parabola through its three lowest
points $(K^{(k)}{-}\Delta,y_-)$, $(K^{(k)},y_0)$, $(K^{(k)}{+}\Delta,y_+)$. Writing
$y=A(K-K^{(k)})^2+B(K-K^{(k)})+y_0$, the three points give
$B=(y_+-y_-)/2\Delta$ and $A=(y_+-2y_0+y_-)/2\Delta^2$. The vertex sits at
$K-K^{(k)}=-B/2A$, i.e.
\[
\boxed{\;K_d^{*}=K^{(k)}+\frac{\Delta}{2}\,\frac{y_--y_+}{y_--2y_0+y_+},\;}
\]
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:vertex} --- sub-grid precision with no extra solver runs
(\code{code/pipeline/compute/retrieve\_kd.py}, \code{kd\_star\_from\_residuals}).

\section{Statistics: bootstrap and quadrature, justified}\label{app:stats}
\emph{The bootstrap plug-in principle.} The true distribution $F$ of the
sensor noise is unknown, but the $N$ observed sensors are themselves a sample from
$F$, so their empirical distribution $\widehat F$ is a consistent estimate of it.
Drawing $N$ sensors with replacement from $\widehat F$ therefore mimics
collecting a fresh dataset from $F$ without assuming any noise shape; re-fitting
$K_d^{*}$ on each resample maps the sensor scatter into the sampling distribution
of $K_d^{*}$, whose $2.5$--$97.5$ percentiles are a distribution-free CI
(\S\,Step~9).

\emph{Why systematics add in quadrature.} If $K_d^{*}$ depends on independent
inputs $x_j$ (basal flux, albedo, $K_s$, \ldots) each with a small error
$\delta x_j$, linearise: $\delta K_d^{*}=\sum_j(\partial K_d^{*}/\partial x_j)\,
\delta x_j$. The variance of a sum of \emph{independent} zero-mean terms is the
sum of their variances --- every cross-term $\langle\delta x_j\,\delta
x_k\rangle$ vanishes for $j\neq k$ --- so
\[
\boxed{\;\sigma_{\rm tot}^2=\sum_j\Big(\frac{\partial K_d^{*}}{\partial x_j}\Big)^2
\sigma_j^2=\sum_j\sigma_j^2,\;}
\]
the quadrature sum the error-budget table of \S\ref{sec:resultstats} reports
(\code{code/pipeline/compute/compute\_error\_budget.py}). Independence is the
assumption that earns the square-root; correlated inputs would need cross-terms.

\section{From the heat-equation PDE to the closure ODE}\label{app:pde2ode}

\begin{plain}
Step~B integrates an \emph{ordinary} differential equation --- one variable, depth ---
yet the underlying physics is a \emph{partial} differential equation in two variables,
depth \emph{and} time. Here is how one becomes the other, and why it is legal only in
steady state.
\end{plain}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures-tikz/pde2odeflow}
\caption{The five moves that collapse the two-variable heat PDE to the
one-variable closure ODE. Move~2 is the hinge: the time term averages to
zero \emph{only} in periodic steady state, which is why the deep column
must be driven \emph{to} that state before Step~B is legal.}
\label{fig:pde2odeflow}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Start --- the heat-equation PDE.} Energy conservation in the column is
\[
\rho(z)\,c_p(T)\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\left[K(T,z)\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right],
\]
with two independent variables $z$ and $t$: the temperature at every depth changes
every hour as the daily wave passes. Solving this forward in time \emph{is} Step~A.

\paragraph{Move 1 --- average over one lunation.} Define the cycle mean
$\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle\equiv\frac1P\int_0^P(\cdot)\,dt$ over one lunation $P$. In
periodic steady state the column repeats every cycle, $T(z,t{+}P)=T(z,t)$. Average both
sides of the PDE.

\paragraph{Move 2 --- the time term vanishes (the whole trick).} The left side is the
time derivative of stored heat: writing the volumetric enthalpy $h(T)=\int\rho c_p\,dT$
gives $\rho c_p\,\partial_t T=\partial_t h$, so
\[
\bigl\langle \rho c_p\,\partial_t T\bigr\rangle
=\frac1P\bigl[h(P)-h(0)\bigr]=0,
\]
because $h$ returns to its starting value after a full cycle. In steady state nothing
accumulates heat on average --- so the time derivative averages to zero and
\textbf{time drops out of the problem}.

\paragraph{Move 3 --- the depth derivative passes through the average.} On the right,
$z$ and $t$ are independent, so the average commutes with $\partial_z$. The averaged
equation is therefore
\[
0=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\bigl\langle K\,\partial_z T\bigr\rangle
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\bigl\langle K\,\partial_z T\bigr\rangle=\text{constant in }z.
\]
No $t$ remains: it is now an equation in depth \emph{alone} --- already an ODE in form.

\paragraph{Move 4 --- fix the constant with the bottom BC.} That constant is the
cycle-mean flux, pinned at the base to the geothermal value
$\langle K\partial_z T\rangle|_{z_{\max}}=Q_b$. Hence
\[
\boxed{\;\bigl\langle K\,\partial_z T\bigr\rangle=Q_b\ \text{ at every depth},\;}
\]
the column carries the same mean flux all the way up (\S\ref{sec:closure}).

\paragraph{Move 5 --- peel the average into an ODE for $\Tm$.} Because $K$ depends on
$T$ and $T$ oscillates, $\langle K\partial_z T\rangle\neq K(\Tm)\,\partial_z\Tm$; name
the gap the rectified flux,
$u_{\rm rect}\equiv\langle K\partial_z T\rangle-K(\Tm)\,\partial_z\Tm$. Substitute and
rearrange:
\[
K(\Tm,z)\,\frac{d\Tm}{dz}+u_{\rm rect}=Q_b
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\boxed{\;\frac{d\Tm}{dz}=\frac{Q_b-u_{\rm rect}}{K(\Tm,z)}\;}
\]
--- a \textbf{first-order ODE in depth}, exactly what Step~B integrates. The
two-variable PDE has collapsed to a one-variable ODE because the time average
annihilated $\partial_t$.

\begin{keypt}[title={Why it is an ODE only in steady state}]
The collapse hinges on Move~2: $\langle\partial_t h\rangle=0$ needs the cycle to close,
$h(P)=h(0)$. Before steady state the deep column is still warming, so
$\langle\partial_t h\rangle\neq0$, the time term survives, and you are stuck with the
full PDE. That is precisely why the \emph{skin} (already periodic) can be reconstructed
but the not-yet-settled \emph{deep column} cannot --- and why the anchor method needs
its outer loop to drive the column \emph{to} the steady state where this ODE becomes
legal in the first place.
\end{keypt}

\paragraph{Step 1 --- the governing equation.}
The closure we just derived (\S\ref{app:pde2ode}), with $u_{\rm rect}\approx0$ below
the skin, is the first-order ODE
\[
\frac{d\Tm}{dz}=\frac{Q_b-u_{\rm rect}}{K(\Tm,z)}\;\approx\;\frac{Q_b}{K(\Tm,z)} .
\]
We integrate it \emph{downward} from the anchor by the midpoint rule (RK2): for each
cell, take the slope at the start, use it to predict the midpoint temperature,
re-evaluate the slope there, and step with that midpoint slope.

\paragraph{Step 2 --- conductivity at the anchor.}
With $K(T,z)=K_c(z)\,[1+\chi(T/T_{\rm ref})^3]$ and $K_c(z)=K_d-(K_d-K_s)e^{-z/H}$:
\begin{align*}
e^{-z_0/H}&=e^{-0.55/0.06}=e^{-9.17}=1.05\times10^{-4},\\
K_c(0.55)&=7.08-(7.08-0.74)(1.05\times10^{-4})=7.079\ \text{mW\,m}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1},\\
1+\chi\,(T/T_{\rm ref})^3&=1+2.7\,(255.11/350)^3=1+2.7\,(0.729)^3=2.046,\\
K(255.11,\,0.55)&=7.079\times2.046=14.48\ \text{mW\,m}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}.
\end{align*}
The contact part $K_c$ has already reached $K_d$ at this depth; the temperature
factor roughly doubles it.

\paragraph{First --- the forward solve behind every $\Tm(z_i;K_d)$.} The model
temperatures are not handed to us; each is the output of a full forward solve at that
trial $K_d$, run with the two boundary conditions and Crank--Nicolson time-stepping (the
fully worked hour is \S\ref{sec:cnhour}). Four facts make it concrete.

\emph{(1) The conductivity is not one number.} A trial $K_d$ does \emph{not} mean
``$K=K_d$ at every depth.'' It is the \emph{deep asymptote} of the contact conductivity
\[
K_c(z)=K_d-(K_d-K_s)\,e^{-z/H},
\]
which climbs from the surface value $K_s=0.74$ at $z=0$ up to $K_d$ at depth, over the
compaction scale height $H=0.06$\,m --- so $K_c\approx K_d$ only below $z\sim0.3$\,m. The
full $K(T,z)=K_c(z)\,[1+\chi(T/T_{\rm ref})^3]$ then adds the radiative temperature term.
$K$ therefore varies with \emph{both} depth and temperature; $K_d$ is the single knob ---
the deep value the contact part reaches --- that the sensor data constrain.

\emph{(2) Crank--Nicolson makes the interior tridiagonal.} Discretising the heat equation
with the half-implicit CN scheme gives, for the uniform toy (\S\ref{sec:cnhour}),
\[
-\tfrac12\alpha\,T_{i-1}^{n+1}+(1+\alpha)\,T_i^{n+1}-\tfrac12\alpha\,T_{i+1}^{n+1}
=\tfrac12\alpha\,T_{i-1}^{n}+(1-\alpha)\,T_i^{n}+\tfrac12\alpha\,T_{i+1}^{n},
\qquad\alpha=\frac{\Delta t\,K}{\Delta z\,C}.
\]

\emph{(3) The two boundary conditions close the top and bottom rows} --- they are the only
edits to the otherwise-interior system:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Surface --- Newton energy balance.} $(1-A)\,S(t)=\varepsilon\sigma T_s^4
  +K\,\partial_z T|_{z=0}$ is solved each step for the skin temperature $T_s$, which then
  feeds the first row's right-hand side.
\item \textbf{Base --- geothermal flux.} $K\,\partial_z T|_{z_{\max}}=Q_b$ enters the last
  row: its diagonal loses $\tfrac12\alpha$ and the flux $\Delta t\,Q_b/C$ is added to its
  right-hand side.
\end{itemize}

\emph{(4) Solve, then repeat.} The Thomas sweep solves this tridiagonal system in one pass
--- one step. Repeating ${\sim}1417$ half-hour steps per lunation for 96 lunations settles the
skin (Step~A); reconstructing below the anchor (Step~B, the walk above) fills the deep
column. The output is $\Tm(z;K_d)$ --- exactly the profile we now read at the sensor depths.

\paragraph{The misfit.} For a trial $K_d$ the deep-sensor RMSE is
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}(K_d)=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl[\Tm(z_i;K_d)-T^{\rm obs}_i\bigr]^2},
\]
the root-mean-square gap between the modeled cycle mean $\Tm(z_i;K_d)$ and the measured
$T^{\rm obs}_i$ over the $N=16$ Apollo~17 sensors below $80$\,cm.

Square, sum, average over $N=16$, take the root:
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}(7.40)=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{16}(2.574)}=\sqrt{0.161}=\mathbf{0.401}\ \text{K}.
\]
That one number is how well $K_d=7.40$ fits the Apollo~17 data.

\paragraph{Step 2 --- sweep: repeat across the grid.}
Re-run Step~1 at all $32$ grid points ($K_d=3$ to $25$\,mW, refined to $0.2$\,mW spacing
near the minimum). RMSE$(K_d)$ traces a smooth
bowl; the three points straddling its bottom are
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
trial $K_d$ (mW\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$) & $6.80$ & $\mathbf{7.00}$ & $7.20$ \\
RMSE (K) & $0.4002$ & $\mathbf{0.3982}$ & $0.3984$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
(grid spacing $d=0.200$\,mW near the minimum). We now find the bottom of that bowl.

\paragraph{Step 3 --- locate the discrete minimum.} Of the three, $K_d=7.00$ gives the
lowest RMSE, so the true minimum is near it. Label the three
$y_-=0.4002$, $y_0=0.3982$, $y_+=0.3984$ at $K_d^{(0)}=7.00$\,mW.

\paragraph{Step 4 --- a parabola through the three points, take its vertex.} RMSE$(K_d)$
is a smooth bowl, so near the bottom it is a parabola; the vertex of the parabola through
the three points is (App.~\ref{app:vertex})
\[
K_d^{*}=K_d^{(0)}+\frac{d}{2}\,\frac{y_--y_+}{y_--2y_0+y_+}.
\]

\paragraph{Step 5 --- put the numbers in.}
\begin{align*}
y_--y_+&=0.4002-0.3984=+0.0018,\\
y_--2y_0+y_+&=0.4002-2(0.3982)+0.3984=0.0022,\\
K_d^{*}&=7.00+\frac{0.200}{2}\cdot\frac{+0.0018}{0.0022}
        =7.00+0.082=7.08\ \text{mW\,m}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}.
\end{align*}

\begin{keypt}[title={Answer}]
\[
\boxed{\;K_d^{*}(\mathrm{A17})=7.08\ \text{mW\,m}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}\;}
\]
the conductivity whose modeled profile best matches the Apollo~17 deep sensors ---
conditional on $Q_b=16$\,mW\,m$^{-2}$ and $\chi=2.7$. The real retrieval does this over
all $32$ grid points, then jitters the sensor depths $1500\times$ to get the $95\%$
interval $[6.16,\,8.07]$. \emph{This is the number Step~B used as its given} ---
the loop has closed.
\end{keypt}
