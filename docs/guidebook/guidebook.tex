\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We measure how well heat conducts through the deep lunar regolith --- the
parameter $K_d$ --- separately at the Apollo~15 and Apollo~17 heat-flow
boreholes, and find them to differ by about a factor of two
($K_{d,\mathrm{A15}}^{*}=4.58$, $K_{d,\mathrm{A17}}^{*}=8.12$
mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$). This document explains \emph{everything} behind that
sentence, assuming no prior knowledge: what heat conduction is and how we model
it; the two published conductivity models and how to reproduce them; the
numerical solver line by line; the subtle bug we found in reaching steady state
and the faster, self-certifying method that replaced it; how $K_d$ is actually
retrieved from the data; how the uncertainty is quantified; why the basal heat
flux $Q_b$ matters; and why the two sites plausibly differ. Figures accompany
every step. Sections are cross-referenced to the actual Python source files so
you can read this document alongside the code.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{What we are trying to do, and why}\label{ch:intro}
% =====================================================================
\section{The question}\label{sec:question}
Every airless body loses its internal heat through a blanket of broken rock ---
\emph{regolith}. How fast that heat escapes, and how steeply temperature rises
with depth, is controlled by the regolith's \textbf{thermal conductivity}: a
number, $K$, that says how many watts of heat flow through a square metre for
each degree of temperature change per metre of depth (units
W\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$). Deep down, that number settles to a value we call
$K_d$ (``$d$'' for deep).

The Apollo~15 and~17 missions buried temperature probes in the Moon (the
Heat-Flow Experiment, HFE) and recorded subsurface temperatures from 1971 to
1977. The standard practice has been to assume \emph{one} global $K_d$ for the
whole Moon. \textbf{Our question is simply: what is $K_d$ at each of the two
boreholes, separately --- and do they differ?} (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}.)

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/fig_context_map.pdf}
\caption{The two heat-flow boreholes. Apollo~15 sits at Hadley Rille on the
Imbrium mare/Apennine boundary; Apollo~17 in the Taurus--Littrow highland valley.
Different terrains suggest possibly different regolith properties.}
\label{fig:map}
\end{figure}

\section{Why it matters}
$K_d$ sets the deep temperature profile, which feeds models of subsurface ice
stability (relevant for future water-mining missions), habitat thermal design,
drilling engineering, and the Moon's heat budget. A wrong, one-size-fits-all $K_d$
propagates errors into all of them. Getting it right \emph{per site} is the
contribution of this work.

\section{The data we use}
The Apollo HFE probes recorded temperatures at multiple depths, from about 0.5\,m
down to 1.39\,m (Apollo~15) and 2.34\,m (Apollo~17). We use only the \emph{deep
sensors} --- those below 80\,cm --- because they sit below the daily temperature
swing and respond only to the slow, steady geothermal signal. Their temperatures
are stable year to year, which tells us the regolith at those depths has reached
its \emph{periodic steady state}: the thermal profile repeats identically from one
lunation (lunar day, $\sim$29.5 Earth days) to the next.

At Apollo~15 we have 7 deep sensors; at Apollo~17, 16. These are the data points
our model must match.

\section{From the raw record to a data point}\label{sec:provenance}
Each Apollo site carried \emph{two} probes, each with several sensors of two
kinds (gradient bridges, TG; and ring/reference thermocouples, TR). The probes
recorded for years, but the early record is contaminated by the heat of drilling
and emplacement; only after the borestem and surrounding regolith
re-equilibrate does a sensor report the true undisturbed temperature.

To turn each sensor's multi-year series into a single equilibrium temperature
$T_{\rm eq}$, the code (\code{src/lunar/apollo\_helpers.py},
\code{find\_stable\_window}) scans the trailing 55--85\% of the record and picks
the earliest window whose linear trend is flatter than $0.08$\,K\,yr$^{-1}$
($\sim$2$\times10^{-4}$\,K\,day$^{-1}$ --- the criterion stated in the letter,
about $4.6\times$ stricter than the often-cited $10^{-3}$\,K\,day$^{-1}$). The
mean of that flat tail is $T_{\rm eq}$; its scatter sets the per-sensor noise.
Sensors deeper than 80\,cm are flagged ``deep'' and become the retrieval target
(7 at Apollo~15, 16 at Apollo~17). Figure~\ref{fig:rawrecord} shows what one
sensor's record actually looks like.

\begin{figure}[p]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_apollo_timeline.pdf}
\caption{Per-probe stability-window timeline for all four Apollo HFE probes

\begin{plain}
In short: we don't trust the first few years (the probe is still cooling off from
being drilled in). We wait until each sensor's temperature stops drifting, average
that quiet stretch, and keep only the sensors deep enough to sit below the daily
wave. Those quiet, deep averages are the dots our model must hit.
\end{plain}

The restored digital record itself comes from Nagihara et al.\ (2018), who
recovered and re-digitised the 1971--1977 HFE tapes. Their digitisation carries a
small depth uncertainty ($\sim$2.5\,cm), which the bootstrap
(\S\ref{sec:bootstrap}) propagates by jittering each sensor's depth.

\section{The shape of the whole argument}
In one breath: \emph{(i)} heat conduction is described by one equation;
\emph{(ii)} two published formulas give $K$ as a function of temperature and
depth; \emph{(iii)} we put one of them in a computer model of the regolith and
run it until it stops changing (``steady state''); \emph{(iv)} we ask which $K_d$
makes the model's deep temperatures match the Apollo sensors; \emph{(v)} the hard
part was reaching steady state correctly --- the bulk of this document --- and
\emph{(vi)} we then quantify the uncertainty and interpret the result. Each clause
is a chapter.

\begin{stepbox}{Step A — from sensor record to $T_{\rm eq}$ (data preparation)}
\textbf{Inputs:} multi-year HFE temperature record at every probe sensor (Nagihara
2018; \code{src/lunar/apollo\_helpers.py}). \\
\textbf{What happens:} for each sensor, scan the trailing 55--85\% of the record
and pick the earliest window whose linear trend is flatter than
$0.08$~K\,yr$^{-1}$; the mean of that quiet tail is the sensor's equilibrium
temperature~$T_{\rm eq}$. \\
\textbf{Output:} a small list of $(z, T_{\rm eq})$ deep-sensor data points --- 7
at Apollo~15 and 16 at Apollo~17 (only $z>80$~cm survives the borestem cut). \\
\textbf{Details:} \S\ref{sec:provenance}; see also Fig.~\ref{fig:borehole}.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}{Step B — from a trial $K_d$ to a profile $T(z;K_d)$ (forward solve)}
\textbf{Inputs:} a single trial value of $K_d$. \\
\textbf{What happens:} plug $K_d$ into Hayne's $K(T,z)$
(Eqs.~\ref{eq:Kc}--\ref{eq:Khayne}); solve the heat equation
\[
\rho\,c_p\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\left(K\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right),
\qquad K(T,z;K_d)
\]
with the radiative surface BC (Eq.~\ref{eq:surfbc}) at the top and the basal-flux
BC (Eq.~\ref{eq:bottombc}, $Q_b$ from Langseth et al.~1976) at the bottom, all
the way to a \emph{certified} periodic steady state via the flux-anchored
equilibrium solver (\code{src/lunar/equilibrium.py}). \\
\textbf{Output:} the cycle-mean profile $\Tm(z;K_d)$. \\
\textbf{Details:} solver in Ch.~\ref{ch:solver}; the certification, in
Ch.~\ref{ch:steady}, is the methodological contribution of this work.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}{Step C — from a curve $T(z;K_d)$ to one number $K_d^{*}$ (retrieval)}
\textbf{Inputs:} the deep-sensor data $(z_i, T_{\rm eq,i})$ from step~A; a grid of
trial $K_d$ values (28 at A15, 30 at A17). \\
\textbf{What happens:} call step~B at each trial $K_d$, compute the
deep-sensor RMSE,
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}(K_d)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[\Tm(z_i;K_d)-T_{\rm eq,i}\big]^{2}},
\]
then refine the minimum with a parabolic vertex through its three lowest points
(\code{pipeline/compute/retrieve\_kd.py}, \code{kd\_star\_from\_residuals}). \\
\textbf{Output:} the best-fit $K_d^{*}$ for the site. \\
\textbf{Details:} \S\ref{sec:sweep}.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}{Step D — how confident are we (uncertainty)}
\textbf{Inputs:} the same data, the same forward model. \\
\textbf{What happens:} \emph{(1)}~bootstrap: resample sensors with replacement
$1500$ times, re-run step~C on each, read the 95\% interval; \emph{(2)}~MCMC: a
joint $(K_d, Q_b)$ posterior; \emph{(3)}~AICc model selection (per-site $K_d$ vs
shared $K_d$); \emph{(4)}~error budget: re-retrieve at perturbed inputs
($Q_b$, albedo, $K_s$, density, \ldots) and sum the induced shifts in quadrature
(\code{pipeline/compute/compute\_error\_budget.py}). \\
\textbf{Output:} a 95\% CI, an error-budget total, a contrast and a $p$-value. \\
\textbf{Details:} Ch.~\ref{ch:stats}.
\end{stepbox}

   = \frac{21\times 10^{-3}}{10.1\times 10^{-3}}

\qquad\text{with RMSE}=0.95\;\mathrm{K}.

Our trial $5.0$ was close. The data prefer $4.58$, $9\%$ smaller.

\noindent Steps (0)--(2) and (5) used nothing beyond a calculator; step (4)
hides the time-stepping the solver performs to \emph{certify} that $T(z,t)$
has reached its periodic steady state, and step (6c) hides a re-run of the
whole pipeline $1500$ times. The box below opens step~(4) on a single time
step at a single cell so the PDE solution itself is concrete; the full
treatment lives in Ch.~\ref{ch:solver} and Ch.~\ref{ch:steady}.

\begin{stepbox}[breakable, enhanced jigsaw]{Inside step (4) --- solving the PDE step by step}

\textbf{(4a)~Discretise the column.}
The depth grid is geometric: $\Delta z=2$\,mm at the surface,
$8\%$ thicker per cell going down, $\sim$69 cells reaching $5$\,m
(\code{src/lunar/grid.py}, \code{make\_geometric\_grid}). Time is sliced into
$\Delta t = 1$\,h, so one $29.5$-day lunation is
$N_t = 24\times 29.5\approx 709$ steps.
At each cell $i$ we store $T_i^n$ (the temperature at depth $z_i$ at time
$t^n$). The PDE
$\rho c_p\,\partial_t T = \partial_z(K\,\partial_z T)$
becomes a recurrence that advances every $T_i^n$ to $T_i^{n+1}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(4b)~Crank--Nicolson update.}
We evaluate the right-hand side half at $t^n$ and half at $t^{n+1}$ ---
unconditionally stable and second-order in $\Delta t$:
\[
C_i\,\frac{T_i^{n+1}-T_i^n}{\Delta t}
= \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[\,F_i^{n+1}+F_i^n\,\right],
\qquad
F_i \;=\; \frac{K_{i+\tfrac{1}{2}}\,(T_{i+1}-T_i)}{\Delta z_{i+\tfrac{1}{2}}}
       -\frac{K_{i-\tfrac{1}{2}}\,(T_i-T_{i-1})}{\Delta z_{i-\tfrac{1}{2}}},
\]
where $C_i = \rho_i\,c_p(T_i)\,\Delta z_i$ is the heat capacity per area of
cell $i$. Collecting $T^{n+1}$ on the left gives a tridiagonal linear system
\[
-a_i^{\rm L}\,T_{i-1}^{n+1}
+ \bigl(1+a_i^{\rm L}+a_i^{\rm R}\bigr)\,T_i^{n+1}
- a_i^{\rm R}\,T_{i+1}^{n+1}
\;=\; b_i^{\rm RHS}\qquad\text{(one row per cell)}.
\]

\noindent\textbf{(4c)~Coefficients at one cell.}
Take $z=0.50$\,m at temperature $T\approx 253$\,K with $K_d^{\rm try}=5.0$:
the Hayne density profile gives $\rho(0.5)\approx 1\,798$ kg\,m$^{-3}$, the
Hemingway polynomial gives $c_p(253)\approx 650$ J\,kg$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, and
$K(T,z)\approx 10.1$\,mW\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ (from step~(2)). At this depth
$\Delta z\approx 4.7$\,cm, so
\[
C \;=\; \rho\,c_p\,\Delta z \;\approx\; 1798\times 650\times 0.047
       \;\approx\; 5.5\times 10^{4}\;\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}.
\]
The CN coupling coefficients are
$a^{\rm L,R} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t\,K_{\rm face}\,/(\Delta z\cdot C)$:
\[
a^{\rm L} \;\approx\; a^{\rm R} \;\approx\;
\frac{3600\times 10.1\!\times\! 10^{-3}}{0.047\times 5.5\!\times\! 10^{4}}
\;\approx\; 1.4\times 10^{-2}.
\]
So this cell's row of the matrix is essentially
$\bigl[\,-0.014,\;1.028,\;-0.014\,\bigr]$ acting on
$\bigl(T_{i-1},\,T_i,\,T_{i+1}\bigr)^{n+1}$. Far from the surface the column
is nearly diagonal --- the deep layers barely talk to each other in one hour.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(4d)~Thomas algorithm --- $O(N)$ solve of a tridiagonal.}
A tridiagonal system is solved in two sweeps (\code{src/lunar/solver.py},
\code{\_thomas}). Forward elimination: starting at the top, replace
$T_{i+1}$ with the relation from row~$i$:
\[
c_i' \;=\; \frac{c_i}{b_i-a_i\,c_{i-1}'},\qquad
d_i' \;=\; \frac{d_i-a_i\,d_{i-1}'}{b_i-a_i\,c_{i-1}'},
\]
where $(a_i,b_i,c_i)$ are the three diagonals and $d_i$ is the right-hand side.
Backward substitution: starting at the bottom,
$T_i^{n+1} = d_i' - c_i'\,T_{i+1}^{n+1}$.
Total cost: $\sim 8N$ multiplications --- 69 cells take under a microsecond.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(4e)~The non-linear surface boundary --- Newton.}
At $z=0$ the surface BC is non-linear in $T_s$ because of the radiative term:
\[
R(T_s) \;=\; (1-A)\,S(t) - \varepsilon\sigma T_s^4
- K_{\rm surf}\,\frac{T_s - T_1^{n+1}}{\Delta z_{\rm surf}} \;=\; 0.
\]
Take $t = 7$ days into the lunation:
$\cos(2\pi t/P)=\cos(1.49)\approx 0.082$, so
$S(t)\approx 1361\times\cos(26.1^\circ)\times 0.082 \approx 100$\,W\,m$^{-2}$.
At the previous time-step $T_s\approx 230$\,K, so Newton iterates
$T_s^{k+1}=T_s^k - R/R'$ with
$R' = -4\varepsilon\sigma T_s^3 - K_{\rm surf}/\Delta z_{\rm surf}$,
converging in $\sim$3 iterations to a few mK
(\code{src/lunar/solver.py}, \code{\_solve\_surface\_newton}).
This $T_s^{n+1}$ becomes the Dirichlet anchor for the linear system above.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(4f)~At the bottom, the flux boundary.}
We impose the Neumann condition $K\,\partial_z T = Q_b$ at $z=z_{\max}=5$\,m
by setting the last row of the matrix to
$T_{N}^{n+1} - T_{N-1}^{n+1} = Q_b\,\Delta z/K$. With $Q_b=21$\,mW\,m$^{-2}$
and $K\approx 10$ here, the imposed differential is
$\sim 21\times 10^{-3}/(10\times 10^{-3})\times 0.5 \approx 1.05$\,K across
the bottom cell --- exactly the geothermal slope (\S\ref{sec:bc}).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(4g)~Walk one full lunation.}
With one CN solve per time step and 709 time steps per lunation, one lunation
is $\sim$8\,000 Thomas sweeps and Newton calls. Each is fast; the JIT-compiled
loop runs in $\sim$8 seconds (\S\ref{sec:oneday}).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(4h)~Outer loop --- flux-anchored equilibrium.}
Brute-force time integration would converge on the $\sim$1000-lunation
\emph{diffusive timescale} (\S\ref{sec:f1}) --- decades of wall time. Instead,
the flux-anchored equilibrium solver (Ch.~\ref{ch:steady}) iterates two
operations: (Step~A) run one lunation forward to settle the surface skin and
read the anchor temperature $\langle T\rangle(z_0)$ at $z_0=0.55$\,m; (Step~B)
\emph{reconstruct} the deep profile below the anchor by directly integrating
the closure $d\langle T\rangle/dz = (Q_b+u_{\rm rect})/K(\langle T\rangle,z)$
with a midpoint rule.
The two-step iteration converges in $\sim 6$--$10$ outer cycles instead of
$1000$ lunations.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(4i)~Certification --- when is it really done?}
The solver does not declare success on \emph{cycle-to-cycle change} (that was
the F1 bug; \S\ref{sec:f1}). It declares success on three independent checks
performed inside \code{\_mean\_flux\_closure} (\code{src/lunar/equilibrium.py}):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{guess-independence}: same converged $\langle T\rangle(z)$ to
  within $0.05$\,K from two different starting profiles (e.g.\ $240$\,K vs
  $260$\,K everywhere);
\item \textbf{flux closure}: the mean conductive flux below the anchor equals
  $Q_b$ to within $10\%$;
\item \textbf{mean drift}: $\le 0.08$\,K over a $120$-lunation honest re-run.
\end{itemize}
Only then is $\langle T\rangle(z;K_d^{\rm try})$ written and step~(5) gets
its inputs.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Putting it together.} For our $K_d^{\rm try}=5.0$ the
two-step iteration delivers a settled column whose deepest cell reads
$\langle T\rangle(1.39\,{\rm m})\approx 254.0$\,K. That is the single number
step~(4) hands to step~(5), the source of the $0.5$\,K residual against
$T_{\rm eq}^{\rm obs}=253.53$\,K, and ultimately of the $0.96$\,K RMSE.
Every box in Fig.~\ref{fig:eqnflow} is now made of arithmetic.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{plain}
The same sentence in everyday words. Apollo's deep thermometers give us a
number per sensor (Step A). The Hayne formula tells us what the conductivity
\emph{would} look like if $K_d$ took a given value, and the heat equation lets us
predict what the deep temperatures would be at that $K_d$ (Step B). Step C
tries many $K_d$'s and picks the one whose predictions hit the data best.
Step D asks how lucky the fit might be. The final answer --- $K_d^{*}$ with a 95\%
interval and a budget --- is reported per site.
\end{plain}

\chapter{The physics, from the ground up}\label{ch:physics}
% =====================================================================
This chapter builds the equation our solver integrates, starting from one
empirical law (Fourier) and one conservation principle (energy). The chain is
short --- Fourier's law $\to$ heat equation $\to$ boundary conditions $\to$
the periodic steady state we will fit. Each step is motivated before any
equation appears. If you remember nothing else, remember this: the whole
retrieval rests on one PDE, two BCs, and the demand that the solution repeats
identically every lunation.

\section{Heat conduction: what Fourier's law says}\label{sec:fourier}
Heat flows from hot to cold. Fourier's law quantifies this: the heat flux $q$
(watts per square metre, W\,m$^{-2}$) through a material is proportional to the
temperature gradient:
\begin{equation}
q = -K\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z},
\label{eq:fourier}
\end{equation}
where $z$ is depth (metres), $T$ is temperature (Kelvin), and $K$ is the
thermal conductivity (W\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$). The minus sign says heat flows
\emph{downhill} in temperature: from the hot surface into the cooler subsurface
during the day, and back up at night.

\begin{plain}
Imagine holding one end of a metal rod over a flame. Heat flows from the hot end
to the cold end. $K$ tells you how fast: copper ($K\approx400$) carries heat
quickly; lunar regolith ($K\approx0.003$) is an extraordinary insulator. The
$\partial T/\partial z$ part says heat flows faster when the temperature changes
sharply over a short distance (steep gradient) and slower when it changes
gradually (gentle gradient).
\end{plain}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_book_fourier.pdf}
\caption{Reading Fourier's law. (a) Heat flows down the column from the hot
surface to the cool subsurface. (b) The ``gradient'' $\partial T/\partial z$ in
the equation is simply the \emph{slope} of the temperature-versus-depth curve:
where the curve is steep (near the surface) the flux is large; where it is gentle
(at depth) the flux is small. The conductivity $K$ is the constant of
proportionality.}
\label{fig:fourier}
\end{figure}

\section{The heat equation: conservation of energy in a column}\label{sec:heateq}
Now demand energy conservation in a thin slab of regolith. The rate at which the
slab stores heat equals the difference between what flows in through its top face
and what flows out through its bottom face. This bookkeeping gives the
\textbf{heat equation}:
\begin{equation}
\rho(z)\,c_p(T)\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
   = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\left(K(T,z)\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right),
\label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the density (kg\,m$^{-3}$), $c_p$ is the specific heat capacity
(J\,kg$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$, how much energy it takes to warm a kilogram by one
Kelvin; for lunar regolith the code uses the Hemingway et al.~(1973) polynomial),
and $t$ is time (seconds).

\begin{plain}
The left side says ``this layer is warming (or cooling) at such-and-such a
rate.'' The right side says ``because this much more heat is arriving from above
than leaving below (or vice versa).'' The three material properties --- $\rho$
(how heavy), $c_p$ (how hard to heat), and $K$ (how easily heat passes through)
--- are the knobs. This single equation is the physics of the entire study.
\end{plain}

\begin{figure}[t!]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.65\linewidth]{figures/fig_book_heateq.pdf}
\caption{Reading the heat equation. Take one thin layer of regolith as a box.
Heat flows in through its top face and out through its bottom face; whatever does
not leave is \emph{stored}, warming the layer. ``Stored equals in minus out'' is
the whole content of the equation: the left side $\rho c_p\,\partial_t T$ is the
storage rate, the right side $\partial_z(K\,\partial_z T)$ is minus the change in
flux across the layer.}
\label{fig:conduction}
\end{figure}

\section{The lunar forcing: why the surface oscillates wildly}\label{sec:forcing}
At the surface, the Sun drives a huge day--night temperature swing. The lunar day
is about 29.5 Earth days long, so the surface bakes for $\sim$14.75 days and then
sits in darkness for $\sim$14.75 days. Surface temperatures swing from
$\sim$100\,K at night ($-173\,$\textdegree C) to $\sim$390\,K at noon
($+117\,$\textdegree C). This is the ``forcing'' that drives everything
(Fig.~\ref{fig:forcing}).

\subsection{Which terms we keep, and which we drop}\label{sec:forcing-neglect}
The pipeline drives the solver with
$S(t)=S_0\cos(\mathrm{lat})\,\max(0,\cos 2\pi t/P)$. This is the simplest forcing
that captures the day-night cycle. Three real terms are absent. It is worth
estimating them so we know how much the simplification costs.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Neglected term & Magnitude & Estimated bias on $K_d^{*}$ \\
\midrule
Solar declination $\pm 1.5^{\circ}$ & $\cos(\mathrm{lat})$ varies by
  $\le 1.5\%$ & $\le 0.05$ mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ \\
Earth orbit eccentricity & $S_0$ varies by $\pm 3.3\%$ over a year &
  $\le 0.1$ mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ \\
Site longitude (LST offset) & a constant phase shift; no effect on cycle mean &
  $\sim 0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The first two enter only through the cycle-mean insolation, which the deep
$\Tm$ profile responds to via Eq.~\eqref{eq:closure}. Their combined effect on
$\Tm$ at $z\sim 1$~m is at most a few tenths of a Kelvin, translating to a
shift in $K_d^{*}$ that is small compared to the bootstrap CI half-width
($\sim 1.7$ mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ at A15). The third (longitude) shifts the
phase of the diurnal wave but not its mean, so the steady-state retrieval is
insensitive. A correct SPICE/DE440 implementation
(\code{src/lunar/ephem.py}) exists for future per-timestamp work that
would close even these terms; the audit appendix (Appendix~\ref{app:flags}, F2)
records the decision to use the idealised forcing for this paper.

\section{The skin depth: why only the top matters for the daily cycle}\label{sec:skin}
This ``thermal wave'' travels downward, but it \emph{dies out} exponentially. Its
amplitude falls by a factor of $e\approx2.72$ over the \textbf{skin depth}:
\begin{equation}
\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa}{\omega}},
\label{eq:skin}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa=K/(\rho c_p)$ is the thermal diffusivity (how fast heat spreads)
and $\omega=2\pi/P$ is the angular frequency of the forcing ($P$ = one lunation).

\subsection{Where the skin depth comes from}\label{sec:skinderiv}
The formula is not a guess; it falls out of the heat equation in one short
derivation. Take Eq.~\eqref{eq:heat} with constant properties,
$\partial_t T=\kappa\,\partial_z^2 T$, and force the surface sinusoidally,
$T(0,t)=\Tm+A\sin\omega t$, with $T\to\Tm$ far below. Look for a travelling-wave
solution
\begin{equation}
T(z,t)=\Tm+\operatorname{Im}\!\big[A\,e^{\,i(\omega t-kz)}\big],
\end{equation}
where the wavenumber $k$ is allowed to be \emph{complex}. Substituting into the
heat equation, $\partial_t\!\to i\omega$ and $\partial_z^2\!\to -k^2$, so
$i\omega=-\kappa k^{2}$ and
\begin{equation}
k=\sqrt{-\,i\omega/\kappa}=\frac{1-i}{\delta},
\qquad \delta\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa}{\omega}} .
\end{equation}
Because $k$ has equal real and imaginary parts, $e^{-ikz}$ splits cleanly into a
\emph{decay} and a \emph{phase lag}:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;T(z,t)=\Tm+A\,e^{-z/\delta}\,
\sin\!\Big(\omega t-\frac{z}{\delta}\Big)\;}
\label{eq:wave}
\end{equation}
The amplitude drops by a factor $e$ every skin depth, and the wave arrives later
with depth (the phase lag $z/\delta$). Equation~\eqref{eq:wave} is \emph{exactly}
the closed form returned by \code{analytical\_thermal\_wave} in
\code{src/lunar/solver.py}; the solver's unit test checks the numerical scheme
against it in the constant-property regime.

Putting in lunar numbers ($\kappa\sim1$--$1.3\times10^{-8}$~m$^2$\,s$^{-1}$ in the
upper regolith, $\omega=2\pi/P=2.46\times10^{-6}$~s$^{-1}$) gives $\delta\approx4$~cm
at the loose surface, growing to $\approx10$~cm by 20~cm depth as the conductivity
rises. After a few skin depths --- roughly 30--50~cm --- the daily wave is
negligible and the regolith feels only the \emph{average} heating over the whole
lunation (Fig.~\ref{fig:waterfall}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_book_skinwave.pdf}
\caption{The same wave sampled at fixed depths. The full $\sim$250\,K surface
swing is already halved a few centimetres down and is essentially flat by
$\sim$40\,cm; the deep lines sit on the gently-rising mean profile. The collapse
is the $e^{-z/\delta}$ envelope of Eq.~\eqref{eq:wave} in action.}
\label{fig:waterfall}
\end{figure}

\begin{keypt}
This split --- a fast, shallow daily wave on top of a slow, deep mean temperature
profile --- is the single most important fact for everything that follows.
The surface oscillates wildly; the deep column sits at a nearly constant temperature
that is set by the \emph{mean} energy balance and the upward geothermal flux. Keep
this picture in mind through every chapter.
\end{keypt}

\section{Boundary conditions: what happens at the top and bottom}\label{sec:bc}
The heat equation (Eq.~\ref{eq:heat}) needs a rule at each end of the column ---
these are called \textbf{boundary conditions}.

\subsection{Top (surface): a radiative energy balance}
The surface has no atmosphere above it, so sunlight absorbed must equal heat
radiated away plus heat conducted downward:
\begin{equation}
(1-A)\,S(t) = \varepsilon\sigma T_s^4 + K\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\Big|_{z=0},
\label{eq:surfbc}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item $A$ is the albedo (reflectivity, $\sim$0.13): fraction of sunlight bounced away;
\item $S(t)$ is the incident solar flux (W\,m$^{-2}$), which swings between
  $\sim$1361 at noon and 0 at night;
\item $\varepsilon$ is the emissivity ($\sim$0.95): how efficiently the surface
  radiates heat as infrared;
\item $\sigma=5.6704\times10^{-8}$ W\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-4}$ is the Stefan--Boltzmann
  constant;
\item $T_s$ is the surface temperature.
\end{itemize}
The $T_s^4$ term makes this equation \emph{non-linear} --- we cannot solve for
$T_s$ by algebra; it must be found numerically each timestep
(\S\ref{sec:newton}).

\begin{plain}
At noon: lots of sunlight arrives, the surface gets very hot, and it radiates that
energy away as infrared ($T^4$ radiation). A tiny bit of heat also conducts down
into the soil. At night: no sunlight, so the surface cools by radiating its stored
heat, and some heat conducts back up from the still-warm subsurface. The balance
between these processes sets $T_s$ at every moment.
\end{plain}

\subsection{Bottom: the geothermal flux}\label{sec:bottombc}
From far below, the Moon's interior supplies a small, steady heat flux $Q_b$
(``basal flux''). This heat comes from radioactive decay in the Moon's interior.
At Apollo~15, the measured value is $Q_b=21$\,mW\,m$^{-2}$; at Apollo~17,
$Q_b=15$\,mW\,m$^{-2}$ (Langseth et al.\ 1976). These are tiny compared to the
$\sim$1361\,W\,m$^{-2}$ noon sunlight, but they set the \emph{deep} temperature
gradient.

At the base of our model column, the boundary condition is:
\begin{equation}
K\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\bigg|_{z=z_{\max}} = Q_b.
\label{eq:bottombc}
\end{equation}

\begin{plain}
Think of $Q_b$ as a slow trickle of heat rising from below, always on, always
the same. It is so weak that at the surface you would never notice it. But at
depth --- below the daily noise --- that trickle is all there is, and it
controls how steeply the temperature rises with depth. That is exactly the
signal we use to measure $K_d$.
\end{plain}

\section{Periodic steady state: the key concept}\label{sec:fluxclosure}
After long enough, the column reaches a \textbf{periodic steady state}: every
lunation is identical to the last. No net heat is being stored anywhere; the
column has fully adapted to both the surface forcing and the basal flux.

Two conditions define this state, and the second one is the backbone of the
entire method:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=4pt]
\item \textbf{The surface skin repeats:} the shallow daily oscillation is
periodic --- today's temperature cycle looks exactly like yesterday's.
\item \textbf{The deep flux is uniform:} below the skin, there are no heat
sources, so in steady state the same geothermal flux $Q_b$ must pass upward
through \emph{every} layer. Combined with Fourier's law, this fixes the
gradient of the cycle-mean temperature $\Tm$ at every depth:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;K(\Tm,z)\,\frac{d\Tm}{dz}=Q_b
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{d\Tm}{dz}=\frac{Q_b}{K(\Tm,z)}\;}
\label{eq:closure}
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keypt}
Equation~\eqref{eq:closure} is the backbone of the whole method.
Read it out loud: ``the slope of the mean temperature profile at any depth equals
the basal flux divided by the conductivity at that depth.'' Once you know the
temperature at \emph{one} depth, this equation fixes the temperature at every
depth below it --- because it tells you the slope everywhere. This is not an
approximation; it is energy conservation applied to a column in steady state.
Chapters~\ref{ch:steady} and~\ref{ch:retrieval} are built entirely on this fact.
\end{keypt}

\begin{plain}
Analogy: a river flowing through a pipe of varying width. In steady state, the same
volume of water passes through every cross-section per second (conservation of
mass). Where the pipe is narrow, the water flows fast; where it is wide, the
water flows slowly. Similarly: where $K$ is small (poor conductor, like the
shallow loose regolith), the temperature gradient must be steep to carry the same
$Q_b$ of heat; where $K$ is large (better conductor, like the compacted deep
regolith), the gradient is gentle.
\end{plain}

\begin{figure}[t!]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/fig_book_fluxclosure.pdf}
\caption{Reading the flux closure $d\langle T\rangle/dz=Q_b/K$. (a) In a widening
pipe the same water crosses every slice each second, so it must move \emph{fast}
where the pipe is narrow. (b) In steady state the same heat $Q_b$ crosses every
layer, so the temperature gradient must be \emph{steep} where $K$ is small (the
loose shallow regolith) and \emph{gentle} where $K$ is large (the compacted
deep regolith) --- the profile bends at the kink.}
\label{fig:fluxclosure}
\end{figure}


\section{Practice problems}\label{sec:prob-physics}
Work these before moving on; full solutions follow each. They use only the
results of this chapter.

\begin{problembox}[Problem 2.1 — skin depth and conductivity]
Apollo~17's deep regolith conducts heat about $1.8\times$ better than Apollo~15's.
Assuming the same $\rho c_p$, by what factor is its diurnal skin depth larger?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
Skin depth $\delta=\sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}$ with $\kappa=K/\rho c_p$, so
$\delta\propto\sqrt{K}$. A factor $1.8$ in $K$ gives
$\sqrt{1.8}\approx1.34$ --- the wave reaches about a third deeper at A17 than at
A15 for the same forcing.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 2.2 — the deep gradient]
Using the steady-state closure $d\Tm/dz=Q_b/K$, estimate the deep temperature
gradient at Apollo~15 ($Q_b=21$~mW\,m$^{-2}$, $K_d=4.58$~mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$).
Why is it so gentle?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
$d\Tm/dz = Q_b/K_d = (21\times10^{-3})/(4.58\times10^{-3}) \approx 4.6$~K\,m$^{-1}$:
the temperature rises only about $4.6$~K per metre. It is gentle because the
basal flux is tiny ($\sim$10$^{5}\times$ smaller than noon sunlight); below the
skin, that faint trickle is the \emph{only} signal, and it is exactly the
fingerprint we use to read $K_d$.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 2.3 — phase lag]
At what depth does the daily wave lag the surface by half a lunation
(a phase of $\pi$)? Take $\delta\approx8$~cm.
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:wave} the phase lag is $z/\delta$. Setting $z/\delta=\pi$ gives
$z=\pi\delta\approx 25$~cm. At that depth the wave is also attenuated by
$e^{-\pi}\approx0.04$ --- only $\sim$4\% of the surface amplitude survives, so the
``half-lunation-late'' wave is already almost undetectable. This is why only the
top few tens of centimetres carry any diurnal information.
\end{solutionbox}

\chapter{The two conductivity models}\label{ch:models}
% =====================================================================
$K$ is not a single number --- it depends on temperature (hotter regolith
radiates across pores, conducting better) and depth (deeper regolith is more
compacted by overlying weight, conducting better). Two published models capture
this.

\section{Hayne et al.\ (2017): the H-parameter model}\label{sec:hayne}

\subsection{The formula}
The solid ``contact'' conductivity rises from a surface value $K_s$ to a deep
value $K_d$ as the regolith compacts, over an $e$-folding depth $H$:
\begin{align}
K_c(z) &= K_d - (K_d-K_s)\,e^{-z/H}. \label{eq:Kc}
\end{align}
At the surface ($z=0$): $K_c=K_d-(K_d-K_s)\cdot 1=K_s$. At great depth
($z\gg H$): the exponential vanishes, $K_c\to K_d$. The parameter $H$ controls
how fast the transition happens.

A radiative term is added on top --- photons can bounce across pore spaces, and
this effect grows with $T^3$:
\begin{align}
K(T,z) &= K_c(z)\,\bigl[\,1+\chi\,(T/T_{\mathrm{ref}})^3\,\bigr].\label{eq:Khayne}
\end{align}

\subsection{Where the form comes from}\label{sec:hayneform}
Neither term is arbitrary; each has a clear physical origin.

\textbf{The contact term.} In dry regolith, heat crosses from grain to grain
through the tiny contact patches where particles touch. Deeper down, the weight of
the overburden compacts the column: grains pack tighter, contact patches grow, and
the conductivity climbs from its loose surface value $K_s$ toward a compacted deep
value $K_d$. The simplest monotonic curve that starts at $K_s$, saturates at
$K_d$, and shares the \emph{same} compaction scale as the measured density profile
$\rho(z)$ is the exponential relaxation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Kc} --- one $e$-folding
depth $H$ for both. It is a one-parameter (in $K_d$) interpolation, not a
first-principles law, but it is the right \emph{shape}.

\textbf{The radiative term.} Across a pore of size $d$, heat also crosses as
thermal radiation. The radiative conductivity of a granular medium scales as
$K_{\rm rad}\sim 4\,\varepsilon\,\sigma\,d\,T^3$ --- the $T^3$ is the derivative of
the Stefan--Boltzmann $T^4$ law, i.e.\ how fast radiated power changes with
temperature. Writing this as a fractional enhancement of the contact term at a
reference temperature gives the bracket in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Khayne}: $\chi$ is the
radiative-to-contact ratio at $T_{\mathrm{ref}}=350$~K. At cold lunar night
($\sim$100~K) the $T^3$ term is negligible; by lunar noon ($\sim$390~K) it can
more than double the conductivity (Fig.~\ref{fig:models}b).

\begin{plain}
Two ways heat gets through loose soil: \emph{through the grains} where they touch
(more contact when packed tighter, hence rising with depth), and \emph{across the
gaps} as infrared glow (stronger when hotter, hence the $T^3$). Hayne's formula is
just those two effects added together, with $K_d$ --- the one number we
re-measure --- setting the deep contact value.
\end{plain}

\subsection{The published parameters}
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrl@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & Meaning & Value & Source \\
\midrule

$K_d$ & deep contact conductivity & 3.4 mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ & Hayne (2017), global \\
$H$ & compaction $e$-folding depth & 6 cm & Hayne (2017) \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{What the code looks like}
In \code{src/lunar/properties.py}:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
def conductivity_hayne(T, z, Ks=7.4e-4, Kd=3.4e-3, H=0.06, chi=2.7):
    Kc = Kd - (Kd - Ks) * np.exp(-z / H)          # contact part: K_s -> K_d
    return Kc * (1.0 + chi * (T / 350.0) ** 3)    # + radiative T^3 term
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Why the literature $K_d$ is 3.4 --- and why we re-measure it}
Hayne et al.\ fit Eqs.~\eqref{eq:Kc}--\eqref{eq:Khayne} to the
\emph{globally averaged} day--night temperature curves measured from orbit by
the Diviner instrument. A single set of parameters reproduced the global average,
and $K_d=3.4$ mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ is that global deep asymptote. It was
never intended to be site-specific. Different geological settings ---
mare basalt versus highland valley debris --- might have genuinely different deep
conductivities. That is the gap we fill.

\begin{plain}
$K_d$ = 3.4 is a whole-Moon average. Our whole project is asking: is the deep
regolith at Apollo~15 the same as at Apollo~17? Spoiler: no. One is about twice the
other.
\end{plain}

The density follows the same exponential shape:
\begin{equation}
\rho(z)=\rho_d-(\rho_d-\rho_s)\,e^{-z/H},\qquad \rho_s=1100,\ \rho_d=1800\ \text{kg\,m}^{-3}.
\end{equation}

\section{Mart\'inez \& Siegler (2021): a density-based model}\label{sec:martinez}
The Martínez \& Siegler form abandons Hayne's depth-based scaling and writes the
conductivity \emph{directly} as a function of density and temperature:
\begin{equation}
K(T,\rho)=(A_1\,\rho+A_2)\,k_{\mathrm{am}}(T)+(B_1\,\rho+B_2)\,T^3.
\label{eq:Kmartinez}
\end{equation}
There are two terms, both density-weighted, and the structure parallels Hayne's
factorisation: a contact part and a radiative part. But every choice is
different, so let us unpack it.

\subsection{The amorphous-solid baseline $k_{\mathrm{am}}(T)$}
Lunar regolith grains are dominated by impact-shocked, partially amorphous
silicate glass --- not pristine crystal. Crystalline phonon conduction rises
linearly with $T$; amorphous phonon conduction does \emph{not}. Woods-Robinson
et al.\ (2019) measured the temperature dependence of synthetic lunar-glass
analogues and reported a 9-term polynomial in $T^{-4}\!,\,T^{-3}\!,\ldots,T^4$
that captures the data from 30~K to 400~K. The Martínez code adopts that
polynomial as $k_{\mathrm{am}}(T)$; it is the \emph{intrinsic} conductivity of a
solid amorphous grain at temperature $T$. In our code (\code{src/lunar/properties.py})
its nine coefficients are \code{MS\_KAM\_A\ldots MS\_KAM\_I}.

\subsection{The contact term $(A_1\rho+A_2)\,k_{\mathrm{am}}$}
Bulk regolith is not a solid block; it is a packing of grains. Two facts about
contact conduction matter:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{The intrinsic grain conductivity sets the upper limit} --- you cannot
  conduct better than the material itself. That is $k_{\mathrm{am}}(T)$.
\item \emph{Packing reduces it.} Heat must funnel through finite contact patches
  whose total area scales with the packing fraction, i.e.\ with bulk density~$\rho$.
\end{enumerate}
The Martínez prescription is simply the linear ansatz: contact conductivity is a
fraction $(A_1\rho+A_2)$ of the intrinsic grain conductivity. The slope $A_1$ and
intercept $A_2$ are calibrated from laboratory glass-bead measurements:
$A_1=5.08\times10^{-6}$~m$^3$\,kg$^{-1}$, $A_2=-0.0051$ (dimensionless).
At lunar deep density ($\rho\approx 1800$~kg\,m$^{-3}$) the prefactor is
$(A_1\rho+A_2)\approx 4.1\times10^{-3}$ --- i.e.\ the bulk regolith conducts at
$\sim$0.4\% of its grains' intrinsic conductivity, which is the right order of
magnitude for a granular medium with $\sim$50\% porosity.

\subsection{The radiative term $(B_1\rho+B_2)\,T^3$}
The $T^3$ structure is the same as Hayne's --- the temperature derivative of
Stefan-Boltzmann emission --- but the prefactor is again density-weighted. The
intuition: tighter packing reduces the size of the pores across which radiation
crosses. The published coefficients are $B_1=2.00\times10^{-13}$ and
$B_2=-1.95\times10^{-10}$ (in SI). At deep density these give a radiative term
that doubles the contact part by $T\approx 360$~K --- the same crossover
temperature we get from Hayne (Problem~3.1).

\subsection{What is the ``free parameter'' here?}
Look at Eq.~\eqref{eq:Kmartinez} again: there is no $K_d$. The model is fully
specified by $\rho(z)$. We turn it into a one-parameter retrieval by introducing
$\alpha$, a multiplier on Hayne's deep density asymptote:
$\rho_d \to \alpha\,\rho_d$. The bulk profile keeps Hayne's exponential shape
but its deep value scales with $\alpha$. Sweeping $\alpha$ and minimising the
RMSE gives $\alpha^{*}$ per site --- the equivalent of $K_d^{*}$ in Hayne's
formulation.

\begin{plain}
Two ways to spell the same physics. Hayne lets you tune the deep
\emph{conductivity}; Martínez lets you tune the deep \emph{density}. If the
inter-site contrast we observe is real, \emph{both} retrievals should report a
denser-and-better-conducting deep column at Apollo~17 --- which they do
(Ch.~\ref{ch:crosschecks}). Two completely independent parameterisations agreeing
is a far stronger statement than either alone.
\end{plain}

\section{Practice problems}\label{sec:prob-models}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 3.1 — when radiation takes over]
At what temperature does the radiative term equal the contact term (so that
$K=2K_c$)? Use $\chi=2.7$, $T_{\mathrm{ref}}=350$~K.
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
Set $\chi(T/T_{\mathrm{ref}})^3=1$: $T=T_{\mathrm{ref}}/\chi^{1/3}
=350/2.7^{1/3}=350/1.39\approx252$~K. Below $\sim$252~K the contact term
dominates; above it (most of the lunar day) radiation across pores is the larger
contributor --- which is why $K$ roughly doubles from night to noon.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 3.2 — the compaction half-depth]
With $K_s=0.74$, $K_d=4.58$, and $H=6$~cm, at what depth is the contact
conductivity halfway between $K_s$ and $K_d$?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
$K_c=K_s+(K_d-K_s)(1-e^{-z/H})$; ``halfway'' means $1-e^{-z/H}=\tfrac12$, i.e.
$e^{-z/H}=\tfrac12$, so $z=H\ln 2=6\times0.693\approx4.2$~cm. The contact
conductivity completes most of its rise within the top few centimetres --- the
same shallow zone where the daily wave lives.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 3.3 — why $K$ outruns $\rho$]
From surface to depth the bulk density rises only $\sim$1.6$\times$ (1100 to
1800~kg\,m$^{-3}$), yet the contact conductivity rises $\sim$6$\times$
($K_d/K_s\approx4.58/0.74$). Why does conductivity climb so much faster than
density?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
Contact conduction depends on the \emph{area} of grain-to-grain contacts, which
grows super-linearly with packing: as the column compacts, each grain gains more
and larger contacts, so $K\sim\rho^{\,n}$ with $n>1$. A modest density increase
therefore produces a much larger conductivity increase --- the physical reason
$K_d$ is a sensitive, site-specific quantity worth measuring on its own.
\end{solutionbox}

depends on $T$ and $z$ in complicated ways. So we \textbf{discretise}: chop the
column into cells and time into steps, and advance the temperature numerically.
This chapter walks through the solver in \code{src/lunar/solver.py} and
\code{src/lunar/grid.py}, piece by piece.

\section{The geometric depth grid}\label{sec:grid}
The daily wave needs millimetre resolution at the top (because the temperature
changes rapidly over a few centimetres), but the deep column can be coarse
(because the temperature there barely changes). So the cells grow geometrically
--- each $\sim$8\% thicker than the one above (Fig.~\ref{fig:grid}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/fig_book_grid3d.pdf}
\caption{The geometric depth grid. Cells are 2\,mm at the surface --- fine enough
to resolve the daily wave --- and grow by $\sim$8\% each layer, reaching tens of
centimetres at depth where the temperature is nearly flat. Just $\sim$69 cells
span the full 5\,m column; a uniform 2\,mm grid would need $\sim$2500.}
\label{fig:grid}
\end{figure}

In \code{src/lunar/grid.py}:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
def make_geometric_grid(z_max=5.0, dz0=0.002, growth=0.08):
    faces, dz = [0.0], dz0
    while faces[-1] < z_max:
        faces.append(faces[-1] + dz)
        dz *= (1 + growth)              # each layer ~8% thicker
    # cell centres z_mid, thicknesses dz follow from the faces
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{plain}
The top cell is 2\,mm thick (to resolve the daily wave precisely). By a depth
of 1\,m the cells are $\sim$10\,cm thick. By 5\,m they are $\sim$1\,m thick. This
gives $\sim$69 cells total: fine where it matters, cheap where it does not. The
alternative --- uniform 2\,mm cells all the way to 5\,m --- would need 2500 cells
and be $\sim$35$\times$ slower.
\end{plain}

\section{One time step: Crank--Nicolson}\label{sec:cn}
We step forward in time by one hour (3600\,s) per step. There are $\sim$709 steps
in one lunation. At each step, we need to compute the new temperature profile
$T^{n+1}$ from the old one $T^n$. We use the \textbf{Crank--Nicolson} scheme,
which averages the heat fluxes at the old and new time levels. This produces a
tridiagonal linear system $A\,T^{n+1}=d$:

\subsection{Deriving the update}\label{sec:cnderiv}
Integrate the heat equation (Eq.~\ref{eq:heat}) over cell $i$ (width
$\Delta z_i$, heat capacity $C_i=\rho c_p\,\Delta z_i$) and over one step
$\Delta t$. The storage term becomes $C_i(T^{n+1}_i-T^{n}_i)/\Delta t$, and the
right-hand side becomes the difference of the two face fluxes
$q_{i\pm1/2}=-K_{i\pm1/2}\,(T_{i\pm1}-T_i)/\Delta z_{i\pm1/2}$, where $K_{i\pm1/2}$
is the \emph{harmonic} mean of the neighbouring conductivities --- the correct
average for heat passing through two layers in series. Crank--Nicolson evaluates
that flux divergence as the \emph{average} of the old ($n$) and new ($n{+}1$)
levels (the $\theta=\tfrac12$ rule). Collecting terms gives, for each interior
cell,
\begin{equation}
-\tfrac12\alpha^L_i T^{n+1}_{i-1}
+\big(1+\tfrac12(\alpha^L_i+\alpha^R_i)\big)T^{n+1}_i
-\tfrac12\alpha^R_i T^{n+1}_{i+1}
=\tfrac12\alpha^L_i T^{n}_{i-1}
+\big(1-\tfrac12(\alpha^L_i+\alpha^R_i)\big)T^{n}_i
+\tfrac12\alpha^R_i T^{n}_{i+1},
\label{eq:cnrow}
\end{equation}
with face couplings $\alpha^{L/R}_i=\Delta t\,K_{i\mp1/2}/(C_i\,\Delta z_{i\mp1/2})$.
The left side is the matrix $A$, the right side the vector $d$. Read off the three
bands: sub-diagonal $a_i=-\tfrac12\alpha^L_i$, diagonal
$b_i=1+\tfrac12(\alpha^L_i+\alpha^R_i)$, super-diagonal $c_i=-\tfrac12\alpha^R_i$ ---
\emph{exactly} the \code{a}, \code{b}, \code{c} arrays built in the code below.

\begin{keypt}
\textbf{Why it is unconditionally stable.} Put a Fourier mode
$T^n_i=g^{\,n}e^{\,i\phi i}$ into the constant-coefficient form of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cnrow}. The per-step amplification factor is
\[
g=\frac{1-2\alpha\sin^2(\phi/2)}{1+2\alpha\sin^2(\phi/2)},\qquad
\alpha=\frac{K\,\Delta t}{C\,\Delta z}\ge0,
\]
and since the numerator never exceeds the denominator in magnitude, $|g|\le1$ for
\emph{every} step size $\Delta t$ and every mode $\phi$. Crank--Nicolson therefore
never blows up --- unlike explicit Euler, which demands
$\Delta t<\tfrac12\Delta z^2/\kappa$. That is why the solver can take comfortable
1-hour steps even with 2-mm surface cells. (The code is Crank--Nicolson, not
explicit Euler --- a point worth stating because it is occasionally mis-described.)
\end{keypt}

In \code{src/lunar/solver.py}, function \code{\_step}:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
K   = K_func(T_prev, z_mid)              # conductivity at current T, each cell
Kf  = harmonic_mean(K)                   # conductivity at cell faces
cap = rho * cp_func(T_prev) * dz         # heat capacity of each cell [J/m^2/K]
aL  = dt * Kf[:-1] / (dz_c[:-1] * cap)  # left-face coupling coefficient
aR  = dt * Kf[1:]  / (dz_c[1:]  * cap)  # right-face coupling coefficient
# The tridiagonal matrix A has three bands:
a = -0.5*aL                # sub-diagonal (coupling to cell above)
c = -0.5*aR                # super-diagonal (coupling to cell below)
b = 1 + 0.5*(aL + aR)     # main diagonal (self + both neighbours)
# The right-hand side d:
d = 0.5*aL*Tleft + (1 - 0.5*(aL+aR))*T_prev + 0.5*aR*Tright
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{plain}
What this does, in words: each cell's new temperature is a weighted average of its
old temperature and its neighbours' temperatures. Cells with high conductivity
exchange heat faster (larger coupling coefficients $a_L$, $a_R$). Cells with high
heat capacity ($\rho c_p \Delta z$) respond more slowly. The 0.5 factors are the
Crank--Nicolson averaging between old and new time levels, which makes the scheme
second-order accurate and unconditionally stable (no timestep restriction from the
grid spacing).
\end{plain}

\section{Solving the system: the Thomas algorithm}\label{sec:thomas}
A tridiagonal system (each equation involves at most three unknowns) can be
solved in just two passes --- one forward, one backward --- in $O(N)$ time.
This is the \textbf{Thomas algorithm}:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
# Forward elimination: sweep top to bottom
for i in range(1, n):
    m     = b[i] - a[i]*cp[i-1]
    cp[i] = c[i] / m
    dp[i] = (d[i] - a[i]*dp[i-1]) / m
# Back-substitution: sweep bottom to top
x[n-1] = dp[n-1]
for i in range(n-2, -1, -1):
    x[i] = dp[i] - cp[i]*x[i+1]
\end{lstlisting}

This is already JIT-compiled with Numba in the code (\code{\_thomas} in
\code{src/lunar/solver.py}), so it runs at near-C speed.

\section{The non-linear surface: Newton's method}\label{sec:newton}
The surface energy balance (Eq.~\ref{eq:surfbc}) has a $T_s^4$ term, so we
cannot solve for $T_s$ by simple algebra. We use Newton's method: guess $T_s$,
compute how far the energy balance is from zero (the ``residual'' $R$), compute
the slope of $R$ vs.\ $T_s$ (the ``Jacobian'' $R'$), and update:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
for _ in range(max_iter):           # usually converges in 2-3 steps
    R  = (1-A)*S - eps*sigma*Ts**4 - Ksurf*(Ts - Tsub)/(0.5*dz0)
    dR = -4*eps*sigma*Ts**3 - 2*Ksurf/dz0    # derivative of R w.r.t. Ts
    Ts = Ts - R/dR                            # Newton update
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{plain}
The residual $R$ says: ``absorbed sunlight minus radiated heat minus conducted heat
--- how far from balance are we?'' If $R>0$, the surface is too cold (not radiating
enough); if $R<0$, it is too hot. Newton's method adjusts $T_s$ in the right
direction, overshooting by just the right amount to converge in 2--3 steps.
\end{plain}

\section{One complete lunation: stepping through the day}\label{sec:oneday}
One lunation = $\sim$709 hourly steps. At each step:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
\item Compute the solar flux $S(t)$ at the current time.
\item Solve the surface boundary condition by Newton's method to get $T_s$.
\item Build the tridiagonal system from the current temperatures.
\item Apply the bottom boundary condition: $K\,dT/dz = Q_b$ at the base.
\item Solve the tridiagonal system with the Thomas algorithm to get the new
      temperature profile.
\item Record the temperatures.
\end{enumerate}

After one full lunation we have the complete time-resolved temperature field
$T(z,t)$ for that cycle.

\section{The spin-up: repeating lunations until steady state}\label{sec:spinup}
To find the steady state, we repeat lunations one after another. After each
lunation, we compare the temperature profile to the previous lunation's:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
for cycle in range(1, n_lunations_spinup + 1):
    T_start = T.copy()
    for k in range(1, n_t):              # march through one lunar day
        T = step(T, ...)                 # the inner time-stepping loop
    if max(abs(T - T_start)) < 0.01 and cycle >= 2:
        break                            # "converged" -- but is it really?
\end{lstlisting}

This brute-force approach works \emph{in principle}: given enough lunations, the
profile converges to the true steady state. But \emph{how many} lunations is the
whole problem of the next chapter.


\section{Practice problems}\label{sec:prob-solver}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 4.1 — why an implicit scheme?]
The surface cells are $\Delta z_0=2$~mm thick and the near-surface diffusivity is
$\kappa\approx1\times10^{-8}$~m$^2$\,s$^{-1}$. What is the largest stable time step
for \emph{explicit} Euler, and how does it compare with the solver's 1-hour
Crank--Nicolson step?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
Explicit Euler needs $\Delta t<\tfrac12\Delta z_0^2/\kappa
=\tfrac12(2\times10^{-3})^2/10^{-8}=200$~s. The solver uses $3600$~s --- already
$18\times$ larger --- and Crank--Nicolson has \emph{no} such limit
(\S\ref{sec:cnderiv}). With explicit Euler the 2-mm cells alone would force
$>$10$\times$ more steps; this is the practical payoff of the implicit scheme.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 4.2 — harmonic vs arithmetic mean]
Two adjacent cells have conductivities $K=1$ and $K=9$ (same width). What face
conductivity does the harmonic mean give, and why is it the physically correct
choice for conduction in series?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
Harmonic mean $=2/(1/1+1/9)=2/(10/9)=1.8$. For layers in series the
\emph{resistances} ($\propto1/K$) add, so the effective conductivity is the
harmonic --- not arithmetic ($5$) --- mean. Using the arithmetic mean would let
heat through the poorly-conducting layer far too easily and bias the deep
gradient that sets $K_d$.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 4.3 — cost of the grid]
The geometric grid has $\sim$69 cells; a uniform 2-mm grid to 5~m would have
2500. Crank--Nicolson cost is $O(N)$ per step (Thomas algorithm). By what factor
is one time step cheaper on the geometric grid?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
Thomas is linear in $N$, so the per-step cost ratio is just $2500/69\approx36\times$.
Add that the uniform grid would also need a smaller step for accuracy near the
surface, and the geometric grid is well over an order of magnitude cheaper for
the same fidelity --- the reason it is used.
\end{solutionbox}

\chapter{Reaching steady state: the old method, the bug, and the fix}\label{ch:steady}
% =====================================================================

This is the most important chapter. It explains the central methodological
contribution of this work: why the original approach to reaching steady state was
flawed, what exactly went wrong, and how the new method fixes it. Read this
chapter carefully; everything in the retrieval (Ch.~\ref{ch:retrieval}) depends
on it.

\section{Why we need steady state at all}\label{sec:whyss}

The Apollo sensors recorded temperatures in regolith that had been sitting
undisturbed for billions of years. The thermal profile at depth is in periodic
steady state: it repeats exactly every lunation. Our model must produce this same
steady state before we can compare it to the data.

$K_d$ is a \emph{steady-state property}: its fingerprint is the settled deep
gradient $d\Tm/dz = Q_b/K_d$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:closure}). A higher $K_d$ means
heat passes more easily, so the gradient is \emph{gentler} (temperature rises
more slowly with depth). A lower $K_d$ means the gradient is \emph{steeper}.
Before the model settles, the deep gradient is still forming and reflects the
arbitrary starting guess, not $K_d$.

\begin{warn}
You cannot read $K_d$ from an unsettled profile. The deep gradient in an
unsettled model is contaminated by the initial condition. It would be like
measuring the insulation of a wall while it is still warming up from one side ---
the temperature slope tells you about the warm-up process, not the insulation.
\end{warn}

\section{The old method: a fixed 30-lunation spin-up}\label{sec:oldmethod}

The original code (before this work) solved the problem by running 30 lunations
of the time-stepping loop (\S\ref{sec:spinup}). Start from a flat temperature
guess (e.g., 250\,K everywhere), step through 30 complete lunar days, and declare
the result ``converged.'' The convergence test checked whether the maximum
temperature change between consecutive lunations was below 0.01\,K.

This seems reasonable: 30 lunations is a month of wall-clock compute. The
convergence test passes. But the result was \textbf{wrong}.

\section{The bug: what actually went wrong (flag F1)}\label{sec:f1}

\textbf{The symptom:} the retrieved $K_d$ changed when we changed the starting
temperature. If we initialised the model at 240\,K everywhere, we got one $K_d$;
at 260\,K, we got a different one. But $K_d$ is a property of the regolith, not
of our guess --- the answer must not depend on where we start.

\textbf{The diagnosis:} the column relaxes to steady state on the \emph{diffusive
timescale}:
\begin{equation}
\tau \sim \frac{z_{\max}^2}{\kappa} \approx \frac{(5\ \text{m})^2}{10^{-8}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}} \approx 2.5\times10^9\ \text{s} \approx \mathbf{1000\ \text{lunations}}.
\end{equation}
\emph{Where this comes from:} balance the heat equation by dimensions --- the
storage term $\partial_t T\sim T/\tau$ on the left against
$\kappa\,\partial_z^2 T\sim\kappa T/L^2$ on the right --- so the time to diffuse
across a length $L$ is $\tau\sim L^2/\kappa$. The quadratic $L^2$ is the whole
story: a thin skin of thickness $\ell\sim20$~cm settles in
$\tau_{\rm skin}\sim\ell^2/\kappa\sim$ a few lunations, but the full 5-m column
takes $(\,25/0.04\,)\approx600$ times longer --- of order $10^3$ lunations.

After 30 lunations, only $\sim$3\% of that relaxation has occurred. The surface
skin (top $\sim$50\,cm) has settled --- it only takes $\sim$10 lunations because
the skin is thin. But the deep column (below $\sim$50\,cm) still holds the
starting guess, essentially untouched.

\textbf{Why the convergence test lied:} the deep drift is so slow --- order of
$10^{-3}$\,K per lunation --- that the cycle-to-cycle change is already below the
0.01\,K threshold while the profile is far from settled. The test measures only
whether progress has \emph{slowed down}, not whether the profile has actually
\emph{arrived}. It's like measuring the deceleration of a car approaching a
traffic light and concluding it has stopped when it's actually still 100 metres
away.

\textbf{The consequence:} the deep sensors at 0.8--2.4\,m sit exactly in the
unsettled zone. Their modelled temperatures are controlled partly by $K_d$ and
partly by the starting guess. When we try to find the $K_d$ that best matches
the data, we are fitting a profile that still remembers our arbitrary starting
temperature. Different starting temperatures lead to different best-fit $K_d$
values. The starting guess has become a hidden free parameter of the retrieval.

\begin{plain}
Think of it this way: the surface skin equilibrates like a shallow puddle ---
quickly. The deep column equilibrates like a lake --- slowly. Thirty lunations is

puddle had settled, said ``yes,'' and assumed the lake had too. It hadn't.
\end{plain}


\section{The fix: the flux-anchored shortcut}\label{sec:newmethod}

The new method avoids waiting for the deep column to relax. Instead of simulating
1000 lunations of heat diffusion, it uses Eq.~\eqref{eq:closure} to
\emph{calculate} the deep profile directly. This section explains it step by
step.

\subsection{The key insight}

Recall the flux closure equation (Eq.~\ref{eq:closure}):
\[
\frac{d\Tm}{dz} = \frac{Q_b}{K(\Tm, z)}.
\]
In steady state, the deep temperature slope at \emph{any} depth is determined by
$Q_b$ (known) and $K$ at that depth (computable once we know the temperature
there). So if we know the temperature at \emph{one} depth below the skin, we can
compute the temperature at the next depth down, and the next, and so on ---
building the entire deep profile without ever simulating the slow diffusive
relaxation.

The one temperature we need is the ``anchor'' --- the cycle-mean temperature at a
depth just below the daily skin, where the skin has already settled.

\subsection{Step A: settle the skin and read the anchor}

A short spin-up (a handful of lunations) is enough to settle the fast surface
skin. After this short run, we read the cycle-mean temperature $\Tm$ at a chosen
\textbf{anchor depth} $z_0$ (0.55\,m in the production code, which is about 3
skin depths --- well below the daily oscillation zone).

\begin{plain}
The skin only needs $\sim$10 lunations to settle because it's thin. We don't
need the deep column to settle yet --- we only need the temperature at 0.55\,m.
\end{plain}

\subsection{Step B: reconstruct the deep profile}\label{sec:reconstruct}

This is the step that replaces waiting 1000 lunations. Starting from the anchor
temperature at 0.55\,m, we ``walk'' downward through the grid, computing the
temperature at each successive depth using the slope rule.

Here is exactly what happens, cell by cell:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=4pt]
\item \textbf{Start at the anchor:} we know $\Tm(z_0)$ from Step~A.
\item \textbf{Compute the slope:} at this depth, $K$ is known (from the Hayne
  formula, using the current temperature), and $Q_b$ is a fixed input. So the
  slope is:
  \[
    \text{slope} = \frac{d\Tm}{dz} = \frac{Q_b}{K(\Tm, z_0)}.
  \]
  Example: with $Q_b = 21$\,mW\,m$^{-2}$ and $K \approx 9$\,mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$
  at depth, the slope is $\sim$2.3\,K/m --- temperature increases by about 2.3\,K
  for each metre deeper.
\item \textbf{Step to the next cell:} the next cell centre is a distance $\Delta z$
  below. The temperature there is:
  \[
    \Tm(z_0 + \Delta z) = \Tm(z_0) + \text{slope} \times \Delta z.
  \]
\item \textbf{Repeat:} move to the new cell, recompute $K$ at the new temperature
  and depth, get the new slope, step to the next cell. Continue all the way to the
  bottom of the column.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/reconstruct_filmstrip.pdf}
\caption{The deep profile reconstruction shown as snapshots. Starting from the
anchor at 0.55\,m (the dot), the code walks downward, computing
$dT/dz = Q_b/K$ at each depth to determine the next temperature.
By the fourth panel the entire deep profile has been built, matching the true
steady state (dashed).}
\label{fig:reconstruct}
\end{figure}

In code (\code{src/lunar/equilibrium.py}, function \code{\_reconstruct\_subskin}):
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
T_new = T_mean.copy()                    # keep the skin as-is from Step A
for i in range(i0, N-1):                 # walk downward from the anchor
    dz = z[i+1] - z[i]                   # thickness of this cell
    q  = Q_b - u_rect[i]                 # u_rect = small correction term
    Ki = K(T_new[i], z[i])               # conductivity at this depth and T
    T_half = T_new[i] + 0.5*q/Ki*dz      # midpoint temperature (for accuracy)
    K_half = K(T_half, 0.5*(z[i]+z[i+1])) # midpoint conductivity
    T_new[i+1] = T_new[i] + q/K_half*dz  # THE STEP: T_next = T_here + slope*dz
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{plain}
This is the part that people find hardest to believe: how can we know the deep
temperature \emph{without simulating} it? The answer is that in steady state,
energy conservation fixes the answer. The same $Q_b$ watts flows through
every layer. At each layer, Fourier's law tells us what temperature gradient is
needed to carry that flux through that layer's conductivity. So we just walk
downward, adding ``slope $\times$ step'' at each cell. The deep profile draws
itself.

It is exactly like calculating the slope of a hillside: if you know the elevation
at one point and the slope at every point (from a map), you can compute the
elevation at any other point by adding up slope~$\times$~distance.
\end{plain}

\subsection{The small correction: rectified flux}
There is one subtlety. Near the skin/deep boundary, the daily temperature
oscillation is small but not zero. The oscillation creates a tiny ``eddy'' heat
flux (called the \textbf{rectified flux}, \code{u\_rect}) that slightly modifies
the mean heat transport. The code subtracts this correction: $q = Q_b - u_{\rm rect}$.
Below about 1\,m, the oscillation is negligible and $u_{\rm rect}\approx0$, so
the slope is simply $Q_b/K$.

\subsection{Iterating Steps A and B}\label{sec:iterate}

Steps A and B are repeated a few times. Why? Because:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item Step A settles the skin assuming the deep column is as we left it.
\item Step B reconstructs the deep column using the anchor from Step A.
\item With the new deep column, Step A produces a slightly different anchor
  (because the boundary between skin and deep has shifted a bit).
\item Step B then gives a slightly different deep profile.
\end{itemize}
But each round the changes are smaller. After 3--5 rounds, the anchor temperature
moves by less than 0.005\,K between iterations --- we declare convergence.

In code (\code{src/lunar/equilibrium.py}, function \code{solve\_periodic\_equilibrium}):
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
stages = [
    dict(z0=0.25, n_in=4,  max_it=4,  tol=0.10),   # coarse, fast
    dict(z0=0.55, n_in=12, max_it=20, tol=0.005),   # fine, accurate
]
T_init = np.full(N, T_guess)                        # arbitrary starting guess

for stage in stages:
    anchor = find_cell_nearest(z, stage.z0)
    prev = infinity
    for it in range(stage.max_iters):
        out = solve_pixel(T_init, n_lunations=stage.n_in)  # STEP A: settle skin
        T_mean = out.T.mean(axis=1)                         # cycle-mean profile
        u = rectified_flux(out.T, z, K)                     # eddy correction
        T_recon = reconstruct(T_mean, anchor, Q_b, K, u)    # STEP B: deep profile
        drift = abs(T_mean[anchor] - prev)
        if drift < stage.tol and it >= 2:
            break                                            # converged!
        prev = T_mean[anchor]
        T_init = T_recon                        # feed back into next Step A
\end{lstlisting}

The two-stage schedule (first anchor at 0.25\,m, then at 0.55\,m) is an
optimisation: the shallow anchor converges fast and gets the profile into the
right ballpark; the deeper anchor then refines it to high accuracy.

\section{Proof: they give the same answer}\label{sec:agree}

The decisive test has two parts.

\textbf{Part 1 --- the old method is guess-dependent.}
We ran the old brute-force method (30 lunations) from two starting guesses:
250\,K and 200\,K. The two resulting profiles \emph{disagree} at sensor depths by
$\sim$40\,K (Fig.~\ref{fig:overlay}a). The answer you get depends on where you
start --- a sign that 30 lunations is nowhere near enough.

\textbf{Part 2 --- the new method is guess-independent.}
We ran the new flux-anchored method from three wildly different starting
temperatures: 200\,K, 250\,K, and 300\,K. All three produce the \emph{same}
profile to within 21\,mK at sensor depths (Fig.~\ref{fig:overlay}b,c). Different
starting points, same destination.

Why does the old method need so long? Fig.~\ref{fig:conv} shows brute-force error
at sensor depths falling as a power law with lunation count; at 30 lunations, the
error is still $\sim$13\,K. The deep column's thermal timescale is $\tau \sim
10^{3}$ lunations --- the old method was waiting for heat to diffuse; the new
method imposes the answer directly via $dT/dz = Q_b/K$.

\section{Self-certification: how we know each solve is correct}\label{sec:cert}

Every call to \code{solve\_periodic\_equilibrium} returns two diagnostic numbers:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Anchor drift:} how much the anchor temperature changed between the
  last two iterations. Must be $<0.03$\,K; typically 3\,mK ($0.003$\,K). This
  proves the iteration has converged.
\item \textbf{Flux closure:} the maximum relative deviation of the mean flux from
  $Q_b$ at all depths below the anchor:
  $\max\bigl|\langle q\rangle(z) - Q_b\bigr|/Q_b$. Must be $<2\%$. This proves
  the profile satisfies energy conservation.
\end{enumerate}

If either check fails, the code signals that the solve did not converge. This is
exactly the verification the old fixed spin-up could never provide.

\caption{Guess-independence and flux closure --- the returned proof that a solve
reached steady state. Different starting guesses produce the same answer (left);

\section{Practice problems}\label{sec:prob-steady}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 5.1 — skin vs column settling time]
Using $\tau\sim L^2/\kappa$ with $\kappa\approx10^{-8}$~m$^2$\,s$^{-1}$, estimate
how many lunations it takes to settle the top $0.5$~m versus the full $5$~m
column. (One lunation $\approx2.55\times10^6$~s.)
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
$\tau(0.5)=0.5^2/10^{-8}=2.5\times10^{7}$~s $\approx10$ lunations;
$\tau(5)=5^2/10^{-8}=2.5\times10^{9}$~s $\approx1000$ lunations. The skin settles
$\sim$100$\times$ faster than the deep column --- exactly why a 30-lunation
spin-up looks ``converged'' (the skin has stopped moving) while the deep profile
is still 97\% controlled by the starting guess.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 5.2 — what the shortcut guarantees]
The flux-anchored method runs $\sim$150 lunations total, versus $10^3{+}$ for
brute force. Beyond being faster, what does it \emph{guarantee} that a fixed
30-lunation spin-up cannot?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
A \emph{certified, guess-independent} steady state. The reconstruction imposes
$d\Tm/dz=Q_b/K$ directly, and the solver returns two checks --- the anchor drift
(iteration converged, $<0.03$~K) and the flux closure (energy conserved, the mean
flux equals $Q_b$ to $<2\%$). A 30-lunation run provides neither: its deep profile
still depends on the arbitrary starting temperature, so the retrieved $K_d$ does too.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 5.3 — error propagation in the walk]
The reconstruction steps downward with $T_{i+1}=T_i+(Q_b/K_i)\,\Delta z$. If the
conductivity $K_i$ used at one step is 5\% too high, what happens to that step's
temperature increment, and why does the iteration still converge?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
Since the increment is $\propto Q_b/K$, a $+5\%$ error in $K$ makes that step's
$\Delta T$ about $5\%$ too small. But $K$ is recomputed from the updated
temperature at every step and every outer iteration, so a biased profile feeds
back a corrected $K$ next round; the flux-closure diagnostic ($<2\%$) is what
certifies the accumulated error has been driven down. This self-correction is why
the method needs only a handful of outer iterations.
\end{solutionbox}

% =====================================================================
\chapter{Retrieving \texorpdfstring{$K_d$}{Kd} from the data}\label{ch:retrieval}\label{sec:retrieval}
% =====================================================================

Now we have a solver that produces a correct, certified steady-state profile for
any trial value of $K_d$. The retrieval is conceptually simple: try many $K_d$
values, compare each to the data, and pick the best one.

\section{One forward run: \texttt{run\_with}}
The hub of the pipeline turns one trial $K_d$ into one settled profile. This one
function connects the grid, the conductivity model, the solver, and the
equilibrium driver:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
def run_with(site, kd):
    grid  = make_geometric_grid(**GRID)           # 69-cell geometric grid
    t     = np.arange(0, T_LUNAR, DT_STEP)       # one lunation of hourly steps
    insol = (1 - site.albedo) * S0 * cos(...)     # sunlight forcing
    K     = lambda T, z: conductivity_hayne(T, z, Kd=kd)  # Hayne with this K_d
    eq    = solve_periodic_equilibrium(
                grid, t, insol,
                Q_b=site.Q_b, K_func=K, ...)      # -> certified steady state
    return grid.z_mid, eq.T_mean                  # depths and settled mean profile
\end{lstlisting}

Each call to \code{run\_with} takes about 9 seconds (the new equilibrium solver).
It returns the mean temperature at every depth in the grid.

\section{The sweep and the minimum}\label{sec:sweep}
We call \code{run\_with} for a grid of $K_d$ values (28 values for Apollo~15,
spanning $1$--$15$ mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$; 30 for Apollo~17, spanning $3$--$25$).
For each $K_d$, we measure the misfit between the model's deep temperatures and
the Apollo sensor measurements:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE}(K_d)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl[\Tm(z_i;K_d)-T^{\rm obs}_i\bigr]^2},
\label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over the $N$ deep sensors ($N=7$ for A15, $N=16$ for A17).

The best-fit $K_d^{*}$ is the value that minimises this RMSE. We refine the
minimum with a parabolic fit through the three points around the grid minimum:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
rmse    = np.sqrt((residuals**2).mean(axis=0))  # RMSE at each trial K_d
k       = np.argmin(rmse)                        # nearest grid minimum
kd_star = parabola_vertex(kd_grid[k-1:k+2],     # sub-grid refinement
                          rmse[k-1:k+2])
\end{lstlisting}

The vertex of a parabola through three equally-spaced points
$(y_-,\,y_0,\,y_+)$ at grid spacing $\Delta$ sits at
\begin{equation}
K_d^{*}=K_d^{(k)}+\frac{\Delta}{2}\,\frac{y_--y_+}{y_--2y_0+y_+},
\label{eq:vertex}
\end{equation}
which pins the minimum to far finer than the grid spacing without any extra
(expensive) solver runs. Each site shows a clean single minimum
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}). The curves are smooth because the solver is deterministic
and the steady state is unique for each $K_d$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/fig_kd_sweep.pdf}
\caption{Deep-sensor RMSE vs.\ trial $K_d$. The minimum is $K_d^{*}$; Apollo~17's
sits at a clearly higher conductivity than Apollo~15's.}
\label{fig:sweep}
\end{figure}

\section{The result}
\[
K_{d,\mathrm{A15}}^{*}=4.58,\qquad K_{d,\mathrm{A17}}^{*}=8.12\quad
\mathrm{mW\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}.
\]
Apollo~17's deep regolith conducts heat about twice as well as Apollo~15's. Both
are higher than the global literature value of 3.4.

\begin{plain}
\textbf{Reconciling our per-site $K_d^{*}$ with Hayne's global 3.4.}
Hayne~(2017) fits one number to the full Moon, weighting maria and highlands by
their global surface coverage ($\sim$17\% mare, $\sim$83\% highland from LROC
mosaic mapping; e.g.\ Speyerer et al.\ 2011). Applying the same weighting to our
two per-site retrievals gives an area-weighted mean
\[
\bar K_d \;=\; 0.17\,(4.58) + 0.83\,(8.12) \;\approx\; 7.5\;\mathrm{mW\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}},
\]
roughly twice Hayne's $3.4$. The discrepancy is real and pulls in the direction
the rest of this study points: \emph{both} of our sites are more conductive
than Hayne's global fit predicts. The likely reasons are the ones we list in
Ch.~\ref{ch:sites} (compaction, rock content at depth) plus the calibration
choice for $\chi$ that §\ref{sec:chi-conditionality} flags. Two points are not
enough to overturn a global fit derived from continuous Diviner coverage; they
are enough to show that the local in-situ signal can sit a factor of 2 above
the global average, and that any future heat-flow probe will see a similar
spread.
\end{plain}

With these retrieved values, the modelled deep temperatures track the data far
better than the one-size-fits-all value (Fig.~\ref{fig:profiles}); at Apollo~17
the misfit roughly halves.

\section{Practice problems}\label{sec:prob-retrieval}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 6.1 — why a parabola?]
Why refine the minimum with a parabola through the three lowest points, rather
than simply reporting the grid $K_d$ with the smallest RMSE?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
The true minimum almost never lands exactly on a grid node; it lies somewhere
between them. The RMSE curve is smooth and locally quadratic near its bottom, so a
parabola through the three lowest points locates the sub-grid vertex --- giving
$K_d^*$ to much better than the grid spacing --- \emph{without} running the
(9-second) solver at extra $K_d$ values.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 6.2 — the cost of the bootstrap]
The A15 sweep runs the solver at 28 values of $K_d$ ($\sim$9~s each). The bootstrap
re-fits $K_d^*$ on 1500 resampled sensor sets. Why is the bootstrap \emph{not}
$1500\times$ the cost of one sweep?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
Resampling the \emph{sensors} does not change the forward model: the settled
profile $T(z;K_d)$ for each trial $K_d$ is the same regardless of which sensors we
compare against. So the 28 profiles are computed \emph{once} (and cached); each
bootstrap resample only re-selects which residuals enter the RMSE and re-locates
the minimum --- arithmetic, not solving. The 1500 resamples therefore add seconds,
not hours. (This is exactly why the code separates \code{run\_with} from
\code{kd\_star\_from\_residuals}.)
\end{solutionbox}

% =====================================================================
\chapter{How sure are we? (uncertainty)}\label{ch:stats}
% =====================================================================

A point estimate ($K_d^*=4.58$) is not useful without knowing how uncertain it
is. We quantify uncertainty three ways.

\section{The bootstrap}\label{sec:bootstrap}
The bootstrap is a resampling technique that does not assume any particular noise
model. The procedure:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Resample the sensors:} draw $N$ sensors from the original $N$
  \emph{with replacement} (some sensors appear twice, others not at all). Also
  jitter each sensor's depth by a random amount drawn from
  $\mathcal{N}(0,\,2.5\,\text{cm})$ to account for the uncertainty in exactly
  where each sensor is positioned (Nagihara 2018).
\item \textbf{Re-fit $K_d^*$:} run the full sweep on this resampled sensor set.
\item \textbf{Repeat $N_{\rm boot}=1500$ times.}
\item \textbf{Read the spread:} the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the 1500
  $K_d^*$ values give the 95\% confidence interval.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Why resampling works.} We do not know the true distribution of the sensor
noise --- but the observed sensors \emph{are} a sample drawn from it. The
bootstrap's plug-in principle treats that sample as a stand-in for the
population: drawing $N$ sensors with replacement mimics ``going back and
collecting another $N$ sensors'' without assuming any noise shape. Re-fitting
$K_d^*$ on each resample maps the sensor scatter into scatter in $K_d^*$, and the
percentiles of the $1500$ fits are then a distribution-free confidence interval.
Jittering each depth by $\mathcal{N}(0,2.5\,\mathrm{cm})$ folds the positional
uncertainty (Nagihara 2018) into the very same loop.

\caption{Bootstrap distributions of $K_d^{*}$ (1500 resamples). The two site
distributions barely overlap --- the contrast is statistically real

\section{The full error budget}\label{sec:errorbudget}
The bootstrap captures the \emph{statistical} scatter from the finite, noisy
sensor set. Several \emph{systematic} inputs also carry uncertainty. We perturb
each one in turn, re-retrieve $K_d$, and record the induced shift; combining the
independent contributions in quadrature gives the total 1$\sigma$ budget
(\code{pipeline/compute/compute\_error\_budget.py}, written to
\code{results/kd\_error\_budget.json}).

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
Contribution to $\sigma_{K_d}$ (mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$) & Apollo 15 & Apollo 17 \\
\midrule
Statistical (bootstrap)        & 0.80 & 0.55 \\
Basal flux $Q_b$               & 1.20 & 2.16 \\
Albedo $A$                     & 0.80 & 3.49 \\
Surface conductivity $K_s$     & 0.53 & 1.96 \\
Density $\rho$                 & 0.01 & 0.42 \\
Sensor depth (borestem)        & 0.15 & 0.00 \\
Stability threshold            & 0.01 & 0.03 \\
Solver / numerical             & 0.15 & 0.15 \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total (in quadrature)} & \textbf{1.75} & \textbf{4.61} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{How each $\sigma$ is generated}
Each row of the table is the half-range of a re-retrieval at perturbed inputs,
not a guess. Specifically:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item $\sigma_{\rm stat}$: half-width of the 16th--84th percentile of the 1500
  bootstrap $K_d^{*}$ values (\code{kd\_retrieval\_results.json}).
\item $\sigma_{Q_b}$: $\sigma_{Q_b}=K_d^{*}\,(Q_b^{\rm high}-Q_b^{\rm low})/(2\,Q_b^{\rm nom})$,
  with the published $Q_b$ envelopes
  ($14$--$25$~mW\,m$^{-2}$ at A15, $10$--$18$ at A17 from Langseth 1976 / Saito
  2007 / Nagihara 2018). This is the analytical degeneracy-line slope evaluated
  at the data --- no extra forward solves needed.
\item $\sigma_A$: re-run the full retrieval at $A\to A\pm 0.01$ (the Vasavada
  2012 lat-band scatter); record the half-range of the resulting $K_d^{*}$
  (\code{compute\_surface\_bias\_test.py}). The $\pm 0.01$ envelope is the
  largest perturbation that keeps the misfit bowl monotonic; beyond that the
  retrieval flips between RMSE branches.
\item $\sigma_{K_s}$: re-run with the surface conductivity at $\pm 10\%$ of its
  Hayne~2017 value (\code{compute\_fixed\_input\_sensitivities.py}).
\item $\sigma_\rho$: re-run with the deep density at $\pm 5\%$ of its Hayne~2017
  asymptote.
\item $\sigma_{z_b}$: half-range over the borestem-cut sweep at
  $\{70, 80, 90\}$~cm (\code{compute\_borestem\_sensitivity.py}); the 60-cm cut
  is included as a diagnostic but excluded from the budget (it readmits a
  borestem-contaminated sensor at A17).
\item $\sigma_{\rm thr}$: half-range over the stability-window flatness threshold
  sweep at $\pm 50\%$ of the nominal $0.08$~K\,yr$^{-1}$
  (\code{compute\_stability\_threshold\_sensitivity.py}).
\item $\sigma_{\rm solver}$: an upper-bound systematic from the equilibrium
  certification --- the $\le 0.08$~K mean-temperature drift at sensor depths
  over a 120-lunation honest run, times the measured $dK_d^{*}/dT$ response of
  $\sim$1.5--2~mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ per K.
\end{itemize}
\noindent Each is recomputed end-to-end by \code{make aux}; the totals in the
table are $\sqrt{\sum_i \sigma_i^2}$. The $\chi$ and $H$ sensitivities are
reported separately as \emph{conditional} terms
(\S\ref{sec:chi-conditionality}), not in the quadrature, because they
parametrise the Hayne form we adopted.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Two lessons.} First, the basal flux $Q_b$ and the albedo $A$
dominate the systematic budget --- $Q_b$ is the single largest term, consistent
with the $K_d$--$Q_b$ degeneracy of Ch.~\ref{ch:qb}. Second, Apollo~17's budget
is about $2.6\times$ wider than Apollo~15's, chiefly through albedo and $K_s$,
because its higher retrieved $K_d$ amplifies the same fractional input
uncertainties.

\section{The largest weakness: conditionality on \texorpdfstring{$\chi$}{chi}}\label{sec:chi-conditionality}
\noindent The error budget above is honest about what \emph{varies} in the
published sources. But the largest single sensitivity in the analysis is not in
that budget at all --- it is the dependence on the radiative coefficient $\chi$
in Hayne's $K(T,z)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:Khayne}).

\begin{warn}
$\chi$ is held fixed at $2.7$ throughout this work because Hayne~(2017) reports
that value as the best global Diviner fit. \emph{But} an alternative published
calibration of the same radiative term --- Vasavada et al.~(2012) --- prefers
$\chi=1.48$. The two numbers describe the same physical effect, evaluated against
different orbital data. Plugging $\chi=1.48$ into our retrieval (with everything
else held fixed) drives the minimum to:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
 & At Hayne's $\chi=2.7$ & At Vasavada's $\chi=1.48$ \\
\midrule
$K_d^{*}$ (Apollo 15) & 4.58 & $\approx 1.5$ \\
$K_d^{*}$ (Apollo 17) & 8.12 & $\approx 22.0$ \\
implied $\sigma_\chi$ (A15 / A17) & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$3.1$ / $13.9$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent\textbf{This is a far larger swing than the quadrature totals
($1.75$ and $4.61$).} The absolute $K_d^{*}$ values are \emph{conditional} on
which radiative calibration the reader accepts.
\end{warn}

\emph{Why we still report them as headline numbers.} Three reasons. \emph{First},
$\chi$ is not a free parameter we are entitled to vary --- it is one knob of the
published Hayne form we adopted, and that form is what makes Hayne's $K_d=3.4$
directly comparable to our per-site retrievals. \emph{Second}, the direction of
the inter-site contrast survives: Apollo~17 ends up \emph{more} conductive than
Apollo~15 at \emph{both} $\chi$ choices (the contrast is $+3.5$ at Hayne and
$+20$ at Vasavada). And \emph{third}, the same conditionality applies to every
$K_d$ in the literature: Hayne's global $3.4$ assumes $\chi=2.7$ too. Quoting
$K_d$ along with $\chi$ is the only honest convention.

\begin{plain}
Bottom line. If a reviewer asks ``what is the real $K_d$ at Apollo 15?'', the
honest answer is: ``$4.58$ mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ \emph{if you accept Hayne's
$\chi=2.7$}; closer to $1.5$ if you accept Vasavada's $\chi=1.48$''. The
contrast between sites is robust, and is the headline finding; the absolute
numbers ride on which radiative calibration you trust.
\end{plain}

\section{Bayesian cross-check (MCMC)}\label{sec:mcmc}
An independent check: instead of sweeping $K_d$ on a grid, we run a Markov-chain
Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler that explores the full joint posterior in
$(K_d, Q_b)$ --- allowing \emph{both} parameters to vary, not just $K_d$. The
prior is uniform on $K_d\in[1,30]$\;mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ and Gaussian on
$Q_b$ at the Langseth value with width $\sigma_{Q_b}$ from §\ref{sec:errorbudget}.
The likelihood is the Gaussian misfit between $\langle T\rangle(z;K_d,Q_b)$ and
the deep-sensor data. The intuition is just Bayes' rule (Fig.~\ref{fig:mcmc}):
prior beliefs times what the data say, normalised.

\section{Model selection (AICc)}\label{sec:aicc}
We compare three models:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item $M_1$: different $K_d$ per site, $Q_b$ fixed (our model, 2 free parameters);
\item $M_2$: one shared $K_d$, different $Q_b$ per site (3 free parameters);
\item $M_3$: one shared $K_d$, $Q_b$ fixed (1 free parameter).
\end{itemize}
AICc (Akaike Information Criterion, corrected for small samples) penalises models
for using more parameters. For $N$ data points, $k$ free parameters, and Gaussian
residuals,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AICc}=N\ln\big(\widehat{\mathrm{RMSE}}^{\,2}\big)+2k
              +\frac{2k(k+1)}{N-k-1},
\end{equation}
where the first term rewards fit, the $2k$ term penalises complexity, and the last
is the small-sample correction (it matters here, with $N=23$ sensors). Only
\emph{differences} between models are meaningful: a gap $\Delta\mathrm{AICc}\gtrsim2$
is notable, $\gtrsim4$ strong. The per-site model $M_1$ beats the shared-$K_d$,
free-$Q_b$ model $M_2$ by $\Delta\text{AICc}=3.99$ and the strict shared-$K_d$,
fixed-$Q_b$ null $M_3$ by $2.71$ --- the data favour a genuine
\emph{conductivity} contrast, not a flux artefact, even after paying for the
extra parameter.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_book_aicc.pdf}

\section{Practice problems}\label{sec:prob-stats}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 7.1 — reading a $p$-value]
The inter-site contrast has $p\approx0.011$. State precisely what that number
means --- and what it does \emph{not} mean.
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
It means: \emph{if} the two sites truly had the same $K_d$, the probability of
seeing a contrast at least as large as the observed one, from sensor noise alone,
is about $1.1\%$. It is \emph{not} the probability that the sites are the same, and
not the probability that our result is correct --- it is a statement about how
surprising the data would be under the ``no difference'' hypothesis.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 7.2 — why not just minimise RMSE?]
Model $M_1$ (per-site $K_d$, 2 parameters) always fits at least as well as $M_2$
(shared $K_d$, 1 parameter). So why can't we just pick the model with the lower
RMSE?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
Because adding parameters can only \emph{lower} RMSE (a free parameter can always
be tuned to fit noise), so RMSE alone would always favour the more complex model
--- that is overfitting. AICc adds a complexity penalty ($+2k$); $M_1$ wins only
because its RMSE gain \emph{exceeds} the cost of its extra parameter
($\Delta\mathrm{AICc}=3.99$). That is the whole point of model selection.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 7.3 — an asymmetric interval]
The A15 bootstrap CI is $[4.12,\,7.45]$ around $K_d^*=4.58$ --- much longer on the
high side. What feature of the retrieval produces this skew?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
The misfit ``bowl'' RMSE$(K_d)$ is asymmetric: it rises steeply on the low-$K_d$
side but is flatter on the high-$K_d$ side (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}). A resampled
sensor set can therefore push the minimum to higher $K_d$ more easily than to
lower, so the mapping from symmetric sensor noise to $K_d^*$ is skewed --- giving a
longer upper tail. The point estimate is well-constrained from below, less so from
above.
\end{solutionbox}

% =====================================================================
\chapter{The role of the basal flux \texorpdfstring{$Q_b$}{Qb}}\label{ch:qb}\label{sec:qb}
% =====================================================================

The bootstrap CI of Ch.~\ref{ch:stats} tells us how scattered $K_d^{*}$ is
\emph{at the fixed published $Q_b$}. But $Q_b$ itself was measured, and it
carries its own uncertainty. This chapter takes the next layer off the onion:
what happens to $K_d^{*}$ when we let $Q_b$ move. The short answer ---
$K_d$ and $Q_b$ trade off along a line and we cannot separate them from one
borehole alone --- sets the strongest honest caveat on the headline numbers.

\section{The degeneracy: $K_d$ and $Q_b$ trade off}
The deep gradient is $Q_b/K$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:closure}). For the \emph{same} set of
deep temperatures, you could explain the observed gradient with:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item high $Q_b$ + high $K_d$ (strong flux, easily conducted), or
\item low $Q_b$ + low $K_d$ (weak flux, poorly conducted).
\end{itemize}
Many combinations along a diagonal in $(K_d, Q_b)$ space fit the data almost
equally well. This is the \textbf{$K_d$--$Q_b$ degeneracy}. We cannot separate
the two from a single borehole's temperature measurements alone.

\section{What we do about it}
We \emph{fix} $Q_b$ at the published values ($21$ mW\,m$^{-2}$ for A15, $15$ for
A17) and report $K_d$ \emph{conditional} on them. This is honest: we are not
claiming to have measured $K_d$ independently of $Q_b$. The headline number
$K_d^*=4.58$ means ``the $K_d$ that best fits the data \emph{given} $Q_b=21$.''

\section{An honest concern: where do the $Q_b$ values come from?}\label{sec:qb-circularity}
The published values we hold fixed --- $21$ and $15$ mW\,m$^{-2}$ from Langseth
et al.~(1976) --- were themselves derived from the \emph{same} HFE thermometers
we are now fitting. Doesn't this make the whole exercise circular?

It would be, if Langseth used the same model we do. He didn't. Langseth's deep-flux
analysis used a \emph{constant} (depth- and temperature-independent) conductivity
fitted to long-term-mean temperature differences at adjacent deep sensors, not a
forward model of the whole profile. The two procedures are sensitive to different
slices of the data: Langseth's $Q_b$ comes from local gradient differences
between bracketing deep sensors; our $K_d^{*}$ comes from matching the
\emph{absolute} temperatures across all deep sensors against a temperature-
and depth-dependent $K(T,z)$. They share data but not formulation. Still:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item It would be cleaner to re-derive $Q_b$ self-consistently with our $K(T,z)$
  --- a future project, and the point of the joint MCMC (\S\ref{sec:mcmc}).
\item The fact that the bootstrap CIs envelope the Saito (2007) and Nagihara
  (2018) re-analyses, which used different methods, is reassuring but not
  decisive.
\end{itemize}

\section{The robust claim}
Even though the absolute $K_d$ values shift when $Q_b$ changes, the
\emph{direction} of the contrast survives: Apollo~17 remains more conductive than
Apollo~15 for all plausible $Q_b$ values ($\sim$83\% posterior probability from
the MCMC). $Q_b$ is one of the largest terms in the error budget --- a direct
fingerprint of this coupling.

\section{Practice problems}\label{sec:prob-qb}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 8.1 — sliding the degeneracy diagonal]
The retrieval at Apollo~15 finds $K_d^{*}=4.58$ at $Q_b=21$~mW\,m$^{-2}$. If a
future re-analysis revised Apollo~15's $Q_b$ down to $18$~mW\,m$^{-2}$ (about
$-14\%$), what would the corresponding $K_d^{*}$ be? Use the closure relation
$d\Tm/dz \propto Q_b/K_d$ and assume the deep gradient the data demand is fixed.
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
The closure says $K_d \propto Q_b$ for a fixed observed gradient, so a $-14\%$
shift in $Q_b$ drags $K_d^{*}$ down by the same fraction:
$K_d^{*}\to 4.58\times(18/21)\approx 3.92$ mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$. The
inter-site \emph{contrast}, in contrast, is approximately preserved because the
$Q_b/K_d$ ratio at A17 would shift by a similar factor --- which is exactly the
$\sim$83\% posterior-direction-survival result from the MCMC.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 8.2 — why one borehole cannot break the degeneracy]
A graduate student proposes: ``Just measure $K_d$ and $Q_b$ both from the data --
the misfit surface should pick a single $(K_d, Q_b)$.'' Explain why this fails
at \emph{one} borehole but might succeed if you had two probes at very different
depths at the same site.
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
At one borehole the deep mean temperatures are sensitive almost only to the
\emph{ratio} $Q_b/K_d$, not the two values separately, because the closure
$d\Tm/dz = Q_b/K(\Tm,z)$ depends on that ratio. Move along the
$Q_b\propto K_d$ diagonal and you sweep through fits of essentially equal
quality. Two probes at different depths could in principle break the
degeneracy because they probe \emph{different} parts of $K(\Tm,z)$ --- but the
real Apollo probes are at similar enough depths (and the $T$ range is narrow)
that the radiative term doesn't differentiate them enough. That is why the
field needs independent measurements of either $Q_b$ (e.g.\ from drilling deeper)
or $K(T,z)$ (e.g.\ in-situ probes like ChaSTE) to truly close the loop.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{plain}
If someone challenges your $K_d$ by saying ``but what if $Q_b$ is different?'' ---
acknowledge it: ``yes, $K_d$ rides on $Q_b$. If Apollo~15's $Q_b$ were higher,
our $K_d$ would be higher too. The robust finding is not the absolute number but
the direction: Apollo~17 is more conductive than Apollo~15. That holds regardless
of reasonable $Q_b$ assumptions.''
\end{plain}

\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/fig_kd_qb_posterior.pdf}
\caption{The $K_d$--$Q_b$ trade-off: many combinations along a diagonal fit
almost equally well, so $K_d$ is conditional on $Q_b$. The contrast between sites
is more robust than either absolute value.}
\label{fig:qb}
\end{figure}

\chapter{Why might the two sites differ?}\label{ch:sites}\label{sec:sites}
% =====================================================================
So far the contrast has been a statistical fact and a methodological caveat.
This chapter asks whether it is also \emph{physically plausible}: do the two
landing sites sample regolith you would expect to conduct heat differently?
A factor-of-two contrast is large but physically reasonable --- the two
boreholes sample genuinely different regolith, and the directions of every
relevant indicator agree with the conductivity ordering.

Heat moves through dry regolith mainly by grain-to-grain contact, so a higher
$K_d$ points to \emph{denser, more compacted, or more rock-rich} deep regolith.
The two sites genuinely sample different geology, and the contrast we observe is
consistent with their independently-known properties.

\section{Apollo 15 --- Hadley Rille (mare basalt)}
The HFE at Apollo~15 was deployed on the \emph{mare} side of the
mare/highland boundary at Hadley Rille, on the floor of Mare Imbrium. The
underlying bedrock is mare basalt --- the iron-rich lavas that filled the
Imbrium basin between $\sim$3.85 and $\sim$3.2 Ga ago. Surface samples from the
Apollo~15 traverses are dominated by mare-basalt fragments mixed with anorthositic
debris that fell back from the Apennine front. The regolith here is relatively
\emph{mature} --- well-comminuted by billions of years of impact gardening, with
a substantial agglutinate component.

Three relevant consequences:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Grain size:} fine, well-mixed; few large blocks at depth.
\item \textbf{Density:} the Hayne (2017) density profile saturates at
  $\rho_d\approx1800$~kg\,m$^{-3}$, consistent with mature mare regolith.
\item \textbf{Contact area:} the well-comminuted grain population packs to
  modest deep contact area --- a modest $K_d$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Apollo 17 --- Taurus-Littrow (highland + dark mantle)}
Apollo~17 sits in the Taurus-Littrow valley, a narrow embayment in the
highland terrain east of Mare Serenitatis. The valley floor sits between
$\sim$2000-m high massifs (North and South Massifs) of \emph{anorthositic
norite} --- ejecta and uplifted material from the Serenitatis basin.

What makes this site special for our purposes is the \emph{dark-mantle deposit}
that drapes much of the valley floor, identified at Shorty Crater and elsewhere.
The dark mantle is interpreted as pyroclastic \emph{orange and black glass beads}
($\sim$3.5 Ga eruption from a deep, primitive source), deposited as a fire-fountain
spray. Three properties matter:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Less mature:} relatively young, less agglutinate development.
\item \textbf{Denser grains:} the glass beads are
  $\sim$2.8--3.0~g\,cm$^{-3}$ vs. $\sim$2.9 for mare basalt --- comparable
  intrinsic density but very different packing geometry.
\item \textbf{Buried block component:} the Taurus-Littrow regolith carries a
  larger fraction of buried ejecta blocks (highland breccias and basalts shed
  from the surrounding massifs); blocks couple thermally far better than dust.
\end{itemize}

\section{Three plausible contributors to the factor-of-two contrast}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Material:} highland breccia + pyroclastic glass + buried massif
  blocks at A17, versus comminuted mare basalt at A15. Greater compositional
  diversity raises the chance of well-conducting components in the deep mix.
\item \textbf{Independent check from Martínez:} re-running the retrieval with
  the density-based Martínez \& Siegler (2021) form prefers ordinary effective
  density scalings, $\alpha\approx 1.13$ at A15 and $\alpha\approx 0.98$ at A17
  (Ch.~\ref{ch:crosschecks}). That check does \emph{not} reproduce the Hayne
  $K_d$ ordering by density alone; it says the A17 contrast should be read as
  contact-network or block-rich structure, not merely as a bulk-density proxy.
\item \textbf{Compaction history:} the Taurus-Littrow regolith is thinner and
  less mature; the deep column likely contains a larger fraction of in-situ
  highland blocks, which contact more grain-to-grain area per unit volume than
  mature comminuted dust.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What this is not}
It is \emph{not} a temperature effect: the deep mean temperatures at A15 and A17
differ by less than a few K, far too little to explain a factor-of-two via the
$T^3$ radiative term alone. It is \emph{not} a basal flux effect: $Q_b$ is
\emph{lower} at A17 ($15$ vs $21$~mW\,m$^{-2}$), so the inter-site contrast in
$K_d$ is, if anything, \emph{enhanced} when you correct for $Q_b$ (the deep
gradient is smaller at A17 \emph{despite} the bigger conductivity --- because
the heat going through is smaller too). It is \emph{not} a measurement artefact:
the contrast survives every robustness check (Ch.~\ref{ch:crosschecks}).

\begin{plain}
Honest framing. Apollo~17's deep regolith conducts heat about twice as
efficiently as Apollo~15's, conditional on the published basal fluxes and the
Hayne shape parameters. The most likely physical explanation: Taurus-Littrow's
deeper layers contain a larger fraction of buried ejecta blocks and dark-mantle
pyroclastics, both of which couple thermally better than Hadley Rille's mature
mare-basalt fines.
\end{plain}

\section{Practice problems}\label{sec:prob-sites}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 9.1 — temperature is not the explanation]
The deep mean temperature at Apollo~17 ($\sim$256~K) is slightly \emph{warmer}
than at Apollo~15 ($\sim$253~K). Could the inter-site $K$ contrast be a
temperature effect (via the radiative $T^3$ term) rather than a regolith-density
effect?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
Compute the radiative multiplier at both temperatures:
$(T/T_{\rm ref})^3 = (253/350)^3 \approx 0.378$ at A15; $(256/350)^3\approx
0.392$ at A17. With $\chi=2.7$ the bracket
$[1+\chi(T/T_{\rm ref})^3]$ shifts only $\sim$2\% between the two sites
(from $\sim$2.02 to $\sim$2.06). That is \emph{vastly} smaller than the
$\sim 1.8\times$ contrast we observe in $K_d^{*}$. The temperature difference
can account for at most a couple of percent of the contrast --- not the bulk.
The remainder must come from genuinely different bulk material.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 9.2 — what would falsify the geology story?]
Suppose a future LRO Diviner thermal-inertia map of Hadley Rille and
Taurus-Littrow showed identical thermal inertia at the two sites. How would
that affect the geological interpretation of the $K_d$ contrast?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
Thermal inertia $I=\sqrt{K\rho c_p}$ is sensitive to roughly the top
$\sim$1 skin depth (a few cm), not the deep column. Identical surface thermal
inertia tells us the \emph{near-surface} packings and bulk densities are
similar between the sites. That would not falsify the $K_d$ contrast --- our
$K_d$ probes the column below 80~cm, an entirely different layer. But it would
sharpen the story: the contrast must originate from a layer that orbital
thermal inertia cannot see. Conversely, if Diviner showed a clear thermal-inertia
contrast at the surface, that would be additional support that the difference
extends to the surface.
\end{solutionbox}

\chapter{Independent cross-checks}\label{ch:crosschecks}
% =====================================================================

A retrieved $K_d$ that fits seven deep thermometers could still be wrong if
the surrounding physics is mis-specified. We therefore test the result against
\emph{two} entirely independent data sources, then add a battery of robustness
checks against our own assumptions.

\section{Diviner surface closure}
\subsubsection*{What it tests}
Whether the same conductivity that explains the \emph{deep, steady} HFE
temperatures also produces a sensible \emph{surface, daily} temperature swing.
The deep sensors and the surface diurnal wave are sensitive to disjoint physics
(deep gradient $\propto Q_b/K$; surface swing $\propto K$ via skin depth), so a
$K_d$ tuned to one need not fit the other.

\subsubsection*{How it works}
LRO's Diviner radiometer (Vasavada et al.\ 2012) has measured the bolometric
brightness temperature of the lunar surface continuously since 2009. Its
near-global cumulative product (GCP) bins the record by local solar time and
latitude. For each site we extract Diviner's mean diurnal curve in the
corresponding lat-band and compare it against the surface temperature $T_s(t)$
returned by our forward solver at the retrieved $K_d^{*}$
(\code{pipeline/compute/compute\_diviner\_closure.py}).

\subsubsection*{What we find}
The model surface diurnal curves agree with Diviner to within $\sim$5--8~K
across the lunation at both sites (Fig.~\ref{fig:diviner}). The deep-sensor
retrieval and the orbital surface measurement are mutually consistent.

\section{Mart\'inez density-based model}
\subsubsection*{What it tests}
Whether the inter-site contrast comes from a peculiarity of Hayne's
parameterisation (where $K_d$ is the free knob) or whether an entirely different
conductivity form gives a consistent diagnostic.

\subsubsection*{How it works}
The Martínez \& Siegler (2021) form (Eq.~\ref{eq:Kmartinez}) writes $K$ directly
in terms of density $\rho$ rather than depth, with no explicit $K_d$
parameter. Instead we vary a density scaling $\alpha$ that multiplies the deep
asymptote of $\rho(z)$, and find the $\alpha^{*}$ that minimises the
deep-sensor RMSE. A higher $\alpha^{*}$ means the model needs a denser deep
column to fit the data.

\subsubsection*{What we find}
Apollo~15 prefers $\alpha^{*}\approx 1.13$ ($\rho_d\approx 2026$~kg\,m$^{-3}$).
Apollo~17 prefers $\alpha^{*}\approx 0.98$ ($\rho_d\approx 1767$~kg\,m$^{-3}$).
So the Martínez check is not a second retrieval of the same inter-site ordering.
It is a useful negative control: within the published density law, a simple
bulk-density rescaling does not explain why Hayne's $K_d^{*}$ is larger at A17.
The contrast is therefore more naturally interpreted as contact geometry,
buried blocks, or other structure that the scalar $\rho_d$ proxy cannot express.

\section{Robustness checks against our own assumptions}
Beyond the two independent data sources, we run a battery of self-checks
(Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Joint $(K_d, H)$ RMSE surface.} Simultaneously varying $K_d$ and
  the compaction $e$-folding depth $H$ over $H\in[3,10]$~cm and
  $K_d\in[1.5,18]$~mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ shows a single, well-defined minimum
  near our 1-D retrieval at both sites. There is no second basin we might be
  missing.
\item \textbf{Borestem cut sensitivity.} Sliding the $z>80$~cm shallow-cut
  threshold over $\{70, 80, 90\}$~cm shifts $K_d^{*}$ by under
  $0.15$~mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ at A15 and not at all at A17 --- the result is
  not driven by any single borderline sensor.
\item \textbf{Stability-window threshold.} Changing the $0.08$~K\,yr$^{-1}$
  flatness criterion by $\pm$50\% shifts $K_d^{*}$ by under
  $0.05$~mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ --- the trend cut is not load-bearing.
\item \textbf{Surface-bias test.} Adding a $\pm$2~K constant offset to every
  deep-sensor reading (to mimic a possible Nagihara calibration bias) shifts
  $K_d^{*}$ by less than its bootstrap CI --- the absolute calibration is not
  load-bearing.
\item \textbf{Single-anchor solve.} Re-running with only the deep stage of the
  flux-anchored solver (skipping the cheap shallow stage) gives the same
  $K_d^{*}$ to three decimal places --- the two-stage schedule is purely an
  optimisation, not a structural assumption.
\end{itemize}
Every check is in \code{pipeline/compute/compute\_*\_sensitivity.py} and is rerun
end-to-end by \code{make aux}.

\section{Practice problems}\label{sec:prob-crosschecks}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 10.1 — why the surface closure tests something different]
The Diviner closure compares the model's predicted \emph{surface} diurnal curve
to orbital observations, even though our $K_d^{*}$ was fitted to \emph{deep}
sensors only. Why is this an independent check rather than a tautology?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
The deep-sensor RMSE is dominated by the cycle-mean profile $\Tm(z)$, which is
controlled by $Q_b/K_d$ and the deep-column average $K$. The surface diurnal
curve is dominated by the skin-depth $\delta=\sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}$ which
depends on $K$ in the top centimetres --- a different layer probed by very
different physics. A retrieval that fits the deep mean and then \emph{also}
matches the unrelated surface swing is much more constrained than either
alone --- the agreement is not built in.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 10.2 --- the Mart\'inez $\alpha$ as a sanity check]
At Apollo~15 the Martínez retrieval prefers $\alpha^{*}\approx 1.13$
($\rho_d\approx 2026$ kg/m³). At Apollo~17, $\alpha^{*}\approx 0.98$
($\rho_d\approx 1767$ kg/m³). Does the fact that A17's fitted density scalar is
not larger than A15's falsify the Hayne $K_d$ contrast?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
No. The two fits are answers to different inverse problems. Hayne's free knob is
a deep conductivity asymptote; Martínez's diagnostic knob is a scalar density
asymptote inside a fixed $K(T,\rho)$ law. The alpha sweep says a density-only
rescaling of that published law does \emph{not} reproduce the Hayne ordering.
That is a constraint on interpretation, not a contradiction: the A17 contrast
must live in contact geometry, block fraction, or other structure that a single
bulk-density scalar cannot represent.
\end{solutionbox}

\chapter{How the code is organised, and what comes next}\label{ch:code}
% =====================================================================
The previous chapters explained \emph{what} the code does. This one explains
\emph{how it is organised}, so you can read the source alongside the maths.
The architecture is intentionally simple: a small core physics package
(\code{src/lunar/}), a thin pipeline that orchestrates the per-trial solves
(\code{pipeline/compute/}), and a figure layer that consumes the JSONs the
pipeline writes (\code{pipeline/figures/}). Read this chapter once if you
want to extend the project or port the inner solver; skip it on a first pass
if you just want to understand the result.

\section{Architecture}
The code is layered (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}):

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{\code{src/lunar/}} --- the physics package. Contains the models
  (\code{properties.py}), the depth grid (\code{grid.py}), the time-stepping
  solver (\code{solver.py}), and the equilibrium driver (\code{equilibrium.py}).
  All constants live in \code{constants.py}; all run configuration in
  \code{config.py}.
\item \textbf{\code{pipeline/}} --- thin drivers that call \code{run\_with} and
  produce figures or output files.
\item \textbf{\code{notebooks/}} --- interactive exploration (the equivalence
  demo, the retrieval, the figures).
\item \textbf{\code{tests/}} --- 43 tests including equilibrium certification and
  direct numerical-internal checks.
\end{itemize}

One function, \code{run\_with} (in \code{pipeline/compute/retrieve\_kd.py}), is
the hub: it wires together the grid, the conductivity model, and the solver, and
returns a settled profile. Everything else --- the sweep, the bootstrap, the
cross-checks --- calls \code{run\_with} in a loop.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/fig_architecture.pdf}
\caption{Three layers; imports point only downward. The numerics live in three
small modules (\code{grid}, \code{solver}, \code{equilibrium}).}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\section{The data model: how a pixel travels through the code}
Everything the solver needs about one site --- grid, time-axis, insolation,
albedo, emissivity, basal flux, conductivity function, initial guess --- is
bundled into a single \code{PixelInputs} dataclass; everything the solver
produces --- the cycle-resolved $T(z,t)$ field, surface skin temperature,
diagnostics --- comes back as a \code{PixelOutputs}. There are no globals; the
solver is a pure function of \code{PixelInputs} (\code{src/lunar/solver.py},
line 69--110). That single design decision is what makes the parallel sweep,
the bootstrap, and the joint $(K_d, H)$ surface trivial: every solve is
self-contained, so the caller is free to schedule them however it likes.

The equilibrium driver layers on top: \code{solve\_periodic\_equilibrium} returns
an \code{EquilibriumResult} carrying the per-cycle \code{PixelOutputs}, the
cycle-mean profile $\Tm$, the anchor drift, and the flux-closure residual --- the
self-certification numbers from \S\ref{sec:cert}.

\section{Parallelism: one solve vs.\ many solves}
A single forward solve (one call to \code{run\_with}) is inherently sequential:
each time step depends on the previous one. You cannot split a single trajectory
across cores.

But the \emph{sweep} (28--30 independent $K_d$ values) and the auxiliary grids
are embarrassingly parallel in principle. The current committed production
drivers run them serially, reusing the in-process profile cache in
\code{pipeline/compute/retrieve\_kd.py}; this keeps logs deterministic and makes
failed runs easier to resume. The equilibrium demo and figure scripts show the
parallel pattern with \code{ProcessPoolExecutor}, and the same pattern can be
applied to the retrieval later. The invariant is simple: \emph{more cores can
speed up a batch of independent solves, not one solve.}

\section{Testing strategy: what 43 tests actually pin down}
The test suite (\code{tests/}) is a tiered safety net:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Unit tests} for the leaf modules (grid construction, conductivity,
  density, specific heat, Thomas algorithm, Newton surface solver).
\item \textbf{Solver integration tests} that compare the numerical CN solve
  against the analytical thermal wave in the constant-coefficient regime ---
  first-order convergence is verified, $L^\infty$ error decreases with $\Delta z$.
\item \textbf{Equilibrium regression tests} that enforce the F1 fix is in: a
  guess-independence test (242~K vs 258~K start give the same converged
  profile to $<0.05$~K) and a flux-closure test (mean conductive flux equals
  $Q_b$ below the anchor to within 10\%). These are the regression locks for
  the entire methodological contribution.
\item \textbf{Dedicated internal checks} for the load-bearing pieces named in
  this chapter: the surface-BC Newton root, the sub-skin reconstruction step, and
  the flux-closure certifier. Invalid conductivity and boundary-condition inputs
  now raise clear \code{ValueError}s instead of turning into downstream
  \code{nan}s.
\item \textbf{Headline regression test}: the canonical
  \code{results/kd\_retrieval\_results.json} is reproduced to within $\pm$0.01
  mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ from a clean retrieve run.
\end{itemize}
Any change to the solver that breaks any of these blows the suite, so future
edits cannot silently re-introduce F1-class bugs.

\section{Performance today, and why a C++ port isn't on the critical path}
The inner time step (\code{\_step} in \code{solver.py}) is JIT-compiled by Numba;
the inner Thomas algorithm and Newton surface solver run at near-C speed. The
remaining Python overhead is in the per-call setup (building
\code{PixelInputs}, dispatching to the worker), which is amortised across the
$\sim$10$^5$ time steps each solve runs.

One equilibrium solve takes $\sim$9~s on a modern laptop; the headline retrieve
finishes in $\sim$12~min and the bootstrap in another $\sim$5. For \emph{this}
study that is fine. A C++ port of the entire solver could be $5$--$10\times$
faster --- worthwhile if the pipeline were to be run over a full Diviner map
($\sim$10$^4$ pixels) or as part of an in-situ mission. We mention it not as
work that has been done but as a clean future direction: with the data-model
and test suite already in place, a port can be validated cell-by-cell against
the committed Python results.

\section{Practice problems}\label{sec:prob-code}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 11.1 — why no globals?]
The forward solver is a pure function of \code{PixelInputs}. Suppose we cheated
and made the conductivity model $K(T,z)$ a global variable instead. Identify two
concrete bugs that would be possible under the global design that are impossible
under the dataclass design.
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
\emph{(1)~Race condition.} Run two pixels in parallel with different
conductivity models (say A15 with Hayne and A17 with Martínez). With a global,
worker~A could overwrite the conductivity model worker~B is reading mid-step,
silently giving worker~B the wrong $K$. With dataclasses each worker carries
its own immutable \code{PixelInputs}.
\emph{(2)~Stale-state across runs.} An interactive notebook session that
re-runs a single cell sometimes leaves the global at the last value set.
Re-running the retrieval after a sensitivity test could silently use the
perturbed model. With dataclasses this is impossible --- the function takes its
inputs.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 11.2 — why is the F1 regression test the most important one?]
The 43 tests cover many things. Argue which single test is the most important to
keep green and explain why.
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
\code{test\_equilibrium::test\_guess\_independence}: it asserts the flux-anchored
equilibrium driver returns the same profile from 242~K and 258~K starting
guesses. If \emph{this} test breaks, F1 has returned and every retrieved $K_d$
becomes a function of the starting guess --- i.e.\ wrong. Every other test could
in principle be broken-but-recoverable; F1 silently corrupts the headline number,
so it is the lock the suite cannot afford to lose.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{problembox}[Problem 11.3 — porting strategy]
You are tasked with porting only the inner \code{\_step} function to C++ (not the
whole solver). What two artifacts of the current codebase make that port
\emph{verifiable}, rather than a leap of faith?
\end{problembox}
\begin{solutionbox}
\emph{(1)~The analytical thermal-wave test} in \code{tests/test\_solver.py}
checks one CN step against the closed-form $A e^{-z/\delta}\sin(\omega t - z/\delta)$
solution. Any port that produces the same output on this constant-coefficient
problem to within first-order numerical precision is at least
mechanically correct.
\emph{(2)~The committed \code{results/kd\_retrieval\_results.json}} pins the
end-to-end retrieval to four significant figures. A port that reproduces $K_d^{*}$
to within $\pm 0.01$ has matched the Python at the integration level. Together
they bracket the port from below (unit) and above (system).
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{What we did.} Retrieved the deep regolith conductivity separately at
Apollo~15 and~17: $4.58$ and $8.12$ mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$.
\item \textbf{How.} A 1-D heat model (Ch.~\ref{ch:physics}--\ref{ch:solver}) with

(Ch.~\ref{ch:steady}), with $K_d$ chosen to minimise the deep-sensor misfit
(Ch.~\ref{ch:retrieval}).
\item \textbf{Why the solver is correct.} It is unique by construction, certifies
its own convergence, is guess-independent, and is confirmed by brute force
converging onto it (\S\ref{sec:agree}).
\item \textbf{Why the result is real.} The contrast is statistically significant
(bootstrap $p\approx0.011$, Ch.~\ref{ch:stats}), survives $Q_b$ uncertainty

(Ch.~\ref{ch:crosschecks}).
\item \textbf{The honest caveat.} $K_d$ is conditional on the assumed $Q_b$
(Ch.~\ref{ch:qb}); the robust statement is the inter-site \emph{direction}.
\item \textbf{The physics.} Apollo~17's deep regolith conducts heat about twice as
efficiently --- plausibly denser, more compacted, or more rock-rich material
(Ch.~\ref{ch:sites}).
\end{itemize}

\section{What a future heat-flow mission should do differently}
The Apollo HFE record is the only in-situ lunar heat-flow data we have, and the
analysis in this book has spent most of its pages working around the things the
1971--77 hardware could not pin down. If you are designing the next probe ---
ARTEMIS, Chang'e, or any of the proposed Lunar Geophysical Network nodes ---
the three biggest design choices that would make a re-do meaningful are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Drill deeper, decisively past the daily skin.} The Apollo probes
  reach $1.39$ m (A15) and $2.34$ m (A17); the deepest sensor at A17 sits only
  $\sim$10 skin depths down. Even $3$--$4$ m would put the bottom sensor in a
  regime where the daily wave is below 1\,mK, eliminating the borestem and
  stable-window debates of \S\ref{sec:provenance} entirely. Annual and
  Milankovitch-scale variations would still need stable-window logic, but on a
  cleaner signal.

\item \textbf{Co-locate a thermal-conductivity probe.} The whole
  $K_d$--$Q_b$ degeneracy (Ch.~\ref{ch:qb}) collapses if any \emph{independent}
  in-situ measurement of $K(T,z)$ is available. A heated needle / line-source
  probe in the borestem at $\sim$1 m depth, run for a few diurnal cycles, would
  pin $K$ to within $\sim$10\% --- and then $Q_b$ falls out of Fourier's law
  unconditionally, without leaning on Langseth's analysis of the same probe.

\item \textbf{Sample more than two sites, with deliberate geological contrast.}
  Our entire factor-of-two contrast (Ch.~\ref{ch:sites}) rests on two
  boreholes. With a four-node network spanning a mare interior, a mare/highland
  boundary, a fresh impact ejecta blanket, and a stable highland plain, the
  \emph{shape} of the conductivity-vs-geology relationship becomes measurable,
  not just the existence of a contrast.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The methodological lesson from this study --- that a published
constant ($\chi$, $Q_b$) can swamp a quoted error budget --- generalises:
future missions should plan to retrieve, not assume, the inputs to whichever
forward model carries the analysis.

\section{Common pitfalls --- for the next student}
The single most valuable thing about this project, from a teaching standpoint,
is the list of \emph{mistakes that were almost made and caught}. Future students
of the lunar heat-flow data should know about them:

\begin{warn}
\textbf{1. The 30-lunation spin-up bug (Flag F1).} A fixed-duration spin-up
\emph{looks} converged --- the cycle-to-cycle change drops below tolerance ---
but the deep column is still controlled by the starting guess. The 1000-lunation
diffusive timescale is far longer than the apparent convergence time. \emph{Fix:}
always certify steady state by (a) guess-independence, (b) flux closure, not by
``did the iteration slow down''. (Ch.~\ref{ch:steady}.)

\textbf{2. The $\chi$ conditionality.} The radiative coefficient $\chi$ in
Hayne's $K(T,z)$ is the largest single sensitivity in the entire retrieval,
swamping every term that is in the formal error budget. Reporting $K_d$ without
also reporting $\chi$ is misleading. (\S\ref{sec:chi-conditionality}.)

\textbf{3. The 60-cm borestem outlier.} A sensor very close to the borestem cut
behaves like a deep sensor but lives in the disturbed zone; including it shifts
$K_d^{*}$ enough to matter. \emph{Fix:} apply the cut at $z>80$~cm even when it
costs you a data point. (\S\ref{sec:provenance}; audit appendix.)

\textbf{4. The Diviner hemisphere bug.} GCP files are indexed by latitude band;
mistakenly fetching the \emph{southern} hemisphere band for a northern site
gives a closure check that looks self-consistent but is meaningless. \emph{Fix:}
always print the fetched lat-band before computing residuals. (Audit appendix.)

\textbf{5. The $Q_b$ circularity.} The basal flux values come from the same
HFE thermometers; the retrieval is conditional, not independent, of Langseth's
analysis. Worth re-deriving self-consistently in future work.
(\S\ref{sec:qb-circularity}.)
\end{warn}

\chapter{The pre-submission audit (flag report)}\label{app:flags}
% =====================================================================
Before submission, the released code and the manuscript were audited against each
other line by line --- every physics claim, every number, every figure script
re-run. The findings were logged as numbered ``flags'' in
\code{docs/notes/FLAG\_REPORT.md} and as \code{\textbackslash note\{\}} markers in
\code{letter.tex}. This appendix summarises that audit, because the most important
flag --- F1 --- \emph{is} the methodological contribution of this work.

\section{The two critical flags}
\textbf{F1 --- the spin-up did not converge.} The original pipeline initialised
each run at a hard-coded per-site anchor ($\Tm=250$\,K at A15, $255$\,K at A17)
and spun up for a fixed 30 lunations. Because the deep relaxation time is
$\sim$10$^{3}$ lunations (\S\ref{sec:f1}), the sensor-depth temperatures retained
strong memory of that anchor, so the anchor behaved as an undeclared free
parameter:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lc@{}}
\toprule
Anchor (A15, production settings) & End-to-end $K_d^{*}$ (mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$) \\
\midrule
245 K & 1.75 \\
250 K (as released) & 4.86 \\
255 K & 11.00 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
A $\pm5$\,K change in an uncited starting guess swung $K_d^{*}$ by a factor of
$\sim$6 --- larger than the inter-site contrast itself. The fix is the
flux-anchored, self-certifying equilibrium solver of Ch.~\ref{ch:steady}: the
converged result agrees to $\le0.023$\,K across the column regardless of the
starting guess, and the headline values settled to $K_{d}^{*}=4.58$ / $8.12$.
(The old A17 value of $11.23$ was inflated by its warm $255$\,K anchor; the
inter-site ordering survived, with a smaller contrast.)

\textbf{F2 --- forcing description.} An earlier draft described SPICE/DE440
ephemeris forcing, but the retrieval actually drives the solver with an idealized
cosine insolation, $S_0\cos(\mathrm{lat})\max(0,\cos 2\pi t/P)$. The methods text
was rewritten to describe the implemented forcing and to bound the neglected
terms (solar declination $\pm1.5^{\circ}$, eccentricity $\pm3.3\%$ in flux). A
correct SPICE implementation (\code{src/lunar/ephem.py}) exists for future
per-timestamp work but is not on the retrieval path.

\section{Reproducibility repairs}
The audit also closed several archive gaps: a Diviner hemisphere bug (southern
GCP bands had been fetched for the northern sites) was fixed and the surface
closure rebuilt as a committed script (\code{compute\_diviner\_closure.py}); the
Bayesian posterior outputs and the error-budget JSON were committed; and stale
methods numbers (sweep grids, the $H$ range, stability thresholds) were corrected.

\section{What the audit confirmed clean}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item The \textbf{Crank--Nicolson} assembly, harmonic-mean face conductivities,
  Newton surface balance (residual and analytic derivative), and the
  geothermal-flux Neumann bottom boundary --- all mathematically correct.
\item The geometric grid ($\Delta z_0=2$\,mm, ratio $1.08$, $z_{\max}=5$\,m).
\item The Hayne (2017) $K(T,z)$, $\rho(z)$, $c_p(T)$ and the Mart\'inez \&
  Siegler (2021) $K(T,\rho)$ coefficients, verified against the cited sources.
\item Every committed headline number matches the manuscript, and the full unit
  test suite passes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Troubleshooting}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{SciPy build error on macOS:} force prebuilt wheels with
  \code{pip install --only-binary=:all: -e ".[dev]"}.
\item \textbf{SPICE kernel download blocked:} \code{src/lunar/ephem.py} reads
  kernels from \code{data/spice/} by default and honors
  \code{SPICE\_KERNEL\_DIR}; point that variable at a local directory holding
  the DE440 kernel and the NAIF lunar/earth PCK.
\item \textbf{Diviner SSL error:} \code{pipeline/fetch\_diviner.py} retries with
  verification disabled --- the PDS data is public, so this is safe.
\item \textbf{Missing LaTeX font/package:} install a complete distribution
  (TeX~Live or MacTeX); minimal installs lack Times + \code{microtype}.
\end{itemize}
