\section{Introduction}
Every orbital instrument that senses the Moon below its optical surface
(thermal radiometers today, microwave and terahertz sounders next)
must invert its measurements through a model of the subsurface
temperature column $T(z)$. Below the top few centimeters, the most
leveraged input to that column is the deep thermal conductivity of the
regolith, $K_d$. The community standard is a single, globally averaged
value, $K_d = 3.4$ mW m$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$, calibrated by Hayne et al.\ to
orbital nighttime cooling curves [1]. However, the diurnal wave that
calibration sees penetrates only ${\sim}30$ cm; at one to two meters
the global value is an extrapolation, not a measurement.

The only in-situ measurements of the lunar subsurface ever made are the
Apollo 15 and 17 Heat Flow Experiments (HFE): borehole probes that
recorded regolith temperatures from 1971 to 1977 [2], recently restored
to modern usability [3]. Figure~\ref{fig:sites} locates the two sites
on the nearside through three orthographic layers: Clementine albedo,
the modeled diurnal-mean surface temperature over LOLA shaded relief,
and LOLA topography, with Apollo~15 at the Hadley--Apennine
mare/highland margin and Apollo~17 in the Taurus--Littrow basaltic
embayment. This work asks the narrowest useful question of those data:
holding the standard conductivity model fixed in every other respect,
what deep conductivity does each borehole actually require? The answer
bears directly on upcoming subsurface radiative-transfer missions such
as the TSUKIMI terahertz surveyor [5], whose forward model needs the
sub-diurnal-skin temperature column that only an in-situ-anchored $K_d$
can provide.

\section{Data and method}
\subsection{Data}
For each site the multi-year record is reduced to one stable
equilibrium temperature per sensor, read from a quiet late-mission
window (shaded in Fig.~\ref{fig:record}). The vertical bands in the
figure mark documented operational disturbances (drilling, ALSEP
outages, major station events) that are excluded from the window,
and the dashed line is a least-squares slope fit to the deepest sensor,
whose residual trend (${+}0.065$ and ${+}0.060$ K yr$^{-1}$) is the
long-term subsurface warming analyzed by Nagihara et al.\ [3]. Sensors
shallower than 80 cm are excluded because the borestem conducts the
surface wave downward (gray rows in Fig.~\ref{fig:record}); this leaves
7 deep sensors at Apollo 15 and 16 at Apollo 17, spanning 80--234 cm.
The basal boundary condition is the revised Langseth et al.\ [2]
borehole heat flux, $Q_b = 21$ and 16 mW m$^{-2}$.

\subsection{Forward model and the flux-anchored solver}
The temperature column obeys the one-dimensional heat equation with the
Hayne conductivity form
$K(T,z) = [K_d - (K_d - K_s)e^{-z/H}]\,[1 + \chi (T/350\,\mathrm{K})^3]$,
whose deep asymptote $K_d$ is the single free parameter [1]. The
observable is the periodic steady state, which brute-force
time-stepping reaches only after ${\sim}10^3$ simulated lunations
(${\sim}26$ minutes per solve). The methodological contribution of this
work is a \textit{flux-anchored} solver: in periodic steady state the
cycle-mean flux is constant in depth and pinned to $Q_b$, so the
two-variable heat equation collapses below the diurnal skin to a
one-variable closure ODE,
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\langle T\rangle}{dz}
   = \frac{Q_b - u_{\mathrm{rect}}(z)}{K(\langle T\rangle, z)},
\end{equation}
where $u_{\mathrm{rect}}$ is the rectified flux carried by the
oscillation. The solver time-steps only the thin skin, reads the
cycle-mean temperature at an anchor depth of 0.55 m, and reconstructs
the deep column by integrating Eq.~(1). One solve takes 0.6 s instead
of ${\sim}26$ minutes (a ${\sim}2500\times$ speedup at
certified-equal answers), and that speed is what makes the 1500-fold
bootstrap of Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}a and the point-by-point flux-plane
map of Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}b affordable.

\subsection{Retrieval and uncertainty}
For each site, $K_d$ is swept across a dense grid; each candidate is
solved to periodic equilibrium and scored by the root-mean-square
misfit to the deep sensors. The retrieved $K_d^{*}$ is the vertex of
the resulting misfit bowl, shown with its 95\% bootstrap interval as a
star and horizontal bar in Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}. Uncertainty is
quantified by a non-parametric bootstrap: 1500 resamples of the sensor
set, with placement depths jittered by their 2.5 cm uncertainty [3],
each re-retrieved in full, an analysis made routine by the fast
solver. Systematic errors are budgeted separately, by re-running the
retrieval over the literature uncertainty range of each fixed input:
the per-site $1\sigma$ totals are 1.88 (A15) and 3.78 (A17)
mW m$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$, dominated by the basal flux at both sites
($\pm 1.6$) and, at Apollo 17, also by the surface albedo, while the
statistical and solver terms are minor ($\leq 0.53$ and 0.04).

\section{Results}
Both boreholes require deep conductivities well above the
Hayne et al.\ [1] global value, and markedly different from each
other (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}):
\begin{equation}
K_d^{*}(\mathrm{A15}) = 4.60^{+0.86}_{-0.21}, \qquad
K_d^{*}(\mathrm{A17}) = 7.08^{+0.53}_{-0.52}
\ \ \mathrm{mW\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}
\end{equation}
($1\sigma$ bootstrap; 95\% intervals $[4.18, 6.96]$ and
$[6.16, 8.07]$; distributions in Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}a). The shapes of
the two misfit bowls in Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep} explain the error shapes:
the Apollo 15 bowl is nearly flat toward high $K_d$, so its interval
stretches far upward ($+0.86$) but little downward ($-0.21$), whereas
the deeper, more symmetric Apollo 17 bowl yields a nearly symmetric
interval. The global
$K_d = 3.4$ lies outside both 95\%
intervals: it misfits the deep sensors by 1.09 K (A15) and 0.89 K
(A17), whereas the site fits reduce this to 1.00 and 0.40 K. The
inter-site contrast has bootstrap median $+2.31$ mW m$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$
with 95\% interval $[-0.12, 3.56]$ and $p = 0.031$: marginal at the
95\% level, and reported here as such rather than overstated.
Table~\ref{tab:results} collects the retrieved values.

\section{Robustness of the contrast}
The dominant systematic is the basal flux itself, which is debated at
both sites [2,4]. Rather than assume the usual proportional
$K_d$--$Q_b$ degeneracy, this work maps it directly, re-retrieving
$K_d^{*}$ at every point of the two-site flux plane. The site responses are
opposite: $K_d^{*}$(A15) rises with its flux while $K_d^{*}$(A17)
falls, so the contrast never changes sign. Figure~\ref{fig:robust}b
shows it as a heatmap of the contrast over the two basal fluxes: warm
(Apollo~17 more conductive) across the whole plausible plane, with the
``no-difference'' line ($\Delta = 0$) reaching only the far
low-A15/high-A17 corner. The dashed contours grade the significance in
units of the bootstrap standard error: at the Langseth et al.\ [2]
fluxes (dot; 21 and 16 mW m$^{-2}$) the contrast is $+2.5$, about
$2.6\sigma$, and it only grows away from that point. The square marks
the independent reanalysis of Saito et al.\ [4], which lowers the
Apollo 15 flux by ${\sim}30\%$: there the contrast nearly doubles,
to $+4.8$. The shaded teal region is the Bayesian MCMC posterior for
the two fluxes (68\% and 95\% credible); it keeps the ordering
$P[K_d^{*}(\mathrm{A17}) > K_d^{*}(\mathrm{A15})] = 99.3\%$ for prior
widths from $\pm 10\%$ to $\pm 40\%$, and its medians (star; 17.7 and
13.7 mW m$^{-2}$) sit slightly below the Langseth et al.\ [2] values,
in the direction Saito et al.\ argued.

A factor-${\sim}1.5$ contrast between the two sites is geologically
unremarkable: Apollo core porosities alone span an effective-medium
conductivity factor of ${\sim}1.5$. The setting is visible in
Fig.~\ref{fig:sites}: in the albedo and topography layers Apollo~17
sits inside the dark, low-lying basaltic embayment of Taurus--Littrow,
while Apollo~15 straddles the bright mare/highland margin at
Hadley--Apennine: two different regolith column histories, so two
different meter-scale conductivities should not surprise.

\section{Conclusions}
(1) The Apollo boreholes require site-resolved deep conductivities,
$4.60$ and $7.08$ mW m$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$; the global 3.4 is excluded at
both sites, so meter-scale lunar thermal models should not carry a
single Moon-wide $K_d$. (2) The inter-site contrast is marginal in
magnitude ($p = 0.031$) but directionally robust across the entire
plausible basal-flux domain (MCMC ordering probability 99.3\%).
(3) The flux-anchored solver makes exhaustive uncertainty analysis of
borehole-constrained thermal models routine (0.6 s per solve, certified
to reproduce the brute-force answer). (4) The
retrievals are conditional on the fixed Hayne functional form: relaxing
the mixing-depth parameter $H$ alone widens the Apollo 17 uncertainty
to ${\pm}3.5$ mW m$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$, a caveat this work states rather
than hides. (5) The retrieved per-site temperature columns supply the
forward-model boundary condition that TSUKIMI-class subsurface sounders
cannot measure from orbit [5].
