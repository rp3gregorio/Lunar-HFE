\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Lunar subsurface thermal models rely almost universally on a single,
globally averaged deep-regolith conductivity, most commonly the
$\Kd=3.4$~\mWmK\ of the \citet{hayne2017} Diviner calibration. This
thesis asks whether that number survives contact with the only in-situ
subsurface temperature measurements ever made on the Moon --- the Apollo
15 and 17 Heat Flow Experiment (HFE) borehole records --- and finds that
it does not, at either site. Holding the \citet{hayne2017} conductivity
form fixed and sweeping its deep asymptote \Kd\ alone against the
deep-sensor ($80$--$234$~cm) misfit, the retrieval gives
$\Kds(\mathrm{A15})=4.60^{+0.86}_{-0.21}$ and
$\Kds(\mathrm{A17})=7.08^{+0.53}_{-0.52}$~\mWmK\ ($1\sigma$ bootstrap),
placing the global value outside both sites' 95\% intervals. The
inter-site contrast, $+2.31$~\mWmK\ (95\% CI $[-0.12, 3.56]$,
$p=0.031$), is marginal at 95\% yet directionally robust: on a directly
solved RMSE$(\Kd,\Qb)$ surface the ordering
$\Kds(\mathrm{A17})>\Kds(\mathrm{A15})$ holds with 99.3\% posterior
probability for basal-flux priors from $\pm10\%$ to $\pm40\%$, because
the two sites respond to flux revisions in opposite directions. The
methodological contribution is a flux-anchored equilibrium solver that
collapses the two-variable heat equation to a one-variable closure ODE
below the diurnal skin, accelerating each periodic-equilibrium solve
from ${\sim}26$ minutes to ${\sim}0.6$ seconds and thereby making the
full uncertainty analysis (1500-draw bootstrap, MCMC cross-checks,
direct degeneracy maps) routine. The retrieved per-site \Kds\ values
supply the meter-scale boundary condition required by upcoming
subsurface radiative-transfer missions, in particular the TSUKIMI
terahertz surveyor \citep{tsukimi2022}.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{One number for a whole Moon}
The temperature of the Moon's subsurface is not measured; it is modeled.
Every orbital instrument that looks below the optical surface --- from
the Diviner radiometer \citep{paige2010,williams2017diviner} to any
future microwave or terahertz sounder --- sees radiation whose emission
depth exceeds the reach of direct measurement, and must therefore invert
its observations through a forward model of the subsurface temperature
column $T(z)$. The single most leveraged input to that column, below the
top few centimeters, is the deep thermal conductivity of the regolith,
\Kd.

The standard source for \Kd\ is the global calibration of
\citet{hayne2017}, which fits a two-layer conductivity--density model to
Diviner nighttime cooling curves and arrives at
$\Kd = 3.4$~\mWmK\ --- one value for the entire Moon. This is a
reasonable choice for a global product built from surface-sensitive
observations, but the diurnal wave that Diviner sees penetrates only a
few skin depths (${\sim}30$~cm); the value of \Kd\ at one to two meters
is an extrapolation, not a measurement. Laboratory measurements of
returned Apollo soils span $0.7$--$2$~\mWmK\
\citep{cremers1971,hemingway1973,horai1981}, the original in-situ HFE
reductions gave $1$--$3$~\mWmK\ \citep{langseth1972,langseth1976},
later reanalyses revised these upward \citep{keihm1984,grott2010}, and
orbital estimates reach ${\sim}7$~\mWmK\ \citep{vasavada2012}: five
decades of estimates disagreeing by an order of magnitude
(Fig.~\ref{fig:prior}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/fig_prior_estimates.pdf}
  \caption[Five decades of meter-scale \Kd\ estimates]{Five decades of
  meter-scale \Kd\ estimates. Laboratory soils, original in-situ
  reductions, reanalysis arrows, orbital estimates, the
  \citet{hayne2017} global fit, and the per-site values retrieved in
  this work.}
  \label{fig:prior}
\end{figure}

\section{The only ground truth there is}
Two experiments constrain the meter-scale thermal state of the Moon in
situ: the Apollo 15 and Apollo 17 Heat Flow Experiments
\citep{langseth1972,langseth1976}, fiberglass-epoxy borestems carrying
platinum-resistance sensor bridges to depths of $1.4$ and $2.3$~m,
which returned five and a half years (1971--1977) of subsurface
temperatures, restored to modern usability by \citet{nagihara2018}.
This thesis treats those two boreholes as what they are --- the only
ground truth available --- and asks the narrowest useful question:
\emph{holding the community-standard conductivity model fixed in every
other respect, what deep conductivity does each borehole actually
require?}

\section{Why per-site values matter}\label{sec:intro-tsukimi}
We use the term \emph{meter-scale regolith} throughout for the layer
constrained by the deep HFE sensors ($80$--$234$~cm). Below the diurnal
skin the modeled column is controlled by \Kd\ through both the
rectified annual mean and the steady geothermal gradient $\Qb/\Kd$, so
the $T(z)$ ingested by any subsurface radiative-transfer (RTM)
retrieval inherits its \Kd\ dependence; a factor-of-two error in \Kd\
propagates through the meter-scale temperature profile into any product
built on it. A concrete consumer is the upcoming TSUKIMI mission (Lunar
Terahertz SUrveyor for KIlometer-scale MappIng; \citealp{tsukimi2022}),
a NICT/JAXA-led terahertz surveyor for subsurface water and resource
mapping whose forward model requires precisely the sub-diurnal-skin
temperature column that only an in-situ-anchored \Kd\ can provide.

\section{Contributions and thesis outline}
This thesis makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A method}: the flux-anchored equilibrium solver
    (Chapter~\ref{ch:method}), which converts the periodic
    steady-state problem from thousands of time-stepped lunations into
    a skin-only time-step plus a one-variable closure ODE --- a
    measured $21\times$ acceleration per solve at the algorithmic
    level, growing to ${\sim}2500\times$ with the compiled kernel, at
    certified-equal answers.
  \item \textbf{A measurement}: per-site deep conductivities
    $\Kds(\mathrm{A15})=4.60$ and $\Kds(\mathrm{A17})=7.08$~\mWmK\
    with a defensible, bootstrap-based uncertainty budget
    (Chapters~\ref{ch:retrieval}--\ref{ch:results}).
  \item \textbf{A robustness case}: a direct (assumption-free) map of
    the $\Kd$--$\Qb$ degeneracy showing the two sites respond to
    basal-flux revisions in opposite directions, which makes the
    \emph{sign} of the inter-site contrast robust across the entire
    published flux envelope even though its magnitude is marginal
    (Chapter~\ref{ch:robustness}).
\end{enumerate}
Chapter~\ref{ch:data} describes the data; Chapter~\ref{ch:model} the
thermal model; Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets the contrast
geologically and states the honest caveats; Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}
closes with implications for future missions.
Appendix~\ref{app:derivation} derives the closure ODE in full;
Appendix~\ref{app:numerics} records the numerical scheme and its
certification, including the audit trail of the three input errors
found and fixed on the way to the final numbers;
Appendix~\ref{app:repro} gives reproducibility instructions.

\chapter{Data: the Apollo Heat Flow Experiments}\label{ch:data}

\section{Instrument and geometry}
Each HFE site carries two probes ${\sim}9$~m apart, each probe a
fiberglass-epoxy borestem with gradient-bridge (TG) and ring-bridge
(TR) platinum-resistance sensors (Fig.~\ref{fig:probes}). A gradient
bridge reads the temperature difference across a known vertical
baseline (${\sim}50$~cm), which is what makes the experiment a
\emph{heat-flow} experiment: with a conductivity model, $q =
-K\,\partial T/\partial z$ follows directly. Apollo 15 (Hadley Rille)
reaches $1.39$~m; Apollo 17 (Taurus--Littrow) reaches $2.34$~m. Sensor
placement depths carry a ${\sim}2.5$~cm uncertainty
\citep{nagihara2018}, which the bootstrap of
Chapter~\ref{ch:retrieval} propagates explicitly.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/fig_probe_geometry.pdf}
  \caption[Apollo 15 borehole geometry and the gradient bridge]{Apollo
  15 borehole geometry and the gradient-bridge sensing principle.
  Shallow sensors (open) are excluded by the $80$~cm borestem cut; deep
  sensors (filled) constrain \Kds.}
  \label{fig:probes}
\end{figure}

\section{From six years of records to one equilibrium temperature per
  sensor}\label{sec:stable-window}
The retrieval does not fit the raw time series. For each sensor we
select a late-mission stability window --- after the drilling
disturbance has decayed and outside eclipse/season anomalies --- and
reduce the record to an equilibrium temperature $T_{\rm eq}$ with a
per-sensor standard deviation (code:
\texttt{lunar.apollo\_helpers.extract\_sensor\_stability}). The
resulting mean-temperature profiles (Fig.~\ref{fig:meanT}) are the
observable this thesis fits: a set of late-window equilibrium estimates,
not a movie.

More precisely, these points are estimates of the undisturbed
periodic-equilibrium profile, not direct proof that every sensor record
had reached exact zero-drift steady state. The Apollo boreholes were
heated by drilling and then relaxed toward the surrounding regolith;
where the strict flat-window criterion is not met, the late-record mean
is retained as the best available asymptotic-temperature estimate and
its residual drift is treated as a data systematic. The thermal model
therefore asks a physically specific question: what steady regolith
column would produce the late Apollo temperature profile after the
emplacement transient has decayed?

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_apollo_mean_T_profile.pdf}
  \caption[Mean temperature profiles at both sites]{Stable-window mean
  temperatures against depth at both sites, with the borestem exclusion
  zone shaded and the smooth model profiles for context. Deep TG/TR
  sensors (filled) enter the retrieval; shallow sensors (open) are

  \label{fig:meanT}
\end{figure}

\section{Why only the deep sensors}\label{sec:deep-cut}
Two independent reasons set the $z \ge 80$~cm cut.
\emph{Physics:} the diurnal wave attenuates exponentially with depth;
by ${\sim}80$~cm its amplitude is far below the sensor noise, so deep
sensors read the cycle-mean state that \Kd\ controls, uncontaminated by
shallow-skin dynamics governed by \Ks\
(Fig.~\ref{fig:amplitude}). \emph{Instrument:} the borestem itself
conducts the surface wave downward, disturbing shallow readings --- the
long-recognized borestem problem of the original reductions
\citep{langseth1976,keihm1984,grott2010}. The cut leaves $N=7$ deep
sensors at Apollo 15 and $N=16$ at Apollo 17; the sensitivity of \Kds\
to the exact cut depth is swept in the error budget
(Table~\ref{tab:budget}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_amplitude_vs_depth.pdf}
  \caption[Diurnal amplitude vs.\ depth]{Observed diurnal amplitude
  against depth at both sites, compared with model attenuation curves.
  Below the shaded borestem zone the measured amplitudes collapse
  toward the digitization floor: the deep sensors see the cycle-mean
  column, which is what \Kd\ sets.}
  \label{fig:amplitude}
\end{figure}

\section{Basal heat flux}
The lower boundary condition is the revised borehole heat flux of
\citet{langseth1976}: $\Qb = 21$~\mWm\ (A15) and $16$~\mWm\ (A17).
These values are themselves debated --- \citet{saito2007} argue A15
lower by ${\sim}30\%$, and \citet{nagihara2018} discuss the record's
limitations --- which is why Chapter~\ref{ch:robustness} treats \Qb\
not as a fixed truth but as the dominant, explicitly mapped
systematic.

\chapter{The thermal model}\label{ch:model}

\section{Governing equation and properties}
The subsurface temperature obeys the one-dimensional heat equation
\begin{equation}
  \rho(z)\, c_p(T)\, \frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
  = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\left[K(T,z)\,
    \frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right],
  \label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}
with the \citet{hayne2017} property set: density
$\rho(z)$ relaxing from a surface to a deep value on the compaction
scale $H$, specific heat $c_p(T)$ from the standard polynomial, and
conductivity
\begin{equation}
  K(T,z) = \underbrace{\left[\Kd - (\Kd-\Ks)\,
      e^{-z/H}\right]}_{\text{contact conduction } K_c(z)}
    \underbrace{\left[1+\chi\left(\frac{T}{T_{\rm ref}}\right)^{3}
      \right]}_{\text{radiative term}},
  \label{eq:conductivity}
\end{equation}
with $\Ks=7.4\times10^{-4}$~\si{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin},
$H=6$~cm, $\chi=2.7$, $T_{\rm ref}=350$~K \citep{hayne2017}. \Kd\ ---
the deep asymptote of $K_c$ --- is the single free parameter of this
work; every other constant is held at its published value precisely so
that the retrieved \Kds\ is interpretable \emph{within} the standard
model rather than as a new model. Figure~\ref{fig:kTz} shows the
resulting $K(T,z)$ field under the retrieved conductivities: five
orders of magnitude of variation compressed into the top few
centimeters, then the flat deep asymptote that the HFE sensors sample.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/fig_book_kTz.pdf}
  \caption[The $K(T,z)$ field under the retrieved conductivities]{The
  \citet{hayne2017} conductivity field $K(T,z)$ evaluated with the
  retrieved per-site \Kds. The steep near-surface rise is the
  compaction term on the scale $H$; the temperature dependence is the
  radiative $\chi (T/T_{\rm ref})^3$ term.}
  \label{fig:kTz}
\end{figure}

\section{Boundary conditions}
At the surface, insolation ($S_0=1361$~\si{\watt\per\square\meter};
\citealp{kopp2011}), albedo, and thermal emission
($\varepsilon=0.95$) balance the conducted flux; the resulting
nonlinear equation for the surface temperature is solved by Newton
iteration at every time step. At the base ($z_{\max}=5$~m) the
geothermal flux is imposed as a Neumann condition $K\,\partial_z T =
\Qb$ through a ghost cell. A fixed-temperature or zero-flux base would
be wrong: the deep profile \emph{keeps rising} at the geothermal
gradient $\Qb/K$, which is physics, not drift.

\section{The two-timescale structure of the problem}
\label{sec:twotimescale}
Figure~\ref{fig:skinwave} displays the structure that everything in
Chapter~\ref{ch:method} exploits: over one lunation the surface swings
by ${\sim}280$~K, yet the swing collapses within the first half-meter.
Above that depth the column is a driven oscillator; below it, the
column is effectively steady, carrying only the geothermal flux and
the rectified imprint of the oscillation. The diurnal skin depth and
the meter-scale sensors live on opposite sides of this divide --- which
is precisely why the deep sensors constrain \Kd\ cleanly, and why
time-stepping the whole 5~m column is wasteful.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/fig_book_skinwave.pdf}
  \caption[The diurnal wave collapsing with depth]{One lunation of
  modeled temperature at increasing depths: the ${\sim}280$~K surface
  swing collapses within the first half-meter. Below the skin the
  column is quasi-steady.}
  \label{fig:skinwave}
\end{figure}

\section{Why brute force is not enough}
The observable is the \emph{periodic steady state}: the temperature
column after the drilling transient has relaxed, repeating cycle after
cycle. Reached naively --- time-stepping Eq.~\eqref{eq:heat} until the
deep column stops drifting --- this costs ${\sim}3000$ simulated
lunations at $522.6$~ms per lunation, i.e.\ ${\sim}26$ minutes per
solve, because the deep-column relaxation time scales as $z^2/\kappa$.
The sweep-plus-bootstrap program of Chapter~\ref{ch:retrieval}
requires tens of thousands of solves; by brute force the uncertainty
analysis alone would cost months of CPU time. The flux-anchored
solver reduces a 61-solve sweep from a projected $26.6$~hours to
$39$~seconds.

\chapter{The flux-anchored equilibrium method}\label{ch:method}

\section{The idea in one paragraph}
Only the top few skin depths of the column ever feel the diurnal
forcing; below the rectification zone the cycle-averaged column is
already in a steady state fully determined by the basal flux. So:
time-step \emph{only the skin} (Step~A), anchor the solution at a depth
$z_0=0.55$~m where the diurnal amplitude has fallen to ${\sim}0.15$~K,
and \emph{reconstruct} everything below the anchor by integrating a
one-variable steady-state ODE (Step~B). Iterate A--B until the anchor
temperature stops moving. The fixed point of this composition is the
periodic steady state of the full problem.

\section{Step B: the closure ODE}\label{sec:closure}
Averaging Eq.~\eqref{eq:heat} over one lunation $P$ in periodic steady
state kills the storage term (the enthalpy returns to its starting
value each cycle), so the cycle-mean flux is constant in depth and
pinned by the bottom boundary to \Qb. Writing the mean flux in terms of
the mean temperature introduces one correction --- the rectified flux
$\urect \equiv \langle K\,\partial_z T\rangle -
K(\Tm)\,\partial_z\Tm$, the covariance of the oscillating conductivity
and gradient --- and yields the closure
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\frac{d\Tm}{dz} = \frac{\Qb - \urect(z)}{K(\Tm, z)}\;}
  \label{eq:closure}
\end{equation}
(the sign convention is $\Qb-\urect$; Appendix~\ref{app:derivation}
gives every algebraic step). Above the anchor, $\urect$ is evaluated
from the Step~A skin solution; below it, $\urect$ is negligible
($<1\%$ of \Qb) and Eq.~\eqref{eq:closure} integrates downward as an
ordinary initial-value problem from the anchored temperature, by
second-order Runge--Kutta on the same geometric grid. Two variables
($z,t$) have become one ($z$): this is the entire speed-up.
Figure~\ref{fig:anatomy} shows the anatomy of one converged solve: the
full $T(z)$ column with the anchor marked, and the cycle-mean flux
holding at \Qb\ below the skin --- the physical fact the method is
named after.

\section{Step A: time-step the skin only}\label{sec:stepA}
Step~A advances the full time-dependent equation, but \emph{only} on a
truncated sub-grid reaching just below the anchor
($[0,\,z_0+0.15~\mathrm{m}]$); everything deeper is reconstructed by
Step~B each outer cycle. This truncation is structural, not merely an
optimization: when Step~A time-steps the full 5~m column, the slow
deep-column relaxation leaks into the anchor and the retrieval
acquires a spurious dependence on the inner-loop length (the
$n_{\rm inner}$ bias dissected in Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}). With
the truncation, the retrieved \Kds\ is independent of $n_{\rm inner}$
at the certified settings and the convergence cost drops by a further
factor of ${\sim}4$.

\section{The outer loop as a fixed-point iteration}
\label{sec:fixedpoint}
The composition (Step~A on the skin) $\circ$ (Step~B below the anchor)
is a contraction: Step~A relaxes any starting skin toward the true skin
solution within a few lunations (the skin's diffusive timescale is
short), and Step~B is \emph{exact} below the anchor given a correct
anchor temperature. The loop therefore converges geometrically; in the
production configuration the anchor drift falls $27 \to 15 \to 8 \to
4$~mK over four outer cycles, entering the $\pm 5$~mK tolerance at
cycle four to five (Fig.~\ref{fig:anchorconv}). Convergence is judged
on the \emph{anchor drift}, never on a fixed cycle count --- the
fixed-count trap and its consequences are part of the certification
record (Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}).

  \caption[Outer-loop convergence of the anchor temperature]{The

\section{Measured cost}\label{sec:cost}
Table~\ref{tab:speed} and Fig.~\ref{fig:speedup} give the measured
wall-clock numbers (median of three runs, Apple silicon laptop). The
algorithmic gain (anchored vs.\ brute force, both interpreted) is
$21\times$ per solve; compiling the Step-A march (a
\texttt{numba} kernel transcribed from a verified C++ reference)
multiplies this by a further $117\times$, to $0.636$~s per solve and
$39$~s for the full 61-point sweep. The C++ reference itself runs at
$3.24$~ms per lunation against $4.71$ for the compiled Python kernel
and $538$ for the interpreted solver --- confirming that the remaining
gap between Python and C++ is a constant factor of ${\sim}1.45$, not
an algorithmic difference. All three implementations agree to
$1.4\times10^{-12}$~K.

\section{Certification, in brief}
Speed bought by a wrong answer is worthless, so equality with the
ground truth is certified rather than assumed, at three levels:
(i)~a fully converged brute-force run reaches the same profile;
(ii)~the discretization was pushed down a time-step ladder until the
\emph{retrieved} \Kds\ --- the quantity actually reported --- stopped
moving, a process that exposed and eliminated a first-order defect in
the time march (the missing wrap step); and (iii)~an independent C++
implementation reproduces the solver to $10^{-12}$~K. The full record,
including the three input errors found along the way and their effect
on the headline number, is Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}.

\chapter{Retrieval and uncertainty}\label{ch:retrieval}

\section{The sweep}
For each site, \Kd\ is swept across a dense grid (denser near the
minimum), each grid point solved to periodic equilibrium, the model
column interpolated to the deep-sensor depths, and the root-mean-square
misfit RMSE$(\Kd)$ recorded. The retrieved \Kds\ is the vertex of a
parabola through the bracketing minimum (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}); grid
convergence of the vertex was verified against coarser and denser

\section{Bootstrap uncertainty}\label{sec:bootstrap}
The primary uncertainty estimate is a non-parametric bootstrap
\citep{efron1993}: 1500 resamples of the deep-sensor set, each with
sensor depths jittered by the $2.5$~cm placement uncertainty
\citep{nagihara2018}, each re-retrieved through the full sweep --- an
analysis that only became affordable with the fast solver. The
bootstrap propagates exactly the two quantities measured at the
boreholes (which sensors exist and where they sit) and assumes nothing
about the basal flux. The result (Fig.~\ref{fig:boot}):
\begin{equation}
  \Kds(\mathrm{A15}) = 4.60^{+0.86}_{-0.21}\,[4.18,\,6.96], \qquad
  \Kds(\mathrm{A17}) = 7.08^{+0.53}_{-0.52}\,[6.16,\,8.07]\ \mWmK
\end{equation}
($1\sigma$ asymmetric about the point estimate; brackets 95\%). The
inter-site contrast, formed by pairing bootstrap draws, has median
$+2.31$~\mWmK, 95\% CI $[-0.12, 3.56]$, $p = 0.031$:
\emph{marginal} at the 95\% level --- the interval touches zero --- a
statement this thesis reports plainly rather than papers over.

\chapter{Robustness: the \texorpdfstring{$\Kd$--$\Qb$}{Kd-Qb}
  degeneracy, measured not assumed}\label{ch:robustness}

\section{Why the obvious shortcut is wrong}
\Kd\ and \Qb\ enter the deep gradient together ($d\Tm/dz \simeq
\Qb/K$), suggesting the proportional invariance $(\alpha\Kd,
\alpha\Qb) \to$ same profile --- an assumption under which a basal-flux
error rescales both sites equally and cancels in the contrast. Direct
re-retrieval \emph{disproves} this invariance for the present problem:
the radiative $K(T)^3$ coupling and the skin-column temperature offset
break it. Figure~\ref{fig:qbdeg} shows the realized $\Kds(\Qb)$
relations, one full re-retrieval per point: $\Kds(\mathrm{A15})$
\emph{rises} with its flux (from $2.07$ to $5.44$~\mWmK\ across
$\Qb=14$--$26$~\mWm) while $\Kds(\mathrm{A17})$ \emph{falls} (from
$10.13$ to $6.80$ across $10$--$18$~\mWm) --- opposite site responses
that no proportional model can represent.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/fig_qb_degeneracy.pdf}
  \caption[The measured $\Kds(\Qb)$ relations]{The measured
  $\Kds(\Qb)$ relation at each site (one full re-retrieval per grid
  point). The two sites respond to a flux revision in opposite
  directions, which is what makes the contrast's sign
  envelope-robust.}
  \label{fig:qbdeg}
\end{figure}

\section{The direct map}
We therefore map the degeneracy by brute honesty: the contrast
$\Delta\Kds$ over the full published flux envelope
($\Qb^{\mathrm{A15}}\in[14,25]$, $\Qb^{\mathrm{A17}}\in[10,18]$~\mWm),
interpolated from the measured curves of Fig.~\ref{fig:qbdeg}, no
degeneracy model at all (Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}a). The contrast is
positive over the \emph{entire} measured domain (minimum
$+1.4$~\mWmK); the most adverse envelope corner compresses it to
$+1.5$ (${\sim}1.6\sigma$), the favorable corner expands it to
$+8.1$. Notably, a $-33\%$ revision of $\Qb^{\mathrm{A17}}$ alone ---
the direction the reanalysis debate points --- \emph{raises} the
contrast.

\section{Bayesian cross-check}
As an independent check on the \emph{direction} of the contrast (not a
second estimate of its magnitude), a Bayesian inversion on the same
directly solved RMSE$(\Kd,\Qb)$ surfaces, propagating the

\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:discussion}

\section{Why would two sites differ?}
A factor-${\sim}1.5$ conductivity contrast between Hadley Rille and
Taurus--Littrow is physically unremarkable. Apollo cores span bulk
porosities $\phi\sim0.30$--$0.45$ \citep{mitchell1973,carrier1991};
the effective-medium scaling $K_{\rm eff}\propto(1-\phi)^2$
\citep{wood2020} yields a factor ${\sim}1.5$ across that range alone.
Compositionally, solid basalt conducts ${\sim}30\%$ better than
anorthosite \citep{horai1972}, and Apollo 17 sits in a basaltic
embayment while Apollo 15 straddles a mare/highland margin
\citep{korotev2005,wieczorek2006,crawford2015}. The product of the two
mechanisms (${\sim}1.9$) covers the retrieved ratio without requiring
either at its extreme.

\section{Honest caveats}\label{sec:caveats}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Marginal significance.} The bootstrap contrast CI
    touches zero ($p=0.031$); the claim defended here is the
    \emph{ordering and its robustness}, not a 95\%-significant
    magnitude.
  \item \textbf{Conditionality on $\chi$.} The radiative coefficient is
    held at the global $\chi=2.7$; \Kds\ trades against it, and a
    per-site $\chi$ cannot be excluded with two boreholes.
  \item \textbf{Conditionality on $H$.} The $(\Kd,H)$ surface is a
    shallow valley (Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}b,c); at fixed published
    $H=6$~cm, \Kd\ is well identified, but the pair is jointly
    identified only up to the valley direction.
  \item \textbf{\Qb\ circularity.} The published fluxes derive from the
    same records; the direct map and flux-agnostic bootstrap are the
    mitigations.
  \item \textbf{Two points, not a map.} Two boreholes cannot constrain
    global variability --- they demonstrate that at least this much
    site-to-site variation exists.
  \item \textbf{Topography.} Taurus--Littrow's valley walls modify the
    radiation environment relative to the flat-terrain forcing assumed
    here.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Relation to prior estimates}
The per-site values land inside the historical spread
(Fig.~\ref{fig:prior}) and reconcile its extremes: laboratory soils
(uncompacted, radiative term small at laboratory conditions) bound
from below; the \citet{vasavada2012} deep orbital estimate and
\citet{feng2020} sit near the A17 and A15 values respectively. The
original \citet{langseth1976} reductions and this work differ chiefly
in the forward model's radiative term and the treatment of borestem
disturbance --- the same directions identified by \citet{keihm1984}
and \citet{grott2010}.

\section{On numerical integrity}
Three of this project's inputs were, at different times, silently
wrong: a stale basal flux, an under-resolved inner loop, and a
first-order defect in the time march. Each was found by the same
discipline --- \emph{certify the quantity you report, not a proxy for
it} --- and their combined effect moved the Apollo 17 headline from
$8.12$ to $7.08$~\mWmK. The full audit trail is preserved in
Appendix~\ref{app:numerics} (Table~\ref{tab:audittrail}) both as
provenance for the final numbers and because the failure modes are
generic to borehole-scale thermal modeling.

\chapter{Conclusions and outlook}\label{ch:conclusion}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The Apollo 15 and 17 boreholes require deep conductivities of
    $4.60^{+0.86}_{-0.21}$ and $7.08^{+0.53}_{-0.52}$~\mWmK; the
    global $3.4$~\mWmK\ lies outside both 95\% intervals. Meter-scale
    lunar thermal modeling should carry site-resolved, not global,
    \Kd.
  \item The inter-site contrast ($+2.31$~\mWmK\ median) is marginal in
    magnitude but directionally robust across the entire published
    basal-flux envelope, with MCMC ordering probability 99.3\%
    regardless of prior width --- a consequence of the two sites'
    opposite responses to flux revision, discovered by mapping the
    degeneracy directly instead of assuming its usual proportional
    form.
  \item The flux-anchored solver makes exhaustive uncertainty analysis
    of borehole-constrained thermal models routine
    ($0.636$~s per solve), and is certified against brute force,
    against a discretization ladder, and against an independent C++
    implementation to $10^{-12}$~K.
  \item For TSUKIMI \citep{tsukimi2022} and successor subsurface
    sounders, the retrieved per-site columns supply the forward-model
    boundary condition orbital instruments cannot measure; conversely,
    a single firm per-site \Qb\ from future landed heat-flow
    measurements \citep{nagihara2018} is the one external input that
    would most tighten this retrieval.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Derivation of the closure ODE}\label{app:derivation}

This appendix derives Eq.~\eqref{eq:closure} from the heat equation in
five moves, none skipped.

\section*{Move 1 --- average over one lunation}
Define the cycle mean $\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle \equiv
\frac{1}{P}\int_0^P (\cdot)\, dt$ over one lunation $P$. In periodic
steady state the column repeats every cycle, $T(z, t+P) = T(z,t)$.
Apply $\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle$ to both sides of Eq.~\eqref{eq:heat}.

\section*{Move 2 --- the storage term vanishes}
Write the volumetric enthalpy $h(T) = \int \rho\, c_p\, dT$, so that
$\rho c_p\, \partial_t T = \partial_t h$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \left\langle \rho c_p\, \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} \right\rangle
  = \frac{1}{P}\int_0^P \frac{\partial h}{\partial t}\, dt
  = \frac{h(P) - h(0)}{P} = 0,
\end{equation}
because $h$ returns to its starting value after a full cycle. In
steady state nothing accumulates heat on average --- time drops out of
the problem. This move is legal \emph{only} in periodic steady state,
which is why Step~A must first drive the skin to that state before
Step~B is invoked.

\section*{Move 3 --- the depth derivative passes through the average}
On the right-hand side, $z$ and $t$ are independent variables, so the
average commutes with $\partial_z$:
\begin{equation}
  0 = \left\langle \frac{\partial}{\partial z}
      \left( K\, \frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right)\right\rangle
    = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}
      \left\langle K\, \frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right\rangle
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \left\langle K\, \frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right\rangle
  = \text{constant in } z.
\end{equation}
No $t$ remains: the averaged equation is an equation in depth alone.

\section*{Move 4 --- pin the constant with the bottom boundary}
The basal boundary condition fixes the constant: at $z_{\max}$ the
conducted flux is \Qb, hence
\begin{equation}
  \left\langle K\, \frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right\rangle = \Qb
  \qquad\text{at every depth.}
\end{equation}
The cycle-mean column carries the geothermal flux all the way up.

\section*{Move 5 --- peel the average off the product}
The mean of a product is not the product of the means; name the gap
\begin{equation}
  \urect(z) \;\equiv\;
  \left\langle K\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right\rangle
  - K(\Tm, z)\, \frac{d\Tm}{dz},
  \label{eq:urect}
\end{equation}
the \emph{rectified flux} --- the covariance of the oscillating
conductivity and the oscillating gradient, nonzero only where the
diurnal wave lives. Substituting Move~4 into Eq.~\eqref{eq:urect} and
solving for the mean gradient gives the closure,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Tm}{dz} = \frac{\Qb - \urect(z)}{K(\Tm, z)},
\end{equation}
a first-order ODE in depth alone. Above the anchor, $\urect$ is
evaluated from the Step~A skin solution; below the anchor it decays
rapidly (at $z_0 = 0.55$~m the diurnal amplitude is ${\sim}0.15$~K and
$\urect < 1\%$ of \Qb), so the deep integration is effectively
$d\Tm/dz = \Qb/K$.

\section*{Step B in practice}
Equation~\eqref{eq:closure} is integrated downward from the anchored
temperature $\Tm(z_0)$ by second-order Runge--Kutta on the same
geometric grid used by Step~A, with $K$ evaluated at the current mean
temperature at each half-step. Because the ODE \emph{is} the
steady-state equation, Step~B is exact below the anchor given a
correct anchor temperature --- the outer loop's only job is to make
the anchor temperature correct.

\chapter{Numerical scheme and certification}\label{app:numerics}

\section{Spatial discretization}
\emph{Grid.} The column $[0, 5~\mathrm{m}]$ is divided into 69
finite-volume cells whose widths grow geometrically ($\Delta z_0 =
2$~mm, growth 8\%): millimeter resolution where the ${\sim}280$~K
diurnal swing lives, centimeter-to-decimetre cells in the smooth deep
column. Temperatures live at cell centres; fluxes cross cell faces.
Integrating the heat equation over a cell and telescoping the interior
fluxes conserves energy to machine precision.

\emph{Face conductivity.} Heat crossing the face between cells $i$ and
$i{+}1$ passes through two half-cells in series, so thermal
resistances add and the face value is the \emph{harmonic} mean,
\begin{equation}
  K_{i+1/2} = \frac{2\,K_i\,K_{i+1}}{K_i + K_{i+1}},
\end{equation}
the physically correct choice where $K$ jumps steeply through the
compaction zone (an arithmetic mean would let a high-$K$ cell
short-circuit its low-$K$ neighbor).

\section{Time integration}
\emph{Scheme.} Crank--Nicolson (the $\theta=\tfrac12$ scheme):
unconditionally stable --- the amplification factor per diffusive mode
is $\xi = (1-\beta/2)/(1+\beta/2)$ with $\beta \ge 0$, so $|\xi| \le
1$ for every mode and any $\Delta t$ --- and second-order accurate.
Explicit Euler at the 2~mm surface cell would demand $\Delta t
\lesssim 200$~s for stability alone; CN frees the step to be chosen by
\emph{accuracy}, certified below at $\Delta t = 1800$~s.

\emph{Nonlinearity.} Properties depend on $T$, so each step performs
midpoint-Picard corrector sweeps: properties are re-evaluated at
$\tfrac12(T^{\,n} + T^{\,n+1})$ and the tridiagonal solve repeated.
The production setting of two sweeps was fixed by a measured scan of
the periodic attractor's residual drift per time-step halving:
$-8.6 / -4.2 / -2.2 / -2.0$~mK at $1/2/4/8$ sweeps.

\emph{Surface.} The nonlinear radiative surface balance is solved by
Newton iteration each step, re-anchored at the implicit surface
temperature.

\emph{Linear algebra.} Each step assembles one tridiagonal system
$A\,T^{\,n+1} = d$: every interior row is the CN stencil; the surface
row is a Dirichlet ghost (the Newton-solved surface temperature moves
to the right-hand side); the basal row is a Neumann ghost
($b_{N-1} \to b_{N-1} - \tfrac12\alpha$,
$d_{N-1} \to d_{N-1} + \Delta t\, \Qb / C$). The Thomas algorithm
solves it in $O(N)$.

\section{Certification of the reported quantity}
The convergence criterion throughout is the stability of the
\emph{retrieved} \Kds\ --- the quantity the letter reports --- never a
proxy such as a fixed cycle count or the flux-closure residual alone.
Three certification campaigns are on record.

\paragraph{The fixed-count trap.} An early configuration declared
convergence after a fixed number of spin-up lunations. The skin
settles in ${\sim}10$ lunations while the deep column is still tens of
kelvin away, so the cycle-to-cycle test passes long before the profile
has arrived (Fig.~\ref{fig:f1bug}). The cure is the anchor-drift
tolerance of \S\ref{sec:fixedpoint}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering

\paragraph{Inner-loop resolution and the truncated Step A.} The
retrieved \Kds\ drifts monotonically with the inner spin-up length
$n_{\rm inner}$ when Step~A time-steps the full column --- the deep
relaxation leaks into the anchor. The structural fix is the truncation
of \S\ref{sec:stepA}; with it, Apollo 15 (the slower-converging site,
lower $K$ hence lower diffusivity) reads
$4.600/4.605/4.606$~\mWmK\ at $n_{\rm inner} = 64/96/128$, and the
truncated solve was validated unbiased against the full-column solve
($4.606$ vs.\ $4.608$ at $n=128$). Production uses $n_{\rm inner}=96$.

\paragraph{The time-step ladder and the wrap step.} Halving the time
step $1800 \to 900 \to 450$~s moves \Kds(A17) by $0.018$ then
$0.007$~\mWmK\ ($7.0795 / 7.0975 / 7.1045$;
Fig.~\ref{fig:dtladder}) --- inside the $0.05$ acceptance band and
consistent with second order. This ladder is itself the instrument
that caught the deepest defect of the project: the original march
looped over a periodic grid spanning $[0, P-\Delta t]$ and relabeled
the $P-\Delta t$ state as $t=0$, a phase slip of exactly $\Delta t$
per lunation that made the periodic attractor first-order in $\Delta
t$ (${+}61$~mK per halving) regardless of the second-order stepper
inside. The fix is a wrap step closing each cycle. As a cross-check,
Richardson extrapolation of the \emph{defective} scheme's ladder
converges to $7.104$~\mWmK\ --- the fixed scheme's finest-rung value
to four digits: two different discretizations, one continuum limit.

  certification ladder for \Kds(A17) under the wrap-corrected scheme.}
  \label{fig:dtladder}

\paragraph{Independent implementation.} A faithful C++ port of the
solver (same grid, scheme, and wrap step) agrees with the Python
implementation to $1.4\times10^{-12}$~K over full retrievals, and
benchmarks the language gap at $3.24$ vs.\ $4.71$~ms per lunation
(C++ vs.\ compiled Python kernel) --- a constant factor, confirming no
algorithmic slack remains.
